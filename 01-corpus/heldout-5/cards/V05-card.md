## Paper identification

The paper revises flood design information and outlet operating rules for the multipurpose Stânca–Costești reservoir on the Prut River in Romania/Moldova. It combines a calibrated hydraulic routing model, reassessed gauge rating curve, statistical design floods based on peak discharge and volume, simulation-optimization of individual outlet activation, and real-time forecast-triggered adaptation among framework rules. The study emphasizes the 2008 compact flood, 2010 multi-peak flood, current hydrology, and climate-change (CC) scenarios across 10%, 5%, 1%, and 0.1% annual exceedance probability (AEP) classes (pp. 1, 6–9, 24–37).

## 2. How the authors reason

Reasoning overview: The authors begin from an old multipurpose reservoir whose original design flood and operating rules may no longer represent the accumulated hydrological record, recent exceptional floods, or climate-change projections. They reason that maximum discharge alone is inadequate: flood volume, duration, time to peak, and shape determine how much of the 665 million m³ flood-control volume is consumed and how long outlets must operate (pp. 1, 4–5, 14–16). The 2008 flood is selected as the critical single-peak shape because its compactness was greatest and its average inflow was nearly twice that of the long 2010 event despite the latter's larger volume (p. 24). A hydraulic model and rating-curve reassessment are needed before design quantiles and operation rules are trusted, because historical high flows were extrapolated and recorded volumes differed between the gauge and reservoir (pp. 10–14). The authors then reason that optimized outlet ranges should flatten downstream flow while limiting reservoir level, successive release changes, and final volume. In real time, the exact flood AEP is unknown and the actual shape differs from a design hydrograph, so managers begin with a lower-severity framework rule and switch upward when forecast flow/volume trends and reservoir level cross divergence points. The framework is deliberately outlet-specific and retains post-flood discretion. Its claim stops at simulation-based rules for one reservoir and stated scenarios; it does not establish that downstream channel capacity is preserved for 1% or rarer floods.

Reconstructed route: recognize aging design/rule information and multiparameter flood uncertainty → reassess observations and hydraulic routing → derive peak/volume/shape/AEP design floods and CC variants → select critical shapes and scenario classes → optimize outlet activation/deactivation ranges → encode constraints and operating-rule tables → use forecast and water-level triggers to switch framework rules → validate through simulated levels/discharges/manoeuvres and post-event comparison → reassess rules after future floods. This logical ordering is the extractor's reconstruction, not a claim about private reasoning.

## 3. How the authors execute

Execution overview: The study first builds a 1D unsteady Mike Flood routing model between the Rădăuți–Prut gauge and downstream of Stânca–Costești, calibrating roughness and revising the gauge rating curve against 2008 and 2010 levels, outflows, and water balances. It then processes 1978–2020 floods with BM and POT methods, selects distributions using goodness-of-fit tests, estimates peak and volume quantiles with uncertainty bounds and CC values, and constructs shape-specific design hydrographs using compactness and historical flood shapes. The simulation-optimization model represents outlet equivalence classes, mass balance, water level, release variation, final volume, and a downstream target/penalty objective; Nelder–Mead searches the outlet start/end levels. The resulting rules are organized by AEP and flood volume, then applied adaptively using forecast triggers and reservoir levels. Results compare design-phase, optimized, current-hydrology, CC, 2008, 2010-shape, and 0.1% cases using maximum level and downstream discharge, with post-flood closure and rule-assessment branches.

Reconstructed workflow: hydraulic/rating calibration → flood record reassessment → peak/volume distribution fitting → joint/shape design-flood construction → outlet-class model and constrained simulation-optimization → framework-rule tables → real-time forecast/level switching → simulated rule evaluation → post-flood storage and critical reassessment. Hydraulic calibration, statistical fitting, rule optimization, real-time operation, and after-event review are distinct phases; real-time updates and outlet actions would repeat during an event.

## 6. Unreported or unresolved items

- The supplied text does not provide a complete numerical dataset, all rating-curve equations, or full Mike Flood calibration/validation statistics.
- Exact distributions, fitted parameters, confidence-interval construction, and dependence between peak, volume, duration, and shape are not fully reported.
- Several rule descriptions use slightly different values or labels across detailed sections and conclusions; the principal switching logic is clear but exact operational thresholds should be checked against the source figures/tables before implementation.
- Nelder–Mead initialization, stopping criteria, penalty weights, timestep, outlet discharge curves, and the full optimized parameter table are not reported.
- Forecast model structure, forecast error/skill, update frequency, and uncertainty of 3-day flow/volume forecasts are not quantified.
- The proposed 10-day rainfall categories are not validated for false alarms, missed events, or lead-time performance.
- Real-time authorization, communications, sensor/gate failure response, emergency override, and operator training procedures are not reported.
- The paper does not independently validate that optimized rules would perform as simulated under future unobserved floods; most extreme results are model scenarios.
- CC scenarios are adopted from cited hydrological projections and are not a complete probabilistic climate-uncertainty analysis.
- Downstream channel exceedance is explicitly accepted for larger floods in the model; dam safety and downstream flood protection are therefore separate outcomes.

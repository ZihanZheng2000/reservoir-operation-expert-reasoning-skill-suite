## Paper identification

The paper studies real-time flood-control operation of the Daecheong multipurpose reservoir in South Korea. Its central problem is that fixed-weight MPC formulations do not represent practical operator preferences that change with hydrological state, while directly optimizing many nonlinear objectives and preference parameters online is difficult. The proposed parameterised dynamic model predictive control (PD-MPC) searches dynamically for objective weights and a highest-target water-level parameter through an evaluator while solving a parameterized linear MPC. The evaluation is numerical, based on three historical flood events with simulated inflow-forecast uncertainty; it is not a deployment or operator-validation study.

## 2. How the authors reason

Reasoning overview: The paper begins from the gap between mathematically optimized reservoir releases and practical real-time operation. It reasons that minimizing spillway peak/volume alone can lead to undesirable schedules, especially with short prediction horizons, because the final planned outflow has delayed effect on within-horizon storage and because repeated re-optimization can cause sudden changes or gate cycling. It therefore adds objectives for stepwise changes, changes from the previous schedule, water-level excursions, gate continuity, retaining peak outflow below observed peak inflow, and turbine use before spillway release. The authors then reason that the relative importance of these objectives and the preferred upper water-level limit depend on the current hydrological state, so fixed weights/parameters are inadequate. Because simultaneous nonlinear optimization of controls and preferences is difficult, they separate the problem into a linear MPC control solve and an evaluator-driven search over weights/parameters. The evidence supports adaptive numerical performance in a Daecheong case; it does not demonstrate a recovered private operator decision process or direct field authorization.

Reconstructed route: (R1) identify practical decision criteria and the failure modes of myopic/fixed-weight MPC; (R2) relate objective priorities and target-level parameters to changing reservoir state; (R3) preserve complex preference evaluation while keeping the online control subproblem linear; (R4) use receding-horizon feedback to recalculate with current state and revised inflow predictions; and (R5) test whether dynamic preferences improve the specified numerical indicators against fixed baselines and under certain/uncertain inflow.

## 3. How the authors execute

Execution overview: The workflow starts with historical Daecheong inflow/outflow/storage data and three long flood events. Real inflow is perturbed to generate horizon-specific uncertain predictions with a zero-mean Gaussian multiplier whose standard deviation grows with forecast lead time, then smoothed with a 3-hour moving average. At each receding-horizon step, the linear reservoir model and operational constraints support an MPC solve for a candidate vector of objective weights and SH. A GA searches candidate vectors using an evaluator that simulates the nonlinear height-volume relation and penalizes the eight practical conditions/objectives, while the linear MPC solves for the corresponding schedule. Only the first outflow is implemented; the loop repeats using updated conditions. The study then branches into baseline comparison, certain/uncertain inflow comparison, prediction-horizon comparison, evaluator-weight analysis, SH analysis, and time-varying weight analysis. These latter branches evaluate the method rather than constitute additional operational actions.

Reconstructed workflow: (E1) define case data and initial state; (E2) generate or select inflow predictions; (E3) propagate the linear reservoir model and constraints; (E4) solve candidate linear MPC schedules; (E5) evaluate candidate schedules with nonlinear penalties; (E6) use GA to update weights/SH and repeat the alternating search; (E7) implement first control and roll horizon; (E8) collect metrics and compare experimental branches.

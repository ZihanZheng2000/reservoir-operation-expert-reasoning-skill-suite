## Paper identification

The paper studies real-time reservoir operation during the flood season when flood control and post-flood water conservation compete. It proposes dynamic control of the flood-limited water level (DC-FLWL) using a two-stage rolling-horizon model that represents forecast uncertainty and an acceptable downstream flood-risk level. The analysis focuses on the prefill module of DC-FLWL, derives analytical hedging rules, and illustrates them with the Nierji Reservoir case in northeastern China. ## 2. How the authors reason

Reasoning overview: The paper begins from a conflict between the empty storage required for flood control and the storage desired for later water use. A fixed FLWL based on design floods does not respond to seasonal variation or real-time forecasts, so it can spill water unnecessarily or leave insufficient storage after an early-ending flood season. The authors therefore reason toward DC-FLWL, but recognize that pre-filling storage can increase future flood risk if the inflow forecast is wrong. They reduce the decision to an allocation of expected residual flood conveyance capacity (RFCC) between carryover water conservation in Stage 1 and a safety margin for uncertain Stage-2 releases. Acceptable flood risk imposes a minimum safety margin, while water-supply/release constraints and FLWL bounds limit the feasible set. A weighted loss function and marginal-utility comparison then determine whether RFCC is assigned to flood safety, water conservation, or both. The resulting policy is conditional on forecast uncertainty, acceptable risk, expected inflow, and the relative weighting of the two objectives; the claim stops at analytical and simulated case-study support.

Reconstructed route: (1) identify the limitation of static FLWL and the need for forecast-responsive operation; (2) select a two-stage rolling-horizon representation so current release/storage decisions account for the forecast horizon; (3) represent uncertain future inflow through a forecast-error variable and express downstream risk as the probability of exceeding a release threshold; (4) convert the risk and water-supply requirements to a minimum safety margin and other deterministic bounds; (5) formulate the trade-off as weighted water-conservation and flood-risk loss; (6) use RFCC conservation and KKT optimality to compare marginal utilities; (7) classify operating regimes by forecast uncertainty, acceptable risk, and expected inflow; (8) implement the corresponding piecewise hedging rules in the Nierji case and compare them with static and water-conservation-only scenarios. The operational description includes prefill, prerelease, and flood-event control, but the formal analysis is explicitly limited to the prefill module.

## 3. How the authors execute

Execution overview: The authors first define a two-stage mass-balance model over a rolling flood-season horizon. Stage 1 is the immediate decision interval, and Stage 2 is the remaining forecast horizon; ending storage is set to the lower FLWL bound in the model. At each rolling decision time, observed/current inflow and the Stage-2 forecast are combined with the forecast-error distribution, downstream release threshold, acceptable risk, and storage/release limits. The prefill module raises storage only when the water level is within the FLWL bounds and decreasing, current inflow is below the specified threshold, and no flood is forecast within the lead time. The optimization then allocates RFCC between Stage-1 excess storage and Stage-2 safety margin. The resulting piecewise rules produce a release from Stage-1 mass balance; if that release violates the minimum downstream requirement, the minimum release is used and actual storage is below the unconstrained optimum. The paper describes prerelease before a forecast flood and crisis-recovery operation during an event, but formally analyzes the prefill module. For the case study, the authors estimate a forecast-error distribution from 1974–2003 data, derive rules under selected uncertainty/risk settings, simulate 1988 and 30 years, and compare three policy scenarios.

Reconstructed workflow: (1) set reservoir and downstream thresholds, FLWL bounds, time step, forecast horizon, target storage, risk level, release requirements, and objective weights; (2) obtain current/forecast Stage-1 and Stage-2 inflows; (3) test DC-FLWL prefill conditions; (4) calculate forecast-error-based release and safety-margin limits; (5) solve the constrained two-stage allocation; (6) select the analytical branch by uncertainty/risk/inflow thresholds; (7) derive Stage-1 release from mass balance and enforce the minimum-release constraint if necessary; (8) roll the decision horizon forward with updated forecasts; (9) for evaluation, estimate the error distribution, derive rules for selected parameters, and simulate alternative policies over historical periods; (10) compare water levels, flood-risk statistics, refilled years, and mean annual conservation. The prefill decision is repeated at rolling time steps; policy comparison and 1988/30-year simulations are evaluation branches rather than additional operational rules.

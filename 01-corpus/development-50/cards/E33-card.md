## Paper identification

The paper studies real-time flood-control operation of the two-reservoir Ankang–Danjiangkou cascade in the Hanjiang River. It proposes a two-stage risk calculation that combines scenario-based forecast risk during each reservoir’s forecast lead-time with routed design-flood risk after that horizon, then embeds the calculated risk as a constraint in a hydropower-maximizing rolling-horizon operation model. The supplied text covers the full article body and printed pages 1–9, with equation typography partly corrupted by text extraction.

## 2. How the authors reason

Reasoning overview: The authors start from a mismatch between real-time decisions and future inflows: forecast error can cause releases or water levels to exceed critical values. Existing multi-reservoir risk methods are described as concentrating on the forecast lead-time and as seldom accounting for different lead-times across reservoirs. They therefore split the horizon at the forecast boundary. During the observed forecast period, scenario forecasts provide the uncertainty representation; after that boundary, inflows are unknown, so historical design hydrographs and routing are used. Because the two reservoirs have different forecast horizons, downstream inflow routing and the relative start times of design hydrographs must reflect the lead-time difference. The resulting full-horizon risk becomes an operational constraint, while hydropower generation is optimized subject to storage, release, routing, and release-change limits. The claim stops at a two-reservoir case with stochastic-inflow uncertainty; release, observation, capacity-curve, and routing errors are explicitly left for future work.

Reconstructed route: (1) identify forecast uncertainty and the multi-reservoir/heterogeneous-horizon gap; (2) partition risk by information availability; (3) align downstream forecasts and post-forecast design hydrographs with lead-time differences; (4) define failure probability over scenario combinations and critical release/water-level thresholds; (5) verify that the calculated risk is not an underestimate against Monte Carlo failure statistics; (6) use the risk as a hard constraint in rolling-horizon hydropower optimization; and (7) compare optimized and regular operation in the case study.

## 3. How the authors execute

Execution overview: The study first represents reservoir state evolution with a water-balance routing model. It generates forecast inflow scenarios by perturbing observed inflow with zero-mean normal relative errors, with variances assigned to the 6-hour Ankang and 12-hour Danjiangkou lead-times. For stage 1 it counts threshold failures in the scenario set. For stage 2 it routes design flood hydrographs from the stage-1 end water-level combinations, using the lead-time alignment across reservoirs, and combines the two risk components. Separately, it creates a Monte Carlo verification ensemble through a main downstream site, sampled flood volumes and observed typical hydrographs, and corresponding slave-site hydrographs. The traditional failure proportion defines the accepted reference risk; the optimized scheme is feasible if its simulated failure proportion does not exceed it. Finally, the risk and physical constraints are passed to a nonlinear rolling-horizon optimizer, whose storage/release decisions are compared with the regular scheme for the 2010 flood season.

Reconstructed workflow: The main operation loop is forecast → optimize subject to two-stage risk → implement the current decision horizon → advance one interval → update forecast and repeat. Scenario generation, multisite stochastic simulation, risk-surface precomputation, and comparison with regular operation are setup/evaluation branches rather than recurring field actions in the reported case.

## 2. How the authors reason

Reasoning overview: The authors frame large-cascade operation as a system-level problem: tributary peaks can encounter at the TGR, while each reservoir also has local flood-control, power, shipping, and safety obligations. Because severe floods are spatially and temporally heterogeneous and reservoirs are far apart, the order and amount of reserved capacity to use cannot be fixed by one rigid schedule. The reasoning therefore combines increasingly informative forecasts with staged flexibility: longer-lead qualitative precipitation information supports early TGR drawdown and reserve creation; shorter-lead quantitative flood forecasts support releasing upstream reserved capacity and staggering peaks; updated forecasts drive rolling cooperation schemes. The authors also reason that surplus capacity at one reservoir can compensate for another's downstream flood-control task, allowing useful higher levels without increasing basin-wide risk, provided flood type, encounter timing, reservoir role, and constraints are considered. Since flood decisions affect departments with different objectives, a technical center generates an ensemble of possible schemes and a state-led multi-department consultation selects the final action. The claim stops at a documented 2020 case and institutional framework; it does not prove that the same outcomes or coordination arrangements will hold in other basins.

Reconstructed route: recognize cascade interdependence and competing objectives; forecast flood magnitude/timing over multiple lead times; determine where reserved capacity can be shared and in what order; generate candidate coordinated schemes with hydrologic/hydrodynamic and socioeconomic information; consult responsible departments and authorities; execute selected releases/impounding with rolling forecast updates; verify flood, power, shipping, and water-level outcomes; and reanalyze the event to revise schemes and institutions.

## 3. How the authors execute

Execution overview: The reported operation uses a basin-wide information and decision system. ICDSS automatically collects hydro-meteorological, reservoir-operation, grid-load, regulatory, and instruction data from a large monitoring network, then links reservoir-operation simulation, hydro-meteorological forecasting, discharge routing, socioeconomic evaluation, crisis plans, and a data–model–user interface. The forecast/routing component combines a Xin'anjiang model with 377 subunits, a one-dimensional hydrodynamic model, and an error-correction module; human–machine interaction can supplement automation. The cooperation component uses forecasts at different lead times, NSGA-II to generate optimal scheme ensembles/Pareto solutions, and hedging/marginal-benefit theory to examine risk–benefit competition. Candidate schemes are submitted by CROC to FCDRH and other departments for consultation, then rolling execution follows forecast updates. During the fourth and fifth 2020 floods, long-lead qualitative warnings prompted TGR pre-discharge and reservation of 17.75×10⁹ m³ (80% of its flood-control capacity); shorter-lead quantitative forecasts triggered upstream reserved-capacity releases to stagger peaks. The article reports 19.0×10⁹ m³ held for these floods (8.2×10⁹ upstream and 10.8×10⁹ by TGR), no water spillage in the cited hydropower example, and level-variation/shipping outcomes. ICDSS reanalysis and crisis plans support post-event learning and failure response. This is a real-world case description; it does not provide a complete reproducible optimization formulation or counterfactual experiment.

Reconstructed workflow: collect and manage multi-source data; model upstream operations and forecast/routing; generate multi-lead cooperative schemes; evaluate flood, power, shipping, ecology, and risk implications; consult departments and authorize the final scheme; execute rolling releases/impounding and schedule changes; monitor and compare outcomes; and reanalyze after the flood while retaining emergency plans for system failures.

## Paper identification

The paper studies how a reservoir can dynamically allocate storage between present-stage flood-water conservation and later-stage flood control during dynamic control of the flood-limited water level (DC-FLWL). It is a theoretical and illustrative case-study analysis using a two-stage multiobjective optimal-operation model with uncertain second-stage inflow, a water-conservation preference weight, an acceptable downstream flood risk, and storage/release constraints. The supplied text is page-separated and retains PDF page numbers 1963–1981; references below use the supplied page markers (pp. 1–18 in the text, corresponding to journal pages 1963–1980).

## 2. How the authors reason

Reasoning overview: The paper starts from the conflict between fixed flood-control storage and water conservation: a fixed allocation can be unused in some events and insufficient in others. It therefore treats storage allocation as a two-stage hedging decision that uses forecast information while accounting for forecast uncertainty and stakeholder preference. The feasible decision region is bounded by mass balance, nonnegative and maximum excess storage, downstream release requirements, and a minimum safety margin derived from acceptable flood risk. Within the interior of this region, the authors use equality of weighted marginal utilities to characterize the trade-off; at the boundaries, the binding constraint determines the rule. Preference thresholds at the critical points identify when hedging begins, when the rule changes type, and when one objective dominates. Forecast-uncertainty thresholds are then selected according to which constraint becomes binding and whether the resulting forecast remains useful. The claim stops at analytical model results and an illustrative reservoir case; the paper does not establish deployed operational performance.

Reconstructed route: (1) Frame fixed FLWL allocation as unable to adapt reliably to event-specific inflow and forecast information. (2) Represent present conservation and future flood control as two objectives linked by carryover storage and safety margin. (3) Define the feasible DC-FLWL region from physical, downstream-flow, storage, and acceptable-risk constraints. (4) Use the KKT/equal-marginal-utility condition in the unconstrained interior and boundary conditions elsewhere. (5) Derive minimum, switching, and maximum conservation weights and classify Types 1–3 hedging rules. (6) Differentiate the interior solution with respect to forecast uncertainty and identify (r_c), (r_{c0}), (r_e), and (r_{cr}) according to binding constraints. (7) Combine expected inflow, acceptable risk, preference, and uncertainty into a decision procedure and illustrate it with Nierji Reservoir.

## 3. How the authors execute

Execution overview: The authors first specify a two-stage reservoir abstraction and convert forecast uncertainty and acceptable downstream flood risk into a minimum safety margin. They then solve/analyze the constrained weighted objective through mass balance, marginal-utility/KKT conditions, and boundary cases. Analytical expressions and numerical solution of the critical equations produce feasible preference ranges, rule types, and uncertainty thresholds. Separate branches evaluate expected-inflow and acceptable-risk effects, followed by a Nierji Reservoir illustration using stated hydrologic, capacity, forecast-error, discharge, and risk settings. Figures and tables compare parameter cases and threshold regimes; these are analytical sensitivity and case-illustration branches rather than independent operational validation.

Reconstructed workflow: (1) Define stage-1 and stage-2 storage, inflow, release, forecast horizon, and negligible seasonal losses. (2) Compute conservation loss, flood risk, marginal utilities, minimum safety margin, and constrained objective. (3) Use stage-2 mass balance to compute RFCC and plot the feasible ABCD region. (4) Apply KKT/equal marginal utilities and endpoint conditions to derive (x_D,x_B,x_A), then trace Types 1–3. (5) Differentiate with respect to (r) and numerically solve Appendix B equations for the critical thresholds, selecting the applicable branch from inflow/risk conditions. (6) Examine the joint (x)–(r) effects. (7) Insert Nierji parameters, calculate the critical inflow and (r_{max}) values, and interpret the resulting rule and forecast-availability ranges.

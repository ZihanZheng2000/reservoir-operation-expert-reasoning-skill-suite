## 2. How the authors reason

Reasoning overview: The authors begin with the multiobjective nature of flood operation: releasing too much damages downstream, while storing too much can threaten dam safety. They argue that calibrating against a few recorded/design floods can fail to represent the range of heavy to extreme events and that user-supplied model parameters may be difficult to choose reliably. A stochastic approach therefore generates a broad ensemble of inflow hydrographs and uncertain loads, then evaluates the full distribution of key outputs. Because downstream release damage and dam-storage failure risk can be expressed in euros in these cases, the authors aggregate them into an overall risk index with equal weights, while retaining the two separate indices to construct a Pareto front. The “balanced optimum” is the parameter set minimizing the aggregated index; the Pareto analysis checks whether that choice is a reasonable compromise rather than an extreme single-objective solution. The calibration method is kept conceptually separate from the RFCM's per-event MILP objective: the latter minimizes weighted penalty terms for release and storage, whereas the former scores parameter sets through expected damage over the ensemble. The claim stops at a case-based calibration methodology; it does not establish that the same parameter ranges, damage functions, initial-state assumption, or weights apply universally.

Reconstructed route: identify the release-versus-storage conflict; replace few-event/deterministic calibration with a stochastic representative ensemble; characterize each simulated policy by maximum released flow and maximum stored volume; map those variables to downstream damage and conditional dam-failure risk using thresholds and curves; aggregate expected costs and select the minimum; inspect the same candidates through Pareto dominance; interpret sensitivity and case differences; and record the need for authority input, initial-level distributions, and real-time evaluation before deployment.

## 3. How the authors execute

Execution overview: The implementation combines stochastic flood generation, event-level reservoir optimization, and parameter-level risk evaluation. For each reservoir, a random sample of 1000 event probabilities is generated; extreme-value precipitation (SQRT-ETmax), IDF curves, ARMA storm timing, curve-number rainfall–runoff conversion, SCS unit hydrographs, and Muskingum routing produce synthetic flash-flood inflow hydrographs. The RFCM receives an initial storage vector and inflow matrix and returns release and storage matrices for each candidate parameter set. Its MILP objective minimizes a weighted sum of piecewise-linear release and storage penalties subject to hydraulic and operational constraints. For every event, maximum release and maximum storage are extracted, empirical distributions are formed with Gringorten plotting positions, and expected annual damage calculations produce Iq and Iv. Their sum Ir ranks candidate parameter sets; the minimum is reported as the balanced solution. A second analysis constructs the Pareto front of Iq versus Iv, identifies the point closest to the origin as balanced, and compares alternatives and operation trajectories. Three independent Spanish reservoirs—Puentes, Talave, and Fuensanta—are evaluated using case-specific damage curves, thresholds, and characteristics. This is a synthetic, offline calibration/evaluation workflow; no forecast-informed real-time data stream or field deployment is included.

Reconstructed workflow: define case characteristics and damage/threshold functions; generate a representative synthetic flood ensemble; encode the RFCM and candidate parameterization; run MILP operations for every event and parameter combination; extract maximum release/storage; estimate empirical distributions and expected damage indices; select the minimum overall-risk set; construct and inspect the Pareto front; compare calibrated and non-calibrated/alternative policies; and document case-specific sensitivity and missing deployment evidence.

## 6. Unreported or unresolved items

- The paper does not provide a real-time forecast input, forecast-error treatment, update cadence, or operational test; it calibrates an offline flood-control model against synthetic inflow ensembles.
- Detailed distributions and parameters for SQRT-ETmax, IDF curves, ARMA storm timing, curve-number runoff, SCS unit hydrographs, and Muskingum routing are not reproduced; the ensemble-generation paper is cited for detail.
- The random sample/seed, exact event-selection or rejection rules, and formal representativeness checks for the 1000-event ensembles are not reported.
- Initial storage is fixed at TCP for every event; the effect of realistic initial-level distributions is explicitly deferred to future work.
- Complete release/storage penalty curves, piecewise-linear breakpoints, MILP solver settings, timestep, horizon, and all hydraulic/operational constraints are not given in the supplied text.
- The exact parameter search grid and spacing for wq, PVfcl, and b are not fully reported; OCR makes some Table 5 range symbols difficult to resolve. M=6 is attributed to prior sensitivity analysis, but that analysis is not included here.
- Damage curves and monetary costs depend on technical impact data and local authority knowledge; full curve derivation, uncertainty, and stakeholder validation are not supplied.
- Storage risk assumes dam overtopping leads to failure and estimates conditional failure with rare synthetic floods (approximately Tr=10–200,000 years); model uncertainty and alternative failure modes are not reported.
- The equal weighting of Iq and Iv is justified by common euro units and lack of supplementary information, but no elicited risk preferences or authority-approved indifference curve is reported.

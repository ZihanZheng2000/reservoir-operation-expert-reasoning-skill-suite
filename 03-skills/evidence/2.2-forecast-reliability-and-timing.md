# Evidence for unit 2.2-forecast-reliability-and-timing

Paper steps assigned to each small pattern of this unit, with their full card text. Step IDs: Exx = paper, Rn = reasoning step, En = execution step.

## RP03-A Reliability varies with lead time; use forecasts by stage

Near-term forecasts are accurate and long-lead ones coarse; different lead times support different actions. Strength: Established. 7 steps, 7 papers.

### E07-R3: Set the critical threshold and lead-time tolerance

**Description:** The authors set the EFO storage threshold at 111,000 acre-feet, the maximum conservation storage under existing operations and 3 ft below the emergency-spillway crest, to maintain a safety margin. They compare forecasted exceedance risk with a tolerance curve: tolerance is 0% for the first five forecast days and increases afterward because near-term forecasts are treated as more reliable and later forecasts leave more response time.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Future ensemble trajectories may exceed the critical storage level. **Inputs:** Threshold, forecast day, ensemble-member storage trajectories, forecast skill and response time. **Judgment/procedure:** Calculate risk as the percentage of the 61 members above 111,000 acre-feet and require risk to be at or below the corresponding tolerance. **Branches:** If risk is at or below tolerance, no EFO flood-control release is required by this test; if above, calculate a release. **Output:** A day-specific risk test and allowable risk level.

**Evidence boundary:** Threshold, risk definition, and tolerance rationale are explicit on pp. 8–9, Section 3.3.1. The curve was produced heuristically with stakeholder-informed objectives (maximize storage reliability while minimizing downstream flooding and emergency spills); exact construction details are referred to supporting information and are not fully reported in the assigned main text.

### E09-R1: Identify when water-use pre-release is valid

**Description:** During a forecast dry period, inflow can be below utilization discharge, so releasing at that rate converts otherwise excess floodwater into conservation/hydropower use. Waiting for the next large-flood forecast can miss this opportunity.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Reservoir is above/near planned upper FLWL and a dry interval is expected. **Inputs:** Inflow, utilization discharge, rainfall forecast/dry duration. **Judgment/procedure:** Start water-use pre-release when inflow is below utilization discharge; switch to flood-control pre-release when a large inflow is forecast. **Branches:** Dry forecast versus imminent flood forecast. **Output:** Two-period release plan.

**Evidence boundary:** §§1–2, pp. 3–4. Trigger details beyond the conceptual rule are not fully operationalized.

### E10-R1: Separate forecast stages by reliability

**Description:** Nowcasts are shortest but most reliable; mid-term forecasts extend horizon but carry greater uncertainty. A single stationary error assumption discards useful information.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Choose operation horizon and uncertainty model. **Inputs:** Historical forecast errors and lead times. **Judgment/procedure:** Define RFH where nowcast/short-term relative error is generally <20%; divide current, second, and third stages. **Output:** Stage-specific uncertainty coefficients and horizon.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.1–2.2, pp. 2–4. 20% threshold is explicitly a paper choice and not authoritative (p. 11).

### E19-R5: Sequence the three forecast defenses

**Description:** Vapor convergence precedes precipitation and streamflow. The authors reason that VPS-FIRO should lower levels when VCWL decreases, increase releases when substantial precipitation is forecast, and reach FLWL using streamflow forecasts before the flood. After the event, lower-risk vapor conditions allow prefill.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Forecast indicators change across their respective horizons. **Inputs:** VCWL/PP, precipitation forecasts, streamflow forecasts, current reservoir level, FLWL, release capacity. **Judgment/procedure:** Use V first for early adjustment, P for stronger prerelease, and S for final drawdown/flood regulation; use post-event forecasts to prefill. **Branches:** High/stable VCWL permits higher levels; falling VCWL prompts proactive drawdown; sharp VCWL decrease prompts prompt drawdown to FLWL. **Output:** Outflow and level trajectory through pre-event, prerelease, regulation, and prefill periods.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.2.2, p. 5, and event interpretation in §§4.2.1–4.2.2, pp. 7–9. The article does not provide a complete pseudocode rule or all conditional thresholds.

### E21-R3: Use forecast lead time as a staged information basis

**Description:** Forecast accuracy decreases with longer lead time. The authors therefore use qualitative estimates two weeks/7–14 days ahead, then quantitative peak flow/time predictions 3–5 days ahead and one day ahead. Early uncertainty supports preparation; later precision supports specific releases and peak staggering.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A flood target is approaching and forecast information updates. **Inputs:** Precipitation/flood forecasts, lead time, expected peak magnitude and time, forecast error. **Judgment/procedure:** Match decision specificity to forecast skill; use early information for capacity preparation and later quantitative information for detailed action. **Branches:** Qualitative long-range forecast → pre-discharge/reserve capacity; quantitative short-range forecast → release reserved upstream capacity and adjust schedules. **Output:** Time-staged operational decisions.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §“Accurate forecast of precipitation and flow,” p. 6 and Table 1. Numerical forecast details are limited to two floods; broader skill characterization is not reported.

### E27-R3: Estimate forecast-error process and simplify applicability

**Description:** Errors at 1–6 h are calculated from forecast/observed inflows for ten floods; tests indicate approximately normal absolute-error distributions. At flows >12,000 m³/s, means and standard deviations vary little with lead time, supporting μ≈0, σ≈600 m³/s stationary approximation for simulation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** generate errors for uncertainty propagation. **Inputs:** rolling hourly forecasts, later observations, lead-time samples, flow regime. **Judgment/procedure:** inspect mean/SD versus lead time and flow; use stationary Gaussian approximation only for high-flow regime. **Branches:** strictly nonstationary error at lower flow; high-flow simplification. **Output:** error-series generator parameters.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4561–4562, Table 1 and Section 2.3. The authors explicitly say low-flow data are too sparse; stationarity is a simulation simplification, not a universal property.

### E42-R6: Interpret lead-time/return-period dependence

**Description:** Longer lead times broaden error distributions; low-return-period design floods are more readily exceeded by ensembles.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** explain differences across lead times and design benchmarks. **Inputs:** lead time, return period, ensemble counts, CI/curtailment outputs. **Judgment/procedure:** compare risk rates and generation losses across four lead times and return periods. **Output:** case-level interpretation that IFU more strongly affects electricity curtailment than high-return-period flood risk.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 13–20, Figs. 7, 11–12, Table 3. The observed relationships are case results; the paper does not fit a universal scaling law.

## RP03-B Forecasts must arrive early enough, with the right indicator

The lead time must cover the time needed to draw down; choose variables that signal floods earlier. Strength: Established. 9 steps, 8 papers.

### E01-R3: Use lead time and release capacity to define an admissible upper level

**Description:** The reservoir must be able to lower its level to the current FLWL before a large inflow arrives. Therefore, the upper bound is linked to effective forecast lead time, downstream safety discharge, and reservoir release capacity.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A candidate upper level could prevent timely drawdown. **Inputs:** Candidate storage (V_u), current-FLWL storage (V_0), future inflow over (T_c), forecast lead time (T_c), and downstream safety discharge (Q_s). **Judgment/procedure:** Use the capacity-constrained mass-balance relation in Eq. (5) to limit the candidate upper level. **Branches:** The level may be allowed above the current FLWL only when pre-release capacity can restore the lower level in time. **Output:** A physically feasible upper-bound candidate.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2, p. 126, Eq. (5). The precise operational scheduling of releases within the lead time is not fully specified beyond the module and case constraints.

### E09-R2: Estimate duration/lead-time information conservatively

**Description:** Duration and forecast lead time govern how much water can be released, but direct short-term dry-duration forecasts are unavailable. Historical data are used to approximate their distributions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Need scenario inputs for dynamic FLWL. **Inputs:** Rainfall series, historical floods and rainfall/flow pairs. **Judgment/procedure:** Count consecutive ≤3-mm dry days; compare negative-exponential fit with asymptotic regression; derive natural lead time as end of main rain to flood peak and fit a normal distribution. **Branches:** Select negative exponential as safer/conservative; use lead times up to effective 10 h. **Output:** Dry-duration and lead-time distributions.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.1–2.2, pp. 4–6, 8–10. No real-time dry-period forecast model is built.

### E11-R1: Choose volume-based control target

**Description:** Large reservoirs can smooth intraday peaks, so maximum 1–5 day flood volume is a useful forecast target for pre-storm storage.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Need a forecast-linked pre-storm level. **Inputs:** Forecast period flood volumes and downstream safety release. **Judgment/procedure:** Use maximum period volumes rather than only peak flow; consider 1,2,3,5-day windows. **Output:** Candidate period controls.

**Evidence boundary:** §§1–2.1, pp. 1–4. Applicability is explicitly focused on reservoirs adequate to regulate peaks.

### E17-R4: Vary the parameters that govern pre-release feasibility

**Description:** Maximum release, ramping rate, and lead time determine whether a policy can ramp releases early enough. Normalizing release/ramping by peak inflow allows comparison across sites.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Explain heterogeneous MPC performance. **Inputs:** (R_{max}), (RR), (LT), event peak inflow, site flood pool. **Judgment:** Sample these three factors globally; treat infrastructure/forecast properties as screening variables. **Output:** Parameter-response surface.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–7. Ranges are chosen for cross-site consistency and do not represent engineering feasibility at every site.

### E19-R2: Identify the forecast-horizon gap in PS-FIRO

**Description:** Precipitation and streamflow forecasts can trigger prerelease, but their horizon limits how early the reservoir can prepare. Vapor conditions are expected to rise before significant precipitation and can provide information beyond the precipitation/streamflow horizon.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Existing forecast horizon is too short for efficient retention/prerelease decisions. **Inputs:** Precipitation, streamflow, PWV, vapor convergence, lead times. **Judgment/procedure:** Use the earliest physically linked indicator for early warning and preserve later forecasts for operational calculations. **Branches:** Vapor is an early indicator; precipitation and streamflow take over as their forecasts become available. **Output:** Rationale for a vapor–precipitation–streamflow sequence.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§1.2–1.4 and §3.2.2, pp. 1–5. The article reports a 72-hour vapor and 48-hour precipitation/streamflow setup for this case, not a universal lead-time rule.

### E19-R3: Define PP as a conservative physical upper bound

**Description:** Starting with the atmospheric water balance, the authors assume all water vapor entering the basin converts to precipitation, with terminal PWV and outward vapor flux set to zero. This yields PP as initial PWV plus inward vapor flux (with evapotranspiration optionally included), a worst-case amount rather than an exact precipitation forecast.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A flood-control decision needs an estimate beyond the direct precipitation horizon. **Inputs:** Initial PWV, inward integrated vapor flux, evapotranspiration, atmospheric-water-balance terms. **Judgment/procedure:** Select conservative boundary assumptions and compute PP using Eqs. (1)–(6); neglect evapotranspiration in the multi-period approximation because it is typically minor. **Branches:** Include water-vapor, cloud-liquid, and cloud-frozen flux components; use multi-period accumulation when needed. **Output:** PP for each future interval/horizon.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.1.2, pp. 3–4, Eqs. (1)–(6). The all-conversion assumption is a safety-oriented modeling assumption; it is not observed precipitation and its conservatism depends on the specified system boundary.

### E27-R2: Select the 6-hour runoff rule indicator

**Description:** The authors select forecast runoff volume over the next 6 h rather than 3 or 12 h because forecast accuracy is high, runoff response is 6–9 h, upstream Shiquan travel time is about 6 h, and 6–8 h volume strongly affects reservoir behavior.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** formulate forecast-based operating rule. **Inputs:** forecast system performance, watershed response/travel times, peak/volume influence analyses. **Judgment/procedure:** choose the horizon that aligns forecast reliability and hydrologic response. **Branches:** 6 h chosen over 3/12 h; rule also branches by reservoir level and runoff thresholds. **Output:** R6 indicator and Table 3 rule.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4563–4565, Section 3.3.2 and Table 3. The rule was built from prior repeated comparisons/example validations; the supplied text does not give all candidate-rule calculations.

### E35-R2: Establish a horizon reference under perfect information

**Description:** Before testing imperfect forecasts, the authors determine the minimum horizon that can release early enough under ideal information.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** choose a useful forecast horizon. **Inputs:** 100-year flood hydrograph, initial high forebay level, candidate horizons 6–48 h. **Judgment/procedure:** compare closed-loop peak releases and forebay traces; select a horizon at which responses stabilize and remain below 200 m³/s. **Branches:** horizons 6/12 h delay release; 18 h or longer give similar safe responses. **Output:** evidence that forecast horizon affects control even without forecast error.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 4.1, pp. 13–14; Fig. 7. The “approximately infinite horizon” interpretation is an assumption from the cited MPC rationale, not a proof.

### E37-R4: Formulate FBO as a forecast-indexed rising-stage rule

**Description:** Forecasted cumulative net rainfall can provide an earlier indicator than current level/inflow alone, but its rule must be tied to water level and release thresholds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** rising-stage flood operation from initial level 325.0 m. **Inputs:** cumulative 72-hour net rainfall, current level, inflow, release thresholds. **Judgment/procedure:** use stepwise cumulative-rainfall bands (≤43, 43–55, 55–62, 62–79, 79–90, >90 mm), with level-dependent releases and free overflow at the highest band. **Output:** FBO release decision.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 3.2, pp. 6–7; Table 2. Rules are explicitly limited to the flood rising stage; (Q_c) is not defined in the supplied text.

## RP03-C Verify forecast quality before use

Whether the forecast meets requirements is a precondition for using it. Strength: Established. 7 steps, 6 papers.

### E01-R6: Represent forecast error probabilistically and calibrate forecast adequacy

**Description:** Since forecast error is unavoidable, the paper assumes relative forecast error can be represented by a zero-mean normal distribution. Its standard deviation is derived from a qualified forecast accuracy obtained from historical records, allowing synthetic forecast series to be generated from observed inflow series.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Monte Carlo needs many plausible forecast/actual pairs. **Inputs:** Observed inflows, qualified forecast accuracy (a), normal quantile, and historical calibration/validation results. **Judgment/procedure:** Estimate (sigma) using Eq. (3), draw standard-normal errors, and generate (hat Q_t=Q_t(1+e_t)). **Branches:** Lead time is evaluated at 1 and 2 days; accuracy declines with lead time. **Output:** Synthetic forecasted flood series and an error distribution for risk simulation.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.1 and 3.3, pp. 125–126 and 129–130, Eqs. (1)–(4), Table 3. Normality is an assumption supported by cited Chinese forecasting practice and prior analysis; the paper does not independently establish universal normality.

### E14-R3: Combine heterogeneous deterministic models by distributional similarity

**Description:** No one AI model wins in all cases. The Cloud model is chosen because it compares forecast and observed distribution characteristics, assigns weights, and can retain complementary model behavior.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Produce a final deterministic inflow forecast. **Inputs:** LSTM, GRU, XGBoost forecasts and observed series divided into M segments. **Judgment/procedure:** Fit Gaussian Cloud characteristics (expectation, entropy, super-entropy), calculate overlap/similarity for each segment and model, average segment similarities, normalize to weights, and combine forecasts. **Output:** Cloud deterministic forecast.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–7, Eqs. (1)–(8), Figure 2. Exact segment boundaries and some model hyperparameters are not in the main text.

### E18-R4: Treat forecast skill and lead time as decision prerequisites

**Description:** FIRO depends on forecast information that is useful before the relevant phenomenon occurs. In the screening framework, forecast skill at a sufficient lead time is identified as a critical component, and poor forecast skill is an example of a Stage A exclusion condition.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Considering whether forecast-informed retention/release can be trusted for a site. **Inputs:** Forecast skill, lead time, precipitation/streamflow products, timing of flood-producing events. **Judgment/procedure:** Ask whether forecast skill is sufficient to support the intended operational flexibility. **Branches:** Poor skill can eliminate a site at Stage A; better skill may permit deeper assessment. **Output:** Forecast-suitability requirement passed to viability/screening.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.1.1, p. 9; forecast lead time is defined there by citation to AMS. The paper does not give a universal numerical skill or lead-time threshold.

### E23-R2: Establish whether ensemble forecasts merit operational use

**Description:** Before using ensemble spread in optimization, the authors evaluate deterministic, probabilistic, and “perfect” rainfall-forced forecasts over five years and multiple lead times. Better ensemble metrics and an additional five days of useful horizon support its use, although rank histograms show under-dispersion.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** select forecast information for optimization. **Inputs:** 2008–2013 forecast/observation period, deterministic and 50-member ECMWF-EPS forecasts, observed-rainfall reference. **Judgment/procedure:** inspect MAE, CRPS, rank histograms, and Brier score (1400 m³/s exceedance). **Branches:** retain ensemble as useful despite incomplete spread; future work for model uncertainty. **Output:** probabilistic forcing selected.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1641–1646, Section 2.4 and Figs. 2–5. Metric superiority is case- and lead-time-dependent; “perfect” forecast is an observed-rainfall reference, not a deployable forecast.

### E28-R2: Convert forecast accuracy into an error distribution

**Description:** The authors need a usable uncertainty distribution with limited data. They assume relative error is normal with zero mean and use either qualified rate α or whole-hydrograph NSE/R² to infer its standard deviation. The NSE route uses the observed/predicted hydrograph rather than only peak or volume.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a forecast accuracy grade or historical forecast series is available. **Inputs:** α or R², permitted relative error εα (0.2 for the qualified-rate expression), observed and predicted inflows, observed mean, sample length. **Judgment/procedure:** use Eq. (3) for α or Eq. (8) for R² under `e_t ~ N(0,σ²)`. **Branches:** α uses peak/volume information; R² uses the full hydrograph; grade ranges may be used when only qualitative grade is known. **Output:** σ for relative forecast error.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–3, Table 1, Eqs. (2)–(8). Normality, zero mean, and the grade mapping are assumptions/standards, not independently established here.

### E28-R5: Calibrate and test the case route

**Description:** A risk estimate requires a forecast model whose accuracy can be checked in the case basin. The authors combine a rainfall–runoff model and a multiple-input single-output linear model, then verify accuracy before applying the forecast-based rule.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** moving from abstract uncertainty to Geheyan operation. **Inputs:** precipitation, evaporation, runoff, upstream inflow, intervening-basin data, 1997–2004 records. **Judgment/procedure:** calibrate on 1997–2002, verify on 2003–2004, assess α, NSE/R², and runoff-volume relative error; Grade A permits official use under the cited Chinese standard. **Output:** forecast model, accuracy values, and inflow hydrographs.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–6, Eqs. (20)–(22), Tables 2–3, Fig. 4. The text reports the split and metrics; it does not report an independent operational trial.

### E36-R2: Choose an error distribution from a forecast metric

**Description:** A forecast-quality metric must be translated into a probability distribution usable for scenario generation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** only α or R² and historical records/grade levels are available. **Inputs:** qualified rate α, permitted relative error εp=20%, or R² and observed hydrograph variance. **Judgment/procedure:** assume zero-mean normal relative error and solve for σ using Eq. (3) for α or Eq. (8) for R². **Branches:** α represents peak-discharge qualification; R² represents whole-hydrograph fit. **Output:** σ and error distribution.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.1.1–2.1.2, pp. 4–6; Eqs. (1)–(8); Table 1. Normality and εp=0.2 are assumptions.

## RP03-D Forecast accuracy is not operational value

High skill does not guarantee better operation; bound the value with no-forecast and perfect-forecast references. Strength: Established. 5 steps, 5 papers.

### E07-R8: Bound the conclusion by forecast skill and site conditions

**Description:** The authors interpret EFO's benefit as conditional on where the hindcast is reliable and on the reservoir's constraints. They note that the upper forecast range is most reliable at 4–15 day leads, that the risk-tolerance curve is sensitive to the training inflow sequence, and that larger events and additional hydraulic/damage modeling are still needed.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Decide whether the proof of concept can transfer to another reservoir or support stronger claims. **Inputs:** Hindcast bias/reliability, lead-time distribution of releases, event coverage, reservoir constraints, and objective function. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat transferability as an interaction between forecast skill/reliability and project-specific limits; require ESP forecasts and a sufficiently long hindcast for another evaluation. **Branches:** Further testing is needed for extreme events, other objectives, multiple reservoirs, and other sites. **Output:** A conditional transferability statement and research agenda.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit on pp. 16–18, Section 5. The paper reports five modeled events with Hopland flows above 15,000 cfs but does not simulate the 1964 flood of record; no universal safety claim is supported.

### E14-R5: Evaluate forecast quality and forecast value separately

**Description:** An optimization can look good under forecasted inflows but be “fake” when judged against actual inflows. Thus forecast metrics, optimization objectives, and post-assessment under Perfect observed inflows are separate evidence layers.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Compare deterministic, ensemble, and operational performance. **Inputs:** Forecast/observation metrics, candidate release trajectories, observed inflows. **Judgment:** Do not infer operational value from forecast skill alone; use Perfect inflows for post-assessment. **Output:** Forecast-quality and forecast-value comparisons.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2, 12–17. “Perfect” is a simulated observed-inflow benchmark, not a deployable policy or true future-information condition.

### E16-R6: Interpret forecast skill and operational response jointly

**Description:** Bias, eCRPS, timing, and ensemble uncertainty affect policy actions differently. The authors therefore diagnose forecast metrics alongside storage/drawdown rather than infer value from one skill score.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Explain differences among event/policy results. **Inputs:** Peak-event observations, ensemble mean bias, eCRPS around 30-day windows, required flood pool. **Judgment:** Relate long-lead skill and timing to drawdown and flood-space requirements, with uncertainty in synthetic sequences. **Output:** Mechanistic but qualified interpretation.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 6–9, Eq. (12)–(13), Figure 7. Synthetic forecasts match bias but not all long-lead eCRPS degradation; one metric cannot explain all behavior.

### E17-R6: Compare HEFS with perfect and baseline references

**Description:** Baseline indicates no-forecast performance, perfect sets information potential, and HEFS tests available ensemble value. Similar HEFS/perfect thresholds indicate practical forecast capture for the event.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Interpret the magnitude of forecast-informed benefit. **Inputs:** Baseline climatology, perfect future inflow, HEFS ensemble, common MPC. **Judgment:** Attribute remaining gap to forecast quality and parameter feasibility, not only policy form. **Output:** Information-value comparison.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 6–12. The 1997 HEFS timing is useful despite underprediction; results may differ for less skillful events.

### E46-R6: Validate generalization and identify forecast value

**Description:** Held-out typhoons test whether calibrated rules achieve stage targets; multi-peak Nari tests transitions and recovery. Pseudo-optimization quantifies the performance gap from lacking perfect forecasts.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** rules are calibrated and need out-of-sample assessment. **Inputs:** four unused typhoons, historical levels/releases, perfect-forecast comparator. **Judgment/procedure:** compare simulated maximum releases/levels and end-of-flood levels with records and pseudo-optimal results. **Output:** validation evidence and bounded claim about robustness.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 11–14, Figures 7–10 and Conclusions. Four-event validation supports case evidence but is not broad climate or deployment validation.

## RP03-E Derive the required forecast accuracy from acceptable risk

Beyond some error the forecast stops being useful; or a risk target implies a minimum accuracy. Strength: Established. 6 steps, 5 papers.

### E05-R6: Treat forecast uncertainty as useful only up to a constraint-dependent threshold

**Description:** The interior solution can respond counterintuitively when (r) becomes large: beyond the stationary point, marginal flood-control utility falls and the model may imply increasing conservation despite poorer forecasts. The authors therefore define the maximum useful uncertainty at the point where this response ceases to be meaningful or a physical/risk constraint binds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Assess whether a forecast with uncertainty (r) can support both objectives. **Inputs:** (RFCC), expected inflow, (x), (r_a), and the constraints on (W_1) and (d). **Judgment/procedure:** Differentiate the optimality relation; define (r_c) by (dW_1^*/dr=0) with mass balance only, (r_{c0}) when (W_1^*=0), (r_e) when (W_1^*=RFCC-d_{min}) and (d=d_{min}), and (r_{cr}) when both (W_1^*=0) and (d=d_{min}). **Branches:** Select (r_{max}) as (r_c), (r_{c0}), or (r_e) by expected-inflow and acceptable-risk conditions; if (r>r_{cr}), even empty storage cannot meet the stated acceptable-risk condition. **Output:** Maximum allowable uncertainty and the active constraint explaining it.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 4.1, pp. 9–13; Figures 6–9; Tables 1–2; Appendices A–B, pp. 16–17. Threshold usefulness is model-defined; it is not independent forecast verification.

### E05-R7: Jointly interpret preference, forecast quality, and operating context

**Description:** Forecast quality informs how much conservation preference can be exercised, while preference changes the maximum uncertainty that still permits conservation. The decision space narrows as uncertainty rises, with the exact direction of (r_{max}) depending on expected inflow and the active constraint.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Choose an operating preference and assess a forecast together. **Inputs:** (x), (r), expected inflow, and (r_a). **Judgment/procedure:** Use Figures 10–11 and the threshold table to identify whether higher preference requires more accurate forecasts, whether the lower/upper preference bounds move, and whether Type 2/3 or objective dominance expands. **Branches:** With relatively small expected inflow, higher conservation preference lowers allowable (r_{max}); with large expected inflow and binding nonnegative-storage constraint, the relationship can reverse as shown in Figure 10c. **Output:** A context-specific preference interval, uncertainty threshold, and rule type.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 4.2, pp. 12–13; Figures 10–11. The relationships are conditional on the model's distribution, constraints, and parameterization.

### E11-R2: Convert forecast skill into an acceptable error bound

**Description:** A design flood probability must remain unchanged under uncertain forecast.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Set dynamic design-period flood. **Inputs:** Forecast skill Cp, variance of observed period volume, desired design exceedance Pdes. **Judgment/procedure:** Use σ²ε=(1−Cp)var(Vts), then choose δP,ts from the forecast-error quantile so Prob(εts≥δP,ts)=Pdes. **Output:** Error allowance and dynamic design volume.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.2, pp. 5–6, Eqs. (1)–(5). Normal error is assumed for illustration.

### E26-R6: Test whether forecast precision changes the trade-off

**Description:** Forecast error affects both objective marginal risks and feasible zones. Better current-phase precision can permit more conservation; better future-phase precision changes shortage-risk balance and may reduce/increase current release depending on conditions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** assess robustness to forecast quality. **Inputs:** σɛ1, σɛ2, I2, A, weights. **Judgment/procedure:** generate HR surfaces at three historical precision levels and compare SWA/EWA, release, and zone widths. **Branches:** current-phase and future-phase precision have different directions; nonmonotonic current release is possible. **Output:** sensitivity evidence and applicability condition.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2747–2750, Figs. 6–7 and Conclusions. Trends are numerical/model-based and context-dependent.

### E28-R6: Interpret accuracy sensitivity and reverse-use the risk curve

**Description:** If forecast accuracy is lower, routed-level dispersion and risk increase. The risk curve can therefore be read forward (risk for an accuracy) or backward (accuracy needed for an accepted risk).

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** selecting an accuracy grade or checking an accepted check-flood risk. **Inputs:** risk-vs-accuracy curves, accepted risk, design/check reference. **Judgment/procedure:** compare estimated risk with the threshold; infer the minimum accuracy from Fig. 10 (example: accepted check risk `3×10^-5` implies R² about 0.85 or qualified rate about 0.55 in the reported case). **Branches:** choose the accuracy metric and grade; the R²-based result is described as more reasonable for the full hydrograph. **Output:** forecast-system target or operating-risk interpretation.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 8–9, Figs. 8–10 and Conclusions. The reverse threshold is an inference for this case and assumes the model/risk settings remain fixed.

### E36-R8: Use risk curves to guide forecast/level requirements

**Description:** Decision makers can act either by improving forecast quality or by lowering/raising the safe water-level bound, depending on the acceptable risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a risk tolerance is specified. **Inputs:** risk-versus-accuracy curve, risk-versus-safe-level curve, acceptable-risk value. **Judgment/procedure:** locate the accuracy or safe-level point where risk falls below tolerance. **Output:** case thresholds: for 6% acceptable risk, α>90.7% or R²>94.1%; at R²=85%, (Z_s=846.85) m is given.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 3.3, pp. 13–15; Figs. 10–11. These thresholds are case-specific model outputs, not universal operating rules.

## RP03-F Beyond the forecast horizon and unequal horizons across reservoirs

Risk continues after the lead time; upstream and downstream reservoirs have different lead times. Strength: Provisional. 3 steps, 2 papers.

### E33-R2: Split the horizon at each forecast boundary

**Description:** Risk methods limited to the forecast horizon cannot assess the remainder of the operation horizon. The authors consequently distinguish stage 1 (forecast lead-time) from stage 2 (beyond-forecast time).

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** forecast information ends before the operation horizon. **Inputs:** lead-time endpoint and scenario forecasts. **Judgment/procedure:** use forecast scenarios where information exists and design-flood routing afterward. **Branches:** stage-1 failures are counted across scenarios; stage-2 failures are computed through routing. **Output:** two risk components that can be integrated.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in Section 2.1, p. 2; Fig. 1; Eqs. (2)–(4). The causal sequence is a reconstruction from the stated method.

### E33-R3: Reconcile heterogeneous lead-times across reservoirs

**Description:** Forecast periods differ between reservoirs, so their downstream forecast information and post-forecast event timing cannot be treated as synchronized.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** adjacent reservoirs have different forecast lead-times. **Inputs:** each reservoir’s lead-time, upstream historical releases, inter-reservoir travel/routing information. **Judgment/procedure:** derive downstream inflow by channel routing and select design hydrographs whose start-time difference agrees with the lead-time difference. **Branches:** the paper demonstrates the two-reservoir case and says extension to more reservoirs requires rebuilding the end-level/risk relationship. **Output:** aligned multi-reservoir scenario and design-event inputs.

## EP02-D Verify operational usability of forecasts

Coverage, extremes, comparison with reference forecasts, site monitoring capability. Strength: Established. 13 steps, 11 papers.

### E13-E9: Verify synthetic forecast ensembles

**Description:** Compare synthetic forecast properties with the actual HEFS hindcast.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Assess whether generated ensembles mimic the target system. **Inputs:** HEFS and synthetic ensembles, selected leads (1, 3, 5, 10 days), wet-season observations, flow strata. **Procedure:** Compare BSE spread/error relationships, cumulative rank histograms, and eCRPS distributions; run DTS two-sample tests at p = 0.1 for rank histograms and pooled eCRPS as specified. Examine lower 90% and upper 10% observations separately. **Output:** Aggregate parity and discrepancy evidence.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 10–14, Figures 5–6. Stratification can obscure absolute skill, which the authors accept because comparison is the aim; DTS non-rejection means statistical indistinguishability under the test, not proof of identity.

### E13-E10: Validate rare-event forecast attributes

**Description:** Test whether synthetic samples cover HEFS behavior for major inflows.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Aggregate parity is insufficient for flood-risk decisions. **Inputs:** Ten largest hindcast extreme events, 100 syn-HEFS samples (and smaller syn-GEFS sample), cumulative forecasts at selected leads, observed cumulative flow. **Procedure:** Compare distributions/ratios of synthetic versus HEFS cumulative ensemble means and variances; inspect whether HEFS values lie within synthetic sample ranges. **Output:** Event-specific coverage and identified bias, especially variance underprediction at longer leads.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 14–15, Figure 7 and supporting figures. This is event-based validation against a single HEFS realization, not independent observation-based validation of forecast truth.

# Evidence for unit 2.1-decision-relevant-uncertainty

Paper steps assigned to each small pattern of this unit, with their full card text. Step IDs: Exx = paper, Rn = reasoning step, En = execution step.

## RP02-A Forecast uncertainty must enter the decision

Decisions rely on forecasts; treating them as certain causes over- or under-preparation. Strength: Established. 12 steps, 10 papers.

### E04-R1: Frame forecast uncertainty as an operating-risk input

**Description:** Forecasts guide future flood and water-resources decisions, but uncertainty can cause either under-preparation with damage/loss or over-preparation with unnecessary cost and anxiety. Because forecasts are updated in real time, the relevant uncertainty is its evolution across lead times, not a single static error measure.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A forecast is used to guide reservoir action. **Inputs:** Forecasts, observed flows, and changing lead time. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat uncertainty evolution and its propagation through operation as decision-relevant. **Branches:** Under- versus over-preparation are motivating consequences; no separate operational policy is derived for each. **Output:** Need for an uncertainty-evolution and flood-risk chain.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit motivation in Introduction, pp. 1–2. This is the authors’ framing, not evidence of an observed operational failure in the case.

### E07-R2: Choose ensemble risk-based decision support

**Description:** The paper treats forecast uncertainty as a safety-relevant design issue. It selects ensemble streamflow predictions and a transparent decision-support procedure so future reservoir states and the consequences of preliminary release can be examined for every forecast member; prior optimization approaches are discussed as potentially difficult for operators to understand.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A release decision depends on uncertain 1–15 day inflows. **Inputs:** Operational HEFS/ESP forecasts, reservoir and downstream constraints, competing supply and flood objectives. **Judgment/procedure:** Represent uncertainty with multiple equally likely forecast members and make decisions from forecasted risk rather than a single forecast or an opaque optimized policy. **Branches:** Use the ensemble-risk route for EFO; reserve PFO as a hypothetical perfect-forecast comparator. **Output:** An interpretable probabilistic release rule.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 2 and 6–9, Sections 1, 3.2, and 3.3.1. The claim about operator interpretability motivates the route; the paper does not conduct a user study of interpretability.

### E22-R1: Treat inflow forecast uncertainty as an operational decision issue

**Description:** Hourly reservoir flood operation is difficult because inflow forecasts are uncertain. Underestimation can reduce flood-control storage and increase downstream releases; overestimation can cause unnecessary water loss. Ensemble forecasts are chosen to represent and quantify this uncertainty.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Flood operation depends on uncertain future inflow. **Inputs:** NWP precipitation, hydrometeorological observations, inflow uncertainty, reservoir state. **Judgment/procedure:** Propagate multiple plausible forecast scenarios instead of relying on one deterministic inflow. **Branches:** Deterministic/no-forecast approach uses measured precipitation; ensemble approach uses bias-corrected NWP precipitation. **Output:** Probabilistic inflow scenarios for optimization.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§1 and 3.2, pp. 1–3, 10–12. The paper does not quantify forecast uncertainty with a full probabilistic calibration or reliability analysis.

### E23-R1: Frame the operating decision under uncertainty

**Description:** Conventional offline rules do not directly use current forecasts. Online MPC can anticipate inflows, but deterministic forecast use can produce fragile pre-release decisions when the expected event fails to occur. Therefore the decision representation must account for forecast uncertainty.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** short-term flood-control decision with forecasted inflows. **Inputs:** current system state, forecast disturbances, constraints, objective. **Judgment/procedure:** compare proactive benefit against deterministic forecast-robustness risk. **Branches:** deterministic single trajectory versus ensemble/scenario formulation. **Output:** requirement for uncertainty-aware MPC.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicitly reasoned in pp. 1636–1637, Section 1. The statement that meteorological uncertainty is the largest component is a paper hypothesis/working assumption; hydrological model uncertainty is incompletely sampled.

### E27-R1: Distinguish forecast-based decision uncertainty

**Description:** Forecast-based control acts before inflow is realized, so forecast mismatch can cause dam-level risk. Conventional measured-inflow operation also has measurement uncertainty, but it is difficult to quantify directly.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** select or assess forecast-based flood operation. **Inputs:** measured/forecast inflows, lead time, reservoir state, rule, channel/dam limits. **Judgment/procedure:** treat mismatch between forecast/measurement and true inflow as uncertainty entering routing. **Branches:** forecast-based risk quantifiable through simulation; conventional risk acknowledged but not directly comparable. **Output:** uncertainty-analysis requirement.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4558–4560, Sections 1 and 2.1. The relative size of measurement errors is argued qualitatively; no conventional-risk dataset is presented.

### E33-R1: Frame forecast uncertainty as an operational risk

**Description:** Real-time decisions are based on forecast inflows, but stochastic inflow can make an apparently optimal decision unsafe. Risk analysis is therefore needed to balance flood hazard and operational benefit.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a real-time decision must cover future periods. **Inputs:** forecast inflows, reservoir state, critical release or level. **Judgment/procedure:** treat forecast error as a source of possible failure. **Branches:** analytical methods are discussed, but the study chooses numerical simulation because multi-reservoir joint uncertainty is high-dimensional and nonlinear. **Output:** need for a scenario-based risk constraint.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in Introduction, pp. 1–2. The paper does not claim that all uncertainty sources are represented; the case model uses stochastic inflow uncertainty only.

### E34-R3: Use scenarios to expose risk and recourse needs

**Description:** A single deterministic forecast cannot represent the range of future inflows. Discretized scenarios allow risk objectives and constraints to be evaluated over possible outcomes.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** operating with uncertain forecast inflows. **Inputs:** deterministic forecast traces, calibrated copula, scenario count. **Judgment/procedure:** sample correlated forecast errors and superpose them on forecasts, with equal scenario likelihood. **Branches:** scenario confidence can miss actual flows, especially at longer lead-times; rolling updating is the correction mechanism. **Output:** scenario ensemble for optimization and evaluation.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.1.3, p. 4; Section 4.1, p. 9; Fig. 5. Case scenario count is 40; generated scenarios cover most, but not all, actual processes.

### E35-R1: Identify the short-term control conflict

**Description:** The reservoir must release enough water to protect a narrow downstream reach while retaining/recovering water for supply. Forecast errors can delay releases and cause excess downstream spillage.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** flood inflows arrive while storage is high or capacity is limited. **Inputs:** current forebay level, predicted inflow, downstream limit, water-supply target. **Judgment/procedure:** treat flood mitigation and end-of-event storage recovery as simultaneous objectives. **Branches:** guide curves/deterministic MPC are baseline approaches; stochastic MPC is considered when forecasts are uncertain. **Output:** need for uncertainty-aware receding control.

**Evidence boundary:** Introduction, pp. 1–3; Sections 3.1–3.2, pp. 7–9.

### E35-R3: Diagnose deterministic forecast vulnerability

**Description:** A single perturbed forecast trajectory can under- or overestimate inflow and cannot hedge unresolved uncertainty.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** deterministic MPC receives DSF rather than perfect inflow. **Inputs:** ±30% perturbed DSF, current state, hard channel/spillway limits. **Judgment/procedure:** compare DSF-MPC with the perfect reference; observe whether late information shifts releases too late. **Output:** motivation for PSFs/TB-MPC.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 4.2, pp. 14–15; Fig. 8. The paper uses synthetic perturbation, so this diagnostic is not a test of a measured forecast product.

### E36-R1: Treat forecast uncertainty as decision-relevant

**Description:** Release decisions depend on forecast inflow, while observations, model structure, parameters, and boundary conditions introduce uncertainty. Ignoring it makes an optimized plan appear more certain than it is.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** real-time operation uses forecast inflow. **Inputs:** forecast/observed inflow records, reservoir state, operational constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** trace uncertainty into the operation rather than applying a fixed rule only. **Branches:** α or R² can be used as the accuracy summary. **Output:** need for explicit stochastic optimization and risk analysis.

**Evidence boundary:** Introduction, pp. 1–3; Section 2, p. 3. The paper emphasizes inflow uncertainty but does not model every listed source.

### E36-R3: Represent uncertain forecasts as scenario paths

**Description:** A scenario tree provides a discrete approximation to continuous uncertain inflows while retaining time-ordered realizations.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** stochastic optimization needs possible future inflows. **Inputs:** observed inflow, σ, standard-normal samples, horizon and time interval. **Judgment/procedure:** generate (Q_{syn,t}=Q_{obs,t}(1+σe_t)) by Monte Carlo; arrange root-to-leaf paths as the tree. **Output:** scenario set and probabilities.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.1.3, pp. 6; Eq. (9); Section 3.2, p. 10. The case uses 100 equally likely scenarios over 12 three-hour periods.

### E49-R1: Treat forecast uncertainty as the primary operational risk source

**Description:** Real-time decisions depend on forecast flow; deviations between expected and actual levels/releases create flood risk. The authors therefore represent forecast errors probabilistically rather than relying only on a deterministic forecast.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** real-time operation must act before the flood process is observed. **Inputs:** historical observed and forecast inflows, lead times, forecast error samples. **Judgment/procedure:** define forecast uncertainty as observed minus forecast inflow and reproduce its historical stochastic characteristics. **Branches:** uncertainty depends on lead time and has temporal dependence. **Output:** scenario-generating uncertainty model.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 2 and 4, Eq. (1). Forecast uncertainty is treated as the primary source in this study; other uncertainties are not fully modeled.

## RP02-B Uncertainty does not come only from forecasts

Initial level, hydrograph shape, discharge capacity, storage curves, and wind also change outcomes. Strength: Established. 9 steps, 8 papers.

### E01-R2: Treat both forecast error and flood-shape uncertainty as risk sources

**Description:** Earlier FLWL approaches cited by the authors did not include inflow forecasting uncertainty. The paper reasons that both forecast error and uncertainty about the rising/receding flood hydrograph can cause the reservoir to be too high when a large inflow arrives.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A dynamic level is being considered using an imperfect forecast. **Inputs:** Forecasted and actual inflow, effective lead time, possible hydrograph shapes, and reservoir level. **Judgment/procedure:** Separate decision error caused by forecast classification from risk caused by the future hydrograph and route both into the risk calculation. **Branches:** Forecast error is split into incorrect refill versus incorrect pre-release; hydrograph uncertainty is handled through (R(Z,P)). **Output:** A multi-source risk model rather than a deterministic forecast rule.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.1 and 2.4.1, pp. 125–127; Eqs. (1)–(8). The supplied text does not provide a separate empirical distribution for hydrograph shape beyond the frequency/routing formulation.

### E06-R1: Frame the uncertain operating risk

**Description:** Flood forecasts guide reservoir operation, but forecast error, hydrograph timing/shape, discharge capacity, storage–level relations, and wind effects make targets such as highest water level and peak outflow random. The paper therefore defines flood-control risk as the probability that a load indicator exceeds a safe resistance.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A flood-control decision must be assessed under uncertain future inflow and system response. **Inputs:** Candidate uncertainty sources and safe water-level or safe-release thresholds. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat highest water level or downstream peak discharge as load and the corresponding safe value as resistance. **Branches:** The general framework allows either water-level or discharge exceedance; the TGR case evaluates highest-water-level exceedance. **Output:** A multi-uncertainty risk-analysis problem.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 1–2 and Section 4.1, Eq. (10). The paper does not model social/economic flood consequences; “risk” is an operational exceedance indicator.

### E06-R5: Represent remaining uncertainties with tractable distributions and sampling

**Description:** Discharge capacity, storage error, backwater height, and wave run-up are treated as independent variables suitable for LHS. Their distributions encode the paper’s case assumptions: normal discharge coefficient and storage error, Gumbel backwater height, and Rayleigh wave run-up.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Hydraulic and structural uncertainties must be propagated jointly with the simulated inflow. **Inputs:** Set discharge, measured storage, reservoir characteristics, and wind-related variables. **Judgment/procedure:** Model actual discharge as a coefficient times set discharge; model actual storage as measured storage plus error; use Gumbel/Rayleigh wind distributions. Use LHS to obtain uniform coverage with fewer simulations than ordinary Monte Carlo. **Branches:** Backwater height is found to be about order 10^-4 in the TGR case and is ignored for that case. **Output:** Joint samples of λ, v, e, and h_p for routing.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–4, Section 2.3 and Eq. (3)–(5); Section 5.2.3, Table 5 and Fig. 10. Independence and distributional forms are assumptions or fitted case choices; exact spillway/reservoir-derived variance procedures are not fully reported.

### E08-R2: Derive distributions for initial storage and forecast inflows

**Description:** Excess storage consists of pre-release water for floodwater use and flood-control pre-release. Its distribution determines the uncertain initial storage of the next flood.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Build probabilistic initial conditions. **Inputs:** Rainless-day error, average recession flow, early-flood inflow, forecast-error distributions, safety discharge. **Judgment/procedure:** Use improved truncated Gaussian for rainless-day error and normal relative error for flood inflow; combine excess-storage terms and transform W to V0. **Branches:** Rainless-day horizons 1–7; one-factor versus joint uncertainty. **Output:** Density of V0 and stochastic Q/IU inputs.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.1–2.2, Eqs. (1)–(6), pp. 2–3, Appendices A–B. Independence of weather and flood forecast errors is an assumption.

### E09-R2: Estimate duration/lead-time information conservatively

**Description:** Duration and forecast lead time govern how much water can be released, but direct short-term dry-duration forecasts are unavailable. Historical data are used to approximate their distributions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Need scenario inputs for dynamic FLWL. **Inputs:** Rainfall series, historical floods and rainfall/flow pairs. **Judgment/procedure:** Count consecutive ≤3-mm dry days; compare negative-exponential fit with asymptotic regression; derive natural lead time as end of main rain to flood peak and fit a normal distribution. **Branches:** Select negative exponential as safer/conservative; use lead times up to effective 10 h. **Output:** Dry-duration and lead-time distributions.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.1–2.2, pp. 4–6, 8–10. No real-time dry-period forecast model is built.

### E20-R3: Represent uncertain forcing and initial state stochastically

**Description:** The conceptual framework treats inflow hydrographs and initial storage as random variables, producing output distributions rather than one deterministic trajectory. In the case studies, initial level is simplified to the top of conservation pool for every event, an acknowledged design-practice simplification.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Calibration should cover different hydrologic situations and potentially uncertain initial conditions. **Inputs:** Distributions/ensembles of inflows and initial storage, RFCM parameters. **Judgment/procedure:** Propagate the input ensemble through the operating model and evaluate output distributions; simplify initial state only for the case-study implementation. **Branches:** General framework permits random initial states; this implementation fixes them at TCP. **Output:** Distribution of model behavior for each parameter set.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §2.1, §3, and p. 8. The paper says initial-state distributions and Monte Carlo should be added in future work; thus the implemented cases do not test initial-level uncertainty.

### E30-R1: Define the uncertainty-bearing decision problem

**Description:** Inflow forecast, discharge, and storage-curve errors make actual levels differ from deterministic routing, creating safety risk in real-time decisions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a forecast-based flood-control program is used. **Inputs:** forecast inflow, release relation, storage–level relation, observations, flood duration. **Judgment/procedure:** treat uncertain level as the load and a selected safe/controlled level as the threshold. **Branches:** point-in-time risk and total process risk are both needed. **Output:** probabilistic risk model requirement.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–2. The paper motivates the factors; the exact empirical identification of each case uncertainty is not fully reported.

### E31-R1: Expand the uncertainty representation

**Description:** IFE, OE, and SOE all perturb real-time levels, and temporal continuity makes successive errors correlated. Earlier models’ omissions can reduce reliability of total-risk estimates.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** deterministic real-time routing is being used for a reservoir flood. **Inputs:** forecast, discharge curve, storage curve, temporal error behavior. **Judgment/procedure:** represent each source as a stochastic variable and add adjacent-time correlation for IFE, OE, and SOE. **Branches:** evaluate each source separately and all sources together. **Output:** six-factor uncertainty specification.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–3. The authors state the rationale; cross-source correlations are not included in the stated six factors.

### E39-R1: Select risk sources tied to forecast-informed control

**Description:** For a high-regulation reservoir, flood volume is treated as the main factor affecting regulation; cumulative net rainfall is used as its proxy, and its forecast error is the second source.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** forecast information is used to decide releases before a flood. **Inputs:** forecast cumulative net rainfall Y-bar, actual cumulative net rainfall Y, error E. **Judgment/procedure:** model Y-bar = Y + E and use E,Y as joint risk sources. **Branches:** flood volume W could be used equivalently; the authors retain cumulative rainfall. **Output:** two-dimensional source space.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–5, Section 2.1–2.2, Eqs. (1), (6), (11)–(14). The restriction to volume rather than peak is argued to suit high-regulation reservoirs.

## RP02-C Keep only decision-relevant sources and state exclusions

Too many sources exist; screen by importance and tractability and state what was excluded. Strength: Established. 4 steps, 3 papers.

### E08-R1: Frame the DC-FLWL trade-off and uncertainty scope

**Description:** Pre-refill/pre-release can increase floodwater utilization but forecast error may cause FLWL exceedance. The paper focuses on initial water-level uncertainty and flood-forecast error as primary risk sources.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Consider operating above/around the design FLWL during a flood season. **Inputs:** Recession/rainless-day forecast, forecast inflows, current storage, downstream safety discharge. **Judgment/procedure:** Include the two uncertainty sources jointly; exclude discharge capacity and storage-curve uncertainty by scope choice. **Branches:** Initial-level only, inflow only, or both factors. **Output:** Defined stochastic risk problem.

**Evidence boundary:** §§1–2, pp. 1–3. Exclusions are explicit; extractor does not infer omitted factors are negligible.

### E29-R2: Decide what uncertainty can be represented

**Description:** BFS is selected because it supplies probabilistic inflows around a deterministic hydrological forecast. Quantitative rainfall forecasts were unavailable, so only hydrological/model uncertainty is discussed.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** deterministic inflow forecasts are available but error-free use is inappropriate. **Inputs:** past observed discharge `h`, model output `s`, current observations `h0`, lead time. **Judgment/procedure:** estimate conditional posterior density via prior and likelihood, using a meta-Gaussian normal-quantile transform and log-Weibull marginal distributions. **Branches:** rainfall-input uncertainty is omitted in this implementation. **Output:** probabilistic inflow hydrograph.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–3, Eq. (1), BFS description. The omitted rainfall-input uncertainty is explicit; the text does not claim full predictive uncertainty.

### E37-R1: Compare uncertainty structures of CO and FBO

**Description:** FBO introduces forecast-factor error, but CO’s observed inflow is also imperfect. The mode itself does not determine risk without considering the uncertainty pathway and operation response.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** select a flood-regulation mode for risk analysis. **Inputs:** forecast/observed inflow, initial level, storage/discharge relations, operation and decision factors. **Judgment/procedure:** separate hydrological, hydraulic, and decision-making uncertainties; identify the forecast-versus-observed inflow item as the main difference. **Branches:** CO has no forecast error term; FBO has forecast-factor error. **Output:** comparable risk-factor inventory.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.1–2.2, pp. 2–4; Fig. 1. The paper does not quantify every listed uncertainty.

### E37-R2: Prioritize forecast/net-rainfall uncertainty

**Description:** The authors judge hydrological uncertainty, especially forecasted inflow or forecast factor, to be the most significant and relatively more tractable risk source.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** full risk model would contain many heterogeneous factors. **Inputs:** literature/qualitative factor analysis and available forecast samples. **Judgment/procedure:** focus quantitative analysis on forecasted net-rainfall error while treating other factors as relatively stable or secondary. **Output:** reduced risk-analysis scope.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2, p. 4. This is a modeling choice and boundary, not proof that other errors are negligible in every setting.

## RP02-D Error structure is not a single fixed distribution

Errors vary with lead time, flow, and season, or are non-normal, multimodal, or fuzzy. Strength: Established. 15 steps, 10 papers.

### E02-R2: Represent evolving forecast uncertainty

**Description:** The authors choose MMFE to simulate the evolution of forecast errors and create synthetic ensemble forecasts for the methodological demonstration. They state that a real-time ensemble forecast model would replace MMFE in a real application.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Forecasts are updated over time and their uncertainty changes with lead time. **Inputs:** forecast-update increments and a variance–covariance matrix. **Judgment/procedure:** Assume zero-mean, Gaussian, temporally independent, stationary update increments and use the VCV structure to generate scenario trees. **Branches:** Synthetic MMFE is used for testing; real-world deployment would require a real ensemble forecast source. **Output:** An ensemble of inflow trajectories for each decision time.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.1 and 3.2–3.3, pp. 3–5 and 11–13. The assumptions are explicit. VCV base values and ranges in the case experiments are subjectively designed, not calibrated from a reported live forecast system (pp. 11–12).

### E04-R2: Replace restrictive uncertainty-evolution assumptions

**Description:** Earlier martingale and generalized-martingale approaches are described as relying on assumptions including unbiasedness, Gaussianity, temporal independence, or stationarity; the normal-quantile transform also adds transformation and inverse-transformation work. The authors reason that a method accommodating violated assumptions is needed.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** The observed forecast-error process may be biased, non-Gaussian/heavy-tailed, or nonstationary. **Inputs:** Limitations of prior methods and hydrologic error behavior. **Judgment/procedure:** Select a copula formulation that separates marginal distributions from dependence and permits arbitrary marginals and higher-dimensional dependence. **Branches:** Vine copulas are considered less suitable because additional structure would be needed to select a reasonable specification; meta-elliptical copulas are used. **Output:** CUE model route.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in Introduction and Section 2.1.1–2.1.3, pp. 2–5. The paper’s statement that copula construction “requires” independent variables is reported only as part of its argument; the extraction does not generalize that statement.

### E04-R3: Normalize and decompose forecast-error evolution

**Description:** Absolute error is affected by flow magnitude, so the study uses relative error. The uncertainty change between adjacent forecast updates is represented as a single-period reduction, allowing longer-lead uncertainty to be reconstructed as a sum of reductions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Forecast errors must be compared across different flow magnitudes and lead times. **Inputs:** Predicted flow (Q_{s,t}), observed flow (q_t), and rolling forecast errors. **Judgment/procedure:** Compute relative error (re_{s,t}=(Q_{s,t}-q_t)/q_t), define (rw_{s,t}=re_{s,t}-re_{s-1,t}), and use the additive evolution relation in Eq. (9). **Branches:** When (s=t), observed flow is substituted and error is zero, so the diagonal reduction is (-re_{t-1,t}). **Output:** Four aligned reduction variables for a four-day horizon.

**Evidence boundary:** Equations (2), (4)–(9), Sections 2.1.1–2.1.2, pp. 2–5. The additive decomposition is the paper’s modeling representation; it does not establish a universal physical law.

### E04-R4: Respect seasonal nonstationarity

**Description:** The cited TGR analysis found the full flood-season reduction series to be nonstationary. The authors therefore avoid one distribution for the entire season and distinguish the June pre-flood period from July–September main flood season.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Stationarity of the reduction series is not supported. **Inputs:** TGR forecast-error/reduction series and season dates. **Judgment/procedure:** Fit separate marginal and joint distributions for June and July–September. **Branches:** Season-specific parameters and copulas are used. **Output:** Two calibrated CUE components.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in Section 3.2, p. 720. The supplied text does not report a new stationarity test statistic beyond the cited prior conclusion.

### E04-R5: Select a marginal family from empirical fit evidence

**Description:** The authors compare hydrologically used candidate distributions rather than imposing a normal marginal. They use fit diagnostics to select the generalized logistic (GL) distribution, noting that normality is rejected for X1–X3 in the main flood season.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Each reduction variable needs a marginal CDF. **Inputs:** Observed reduction series for each season and variable. **Judgment/procedure:** Estimate candidate parameters by L-moments; compare bias, RMSE, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov p-values; accept a fit when the reported p-value is above the selected significance level and consider bias/RMSE. **Branches:** α=0.05 generally; α=0.005 for main-season X2 because all candidate p-values were very small. **Output:** GL marginal distributions for subsequent copula fitting.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.1.3.1 and 3.2, pp. 5 and 720–722; Tables 3–4 and Figs. 7–8. Fit selection is case-specific and does not prove GL is universally best.

### E06-R2: Reject restrictive distribution/independence assumptions

**Description:** Prior analytical approaches are described as relying on normal, independent uncertainties, while forecast errors can be skewed, heavy-tailed, and dependent across lead times. Monte Carlo-style stochastic simulation is selected because multiple uncertainties are difficult to describe analytically in a multi-reservoir or complex system.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Uncertainty structure violates or may violate normality and independence assumptions. **Inputs:** Forecast-error characteristics, lead-time dependence, and the number/type of uncertainty sources. **Judgment/procedure:** Prefer stochastic simulation; preserve dependence where reported and avoid a blanket normal approximation for forecast errors. **Branches:** Use copulas for dependent/skewed series; use LHS for the other variables treated as independent. **Output:** A structure-matched simulation design.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 2–3, Sections 1–2. This is the authors’ methodological rationale; the paper does not provide a formal model-selection comparison against every analytical method.

### E06-R3: Select forecast-error marginals and dependence model

**Description:** Because forecast-error distributions are not assumed normal and differ by lead time, the authors fit ten hydrological candidate marginals, select the best using RMSE and AIC, and represent the joint dependence with a metaelliptical Student-t copula. MLE estimates the joint-copula parameters and L-moments estimate marginal parameters.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A joint model is needed for the three lead-time forecast errors. **Inputs:** Observed relative forecast-error series at each lead time. **Judgment/procedure:** Fit EXP, GEV, GLO, GNO, GPA, Gumbel, Normal, P-III, Wakeby, and Weibull candidates; select the smallest reported RMSE/AIC combination, yielding Wakeby for all three lead times; use Student-t copula for joint dependence. **Branches:** The paper discusses vine copulas but chooses metaelliptical structure because it can represent arbitrary pairwise dependence without the stated vine-structure burden. **Output:** Fitted marginal and joint distributions for forecast-error simulation.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–3, Section 2.1; Appendix; Tables 2–3 and Figs. 6–7. The supplied OCR does not expose all joint-copula parameter values clearly.

### E12-R2: Represent forecast uncertainty by its distribution

**Description:** Maximum/minimum-error rules are safe but optimize rare extremes; distribution-based rules target the most probable forecast and assess the remaining risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Need an operating rule under uncertain forecasts. **Inputs:** Forecast errors. **Judgment/procedure:** Fit maximum-entropy error distribution; identify mode δ0 and 99.99% interval δmin–δmax. **Output:** Forecast scenarios for optimization/evaluation.

**Evidence boundary:** §2.1, pp. 3 and 10–12. Distribution fitting details/data are limited in supplied text.

### E13-R1: Diagnose hindcast coverage risk

**Description:** Short (roughly 10–35 year) hindcasts and small ensembles can omit rare forecast-error patterns. A policy calibrated and evaluated on one hindcast realization may not generalize to future forecast progressions, especially where a few atmospheric-river events dominate water and flood outcomes.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Designing or assessing FIRO under sparse extreme-event evidence. **Inputs:** Hindcast duration, ensemble size, observed events, policy dependence on forecasts. **Judgment:** Treat repeated plausible forecast realizations as necessary to assess robustness. **Output:** Need for synthetic forecast generation and out-of-sample testing.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 1–3. The paper argues for robustness testing; it does not establish that all unobserved forecast behavior is correctly represented.

### E27-R3: Estimate forecast-error process and simplify applicability

**Description:** Errors at 1–6 h are calculated from forecast/observed inflows for ten floods; tests indicate approximately normal absolute-error distributions. At flows >12,000 m³/s, means and standard deviations vary little with lead time, supporting μ≈0, σ≈600 m³/s stationary approximation for simulation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** generate errors for uncertainty propagation. **Inputs:** rolling hourly forecasts, later observations, lead-time samples, flow regime. **Judgment/procedure:** inspect mean/SD versus lead time and flow; use stationary Gaussian approximation only for high-flow regime. **Branches:** strictly nonstationary error at lower flow; high-flow simplification. **Output:** error-series generator parameters.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4561–4562, Table 1 and Section 2.3. The authors explicitly say low-flow data are too sparse; stationarity is a simulation simplification, not a universal property.

### E35-R4: Represent uncertainty as evolving and flow-dependent

**Description:** Longer lead-times are less reliable, and forecast uncertainty is expected to be larger during increasing rainfall-driven flow than during falling/no-rain flow.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** construct an ensemble over the forecast horizon. **Inputs:** DSF sequence, initial uncertainty, successive DSF changes, flow direction, selected terminal relative standard error. **Judgment/procedure:** increase relative standard error toward the horizon; hold it when DSF falls and increase it when DSF rises; propagate member correlation through successive differences and truncate negative flows. **Output:** PSF matrix with a mean/spread evolving by lead-time.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.1, pp. 4–6, Eqs. (1)–(7). The cumulative terminal spread is selected a posteriori by trial and error; no formal calibration is reported.

### E37-R3: Test the forecast-error distribution before simulation

**Description:** The Monte Carlo generator requires a probability model. A visual normal fit is insufficient, so a chi-square goodness-of-fit test is applied.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** historical forecast-error sample is available. **Inputs:** 149 net-rainfall forecast-error points, candidate normal parameters, five intervals, significance level 0.05. **Judgment/procedure:** estimate mean/standard deviation, calculate χ², compare with the critical value; accept normality when 4.1926 < 5.9915. **Output:** (X\sim N(0.019,0.188^2)) as reported.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 3.3, pp. 8–9; Fig. 4; Eqs. (8)–(10); Table 3. The source alternates between variance/standard-deviation typography; the conclusion reports (N(0.019,0.188^2)).

### E41-R2: Represent forecast error as fuzzy uncertainty

**Description:** The authors distinguish randomness from fuzziness and argue that forecast error contains vagueness not fully represented by a probability distribution. Credibility is chosen because it is self-dual and gives differentiated confidence values.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** optimize FLWL under imprecise forecast error. **Inputs:** historical relative flood-volume forecast errors. **Judgment/procedure:** use a fuzzy variable with Cauchy membership; use credibility rather than possibility. **Output:** credibility distribution and chance constraint.

## RP02-E Errors are dependent

### E04-R6: Select a dependence model that can represent extreme dependence

**Description:** After checking autocorrelation, the authors treat the variables as only slightly time-dependent and use a four-dimensional Student t copula. **Retained details:** **Trigger:** The four reduction variables must be generated jointly. **Inputs:** Season-specific marginal transforms, dependence observations, and lag diagnostics. **Judgment/procedure:** Inspect autocorrelation at lags 1–23 (reported values usually within −0.2 to 0.2), then estimate Student t copula parameters by maximum likelihood. **Branches:** Separate copulas are fit for June and July–September. **Output:** Season-specific correlation/degrees-of-freedom parameters and joint generator.

## RP02-H The size of uncertainty changes the optimal decision

### E03-R4: Translate forecast uncertainty and acceptable risk into a safety requirement

**Description:** A future release can exceed the downstream conveyance threshold because forecast inflow differs from actual inflow. ### E05-R6: Treat forecast uncertainty as useful only up to a constraint-dependent threshold

**Description:** The interior solution can respond counterintuitively when (r) becomes large: beyond the stationary point, marginal flood-control utility falls and the model may imply increasing conservation despite poorer forecasts. ### E13-E9: Verify synthetic forecast ensembles

**Description:** Compare synthetic forecast properties with the actual HEFS hindcast.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Assess whether generated ensembles mimic the target system. **Inputs:** HEFS and synthetic ensembles, selected leads (1, 3, 5, 10 days), wet-season observations, flow strata. **Procedure:** Compare BSE spread/error relationships, cumulative rank histograms, and eCRPS distributions; run DTS two-sample tests at p = 0.1 for rank histograms and pooled eCRPS as specified. Examine lower 90% and upper 10% observations separately. **Output:** Aggregate parity and discrepancy evidence.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 10–14, Figures 5–6. Stratification can obscure absolute skill, which the authors accept because comparison is the aim; DTS non-rejection means statistical indistinguishability under the test, not proof of identity.

### E13-E10: Validate rare-event forecast attributes

**Description:** Test whether synthetic samples cover HEFS behavior for major inflows.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Aggregate parity is insufficient for flood-risk decisions. **Inputs:** Ten largest hindcast extreme events, 100 syn-HEFS samples (and smaller syn-GEFS sample), cumulative forecasts at selected leads, observed cumulative flow. **Procedure:** Compare distributions/ratios of synthetic versus HEFS cumulative ensemble means and variances; inspect whether HEFS values lie within synthetic sample ranges. **Output:** Event-specific coverage and identified bias, especially variance underprediction at longer leads.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 14–15, Figure 7 and supporting figures. This is event-based validation against a single HEFS realization, not independent observation-based validation of forecast truth.

### E34-E9: Diagnose scenario coverage and forecast behavior

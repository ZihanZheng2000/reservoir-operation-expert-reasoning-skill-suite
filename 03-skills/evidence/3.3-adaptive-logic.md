# Evidence for unit 3.3-adaptive-logic

Paper steps assigned to each small pattern of this unit, with their full card text. Step IDs: Exx = paper, Rn = reasoning step, En = execution step.

## RP07-A Different flood stages need different actions

Prerelease, rising-limb detention, peak, recession, and refill each follow their own rules. Strength: Established. 8 steps, 6 papers.

### E01-R4: Select among pre-release, normal control, and refill according to forecast thresholds

**Description:** The decision depends on whether current and remaining-lead-time maximum inflows exceed the downstream safety discharge. This creates distinct operating alternatives: pre-release before a threatening inflow, normal current-rule flood control, or refill during recession when the forecast threat is below the safety threshold.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Each decision time during a flood hydrograph. **Inputs:** Current inflow (Q_t), maximum forecast inflow over remaining (T_c), (Q_s), current reservoir level, and the candidate upper bound. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare current and forecast maxima with (Q_s), then choose the relevant operating mode. **Branches:** Pre-release when the forecast maximum is in the specified threatening range; normal operation for a large forecasted inflow; refill when the forecasted inflow is below (Q_s). **Output:** An operating mode and associated release/level requirement.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.2–2.4.1, pp. 126–127; Fig. 4, Fig. 5, and Table 4, p. 131. The exact general thresholds are case-specific in Table 4.

### E09-R1: Identify when water-use pre-release is valid

**Description:** During a forecast dry period, inflow can be below utilization discharge, so releasing at that rate converts otherwise excess floodwater into conservation/hydropower use. Waiting for the next large-flood forecast can miss this opportunity.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Reservoir is above/near planned upper FLWL and a dry interval is expected. **Inputs:** Inflow, utilization discharge, rainfall forecast/dry duration. **Judgment/procedure:** Start water-use pre-release when inflow is below utilization discharge; switch to flood-control pre-release when a large inflow is forecast. **Branches:** Dry forecast versus imminent flood forecast. **Output:** Two-period release plan.

**Evidence boundary:** §§1–2, pp. 3–4. Trigger details beyond the conceptual rule are not fully operationalized.

### E19-R5: Sequence the three forecast defenses

**Description:** Vapor convergence precedes precipitation and streamflow. The authors reason that VPS-FIRO should lower levels when VCWL decreases, increase releases when substantial precipitation is forecast, and reach FLWL using streamflow forecasts before the flood. After the event, lower-risk vapor conditions allow prefill.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Forecast indicators change across their respective horizons. **Inputs:** VCWL/PP, precipitation forecasts, streamflow forecasts, current reservoir level, FLWL, release capacity. **Judgment/procedure:** Use V first for early adjustment, P for stronger prerelease, and S for final drawdown/flood regulation; use post-event forecasts to prefill. **Branches:** High/stable VCWL permits higher levels; falling VCWL prompts proactive drawdown; sharp VCWL decrease prompts prompt drawdown to FLWL. **Output:** Outflow and level trajectory through pre-event, prerelease, regulation, and prefill periods.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.2.2, p. 5, and event interpretation in §§4.2.1–4.2.2, pp. 7–9. The article does not provide a complete pseudocode rule or all conditional thresholds.

### E21-R3: Use forecast lead time as a staged information basis

**Description:** Forecast accuracy decreases with longer lead time. The authors therefore use qualitative estimates two weeks/7–14 days ahead, then quantitative peak flow/time predictions 3–5 days ahead and one day ahead. Early uncertainty supports preparation; later precision supports specific releases and peak staggering.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A flood target is approaching and forecast information updates. **Inputs:** Precipitation/flood forecasts, lead time, expected peak magnitude and time, forecast error. **Judgment/procedure:** Match decision specificity to forecast skill; use early information for capacity preparation and later quantitative information for detailed action. **Branches:** Qualitative long-range forecast → pre-discharge/reserve capacity; quantitative short-range forecast → release reserved upstream capacity and adjust schedules. **Output:** Time-staged operational decisions.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §“Accurate forecast of precipitation and flow,” p. 6 and Table 1. Numerical forecast details are limited to two floods; broader skill characterization is not reported.

### E21-R4: Allocate reserved capacity by flood type, role, and encounter timing

**Description:** The upper cascade reserves capacity for cooperation; surplus capacity can share downstream tasks when not all flood types need simultaneous protection. Release order depends on flood-season stage, reservoir classification, flood encounter situation, local task, and role relative to TGR.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A downstream/control reservoir needs help and upstream capacity is available. **Inputs:** Flood type, flood-season stage, reservoir class, current inflow/level, reserved/surplus capacity, travel time, downstream objective. **Judgment/procedure:** Select which reservoir's reserved capacity to use and when, so peaks are staggered and basin risk is reduced. **Branches:** Use different schemes for mainstream/tributary events; release capacity gradually when post-flood inflow declines; avoid releasing all capacity when simultaneous flood-type protection is not needed. **Output:** Ordered, coordinated capacity-release plan.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 3, 6, and Methods p. 7. Exact allocation algorithm/thresholds and formal risk proof are not reported.

### E46-R1: Decompose the flood response into stages

**Description:** Before arrival the reservoir must create detention capacity, before the peak it must detain inflow without exceeding safety, and after the peak it must recover conservation storage. These objectives differ enough to warrant separate rules and transitions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** flood operation begins or changes phase. **Inputs:** measured inflow, water level, rainfall, peak/recession condition, target levels. **Judgment/procedure:** classify current stage from observable hydrologic conditions. **Branches:** three stages and post-peak sub-stages; multi-peak events may re-enter relevant behavior. **Output:** active stage objective and rule set.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–3, Section 1 and Figure 1. The stage labels are explicit; exact transition logic is case-specific.

### E46-R4: Choose rule parameters by stage-specific trade-off

**Description:** Each stage has a distinct objective function and weighting factor. The first stage trades level accuracy against smooth release; the second trades peak-release reduction against avoiding unnecessary surcharge above NL; the third minimizes end-of-operation level error.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** multiple feasible rules satisfy safety but differ in smoothness or conservation. **Inputs:** simulated end-stage levels, release changes, maximum releases, penalties, PMF levels. **Judgment/procedure:** vary wI and wII and number of post-peak steps, then inspect empirical target-level coverage and maxima. **Output:** selected wI = 0.2, wII = 2, and l = 2 for the case.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–7 and 8–11, Tables 2, 4, and 6. Weight selection is case-based; no formal uncertainty quantification is reported.

### E49-R6: Explain risk hedging through release timing

**Description:** The authors interpret lower adverse downstream CVaR as resulting from spreading total release over more time before the flood peak, reducing the maximum release and possibly upstream risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a selected solution improves loss but may increase another probability metric. **Inputs:** time-varying CVaR, release/storage trajectories, adverse scenarios. **Judgment/procedure:** compare solution trajectories by period and identify prerelease/homogenization. **Branches:** Solution R has higher CVaR during rising-flood periods but lower CVaR during peak-release periods. **Output:** mechanism explanation for the compromise.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 11–12, Figs. 9–11. The mechanism is an interpretation of simulated trajectories, not a causal field experiment.

## RP07-B Switch actions by thresholds

When a forecast value, risk, or available water crosses a threshold, switch to another branch. Strength: Established. 10 steps, 9 papers.

### E07-R4: Resolve excess risk with a constrained prerelease

**Description:** When the risk test fails, the authors reason that only enough forecast members need to be brought below the threshold to meet the tolerance. They select the lowest-ranked members among those exceeding the threshold, derive release schedules for those members, and retain the most demanding first-day release so all forecast-day risk tests are met while respecting physical and downstream limits.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** For any forecast day, (r_t>R_t). **Inputs:** Excess members, their forecast storage exceedance, forecast horizon, release limits, Hopland 8,000 cfs limit, and ramping constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** Select the smallest set of excess members needed to meet tolerance; distribute each member's volume difference across days through the forecast day; cap infeasible schedule entries at the forecast release limit and redistribute the adjusted volume to preceding days; take the maximum day-1 release over selected members and forecast days. **Branches:** No excess risk means no schedule for that forecast day; a schedule above a limit is adjusted and redistributed. **Output:** A current-day flood-control release that reduces forecasted risk to tolerance.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit computational logic on pp. 9–12, Sections 3.3.1 and Figs. 5–9. The 8 February 1986 example reports 14/61 members above threshold at day 8, (r_8=23.0\%), (R_8=9.8\%), 8 risky members selected, and a 529 cfs day-1 release for that horizon; the all-horizon maximum in the example is 1,240 cfs at day 14. The paper does not provide a formal optimality proof for the heuristic schedule.

### E07-R6: Delegate between flood control and water supply by forecasted risk

**Description:** The paper extends the risk tolerance curve from a release threshold to an operations-delegation signal. When forecasted risk is above tolerance, flood-control operations are simulated; when below, water-supply operations consistent with existing practice are simulated. This replaces storage relative to the guide curve as the conceptual basis for switching operational responsibility.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Compare forecasted risk with the tolerance curve. **Inputs:** Risk by forecast day, tolerance curve, water-supply release requirements, flood-control constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** Route the system to flood-control release logic for excess risk and to water-supply operations for risk within tolerance. **Branches:** Above tolerance → USACE/flood-control mode; below tolerance → Sonoma Water/water-supply mode. **Output:** Operational mode and associated release calculation.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit on pp. 12–13 and 17–18. The delegation is proposed/simulated as a management use of the curve; the paper does not report an implemented institutional authorization protocol.

### E10-R4: Allocate RFH storage to current action

**Description:** Within RFH, current versus second-stage risk determines detention or prerelease allocation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Outer allocation is available at each operation time. **Inputs:** s12, current/second forecast flows, risk densities. **Judgment/procedure:** For s12≥0, allocate detention under KKT; for s12<0, allocate prerelease; endpoint branches select stage with larger marginal risk, interior branch equalizes it. **Output:** s1 and s2, especially current s1.

**Evidence boundary:** §2.3.3, pp. 5–7, Eqs. (17)–(25).

### E15-R4: Use hierarchical pre-fill/pre-release branches

**Description:** A single threshold cannot capture different forecast magnitudes under a release cap. Multiple flow levels and thresholds create graduated actions that preserve generation opportunities while returning to the flood limit before larger events.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Compare forecast inflow, current inflow, and thresholds. **Inputs:** Forecast inflow, current inflow, current storage, optimized levels/thresholds, (R_{lim}), risk-aware volume bound. **Branches:** Below threshold 1 → pre-fill to level 1; between threshold 1 and level 1 → release at level 1 to flood limit; between level 1 and threshold 2 → pre-fill using maximum release cap; between threshold 2 and maximum release → pre-release at cap; current inflow above cap → normal flood control at cap. **Output:** Stage-specific release/storage action.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 7–8, Figure 4. Exact generalization beyond the two-level illustration is encoded in optimized parameters; the rule is not a fixed universal threshold set.

### E24-R6: Convert allocation into operational targets and boundary actions

**Description:** Optimal EFSM determines expected downstream flow targets, reservoir releases after subtracting intermediate flow, and carryover storage. If storage would be below 0 or above K, capacity constraints replace the unconstrained allocation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** implement a class-specific hedging decision. **Inputs:** δ1/δ2, expected upstream/intermediate flows, qthres, initial storage, capacity K. **Judgment/procedure:** set r̄1=qthres−δ1 and r̄2=Tqthres−δ2; schedule r̄u,t=r̄t−q̄d,t; compute s1 and clip/resolve at s1=0 or K using Eqs. (28)–(29). **Branches:** unconstrained; lower-storage bound; upper-storage bound; negative Δ/extreme flood invokes storage and evacuation rationale. **Output:** stage targets, release schedule, carryover storage.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 04014041-6–7, Eqs. (25)–(29). The paper does not specify real-time authorization, gate operations, or emergency fallback implementation.

### E26-R5: Turn KKT solution into three operational zones

**Description:** The current release curve intersects feasible lower/upper release bounds, defining SWA/EWA trigger points and MWA. Relative to A, low-flood/high-shortage conditions conserve maximally; intermediate conditions use R1*; high-flood/low-shortage conditions release at Ru1.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** operate once thresholds are calculated and A observed/forecast. **Inputs:** expected availability A, demand D1, SWA/EWA/MWA, R1*, release bounds. **Judgment/procedure:** apply piecewise rule: Zone I R1=D1; Zone II R1=R1*; Zone III R1=Ru1. **Branches:** A below SWA, within trigger range, or above EWA. **Output:** current release and conservation amount.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2736–2737, Fig. 3 and Eq. (4). Zone labels and directional explanations are explicit; exact threshold computation is tied to model parameters and not a universal rule.

### E29-R5: Apply forecast-triggered prerelease/refill logic

**Description:** The forecast maximum over the lead time determines whether water is retained or released. The rule must balance expected flood protection and water use while staying within bounds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** each real-time time step before the forecasted inflow maximum. **Inputs:** current level, upper/lower bound, forecast maximum, critical inflow `SQin`, lead-time steps `L`, outflow, storage–level relation. **Judgment/procedure:** if forecast max ≤ `SQin`, store inflow while staying below `Zu`; if > `SQin`, prerelease evenly (`W0=L`) before the peak, while keeping the expected level above `Zl`. **Output:** time-varying release and reservoir level.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–4, Eq. (3), `SQin=35,000 m³/s` on p. 4. Exact dispatch/measurement latency and feasibility safeguards are not reported.

### E30-R6: Read risk curves for action or design targets

**Description:** Risk decreases as the controlled level rises; correlation changes the curve non-monotonically in parts of the example. Decision makers can compare estimated risk with an acceptable level or derive a controlled level/remaining precipitation allowance.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a risk estimate exceeds an acceptable level or future precipitation is being screened. **Inputs:** risk–level curve, correlation scenario, acceptable risk, subsequent precipitation forecast. **Judgment/procedure:** if risk is too high, revise the operating program or use emergency responses; for a fixed acceptable risk, solve for `HR`, reserve storage, and compare forecast precipitation with acceptable precipitation. **Output:** candidate level, storage reserve, or response trigger.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 7–8, Figs. 10–12, Applications 1–2. Suggested emergency actions are examples, not actions executed in the study; threshold is assumed by decision makers.

### E31-R6: Translate risk information into operating implications

**Description:** If estimated risk exceeds an acceptable level, a decision maker may revise the operating program or take emergency actions. Risk curves can also quantify a stochastic exceedance chance for a selected level.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** computed total risk is compared with an accepted threshold. **Inputs:** risk curve, selected safe level, acceptable risk. **Judgment/procedure:** compare `PR` with the threshold and use the result to inform an operating-program or emergency-response decision. **Output:** risk-informed recommendation/diagnostic.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 8 and 12, Applications 1–2 and conclusions. The model does not execute, optimize, or field-validate the proposed response.

### E37-R4: Formulate FBO as a forecast-indexed rising-stage rule

**Description:** Forecasted cumulative net rainfall can provide an earlier indicator than current level/inflow alone, but its rule must be tied to water level and release thresholds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** rising-stage flood operation from initial level 325.0 m. **Inputs:** cumulative 72-hour net rainfall, current level, inflow, release thresholds. **Judgment/procedure:** use stepwise cumulative-rainfall bands (≤43, 43–55, 55–62, 62–79, 79–90, >90 mm), with level-dependent releases and free overflow at the highest band. **Output:** FBO release decision.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 3.2, pp. 6–7; Table 2. Rules are explicitly limited to the flood rising stage; (Q_c) is not defined in the supplied text.

## RP07-C Rolling update: execute only the next step

Plan over the whole horizon, execute only the current period, and recompute when new information arrives. Strength: Established. 9 steps, 9 papers.

### E02-R8: Implement only the immediate release and re-plan

**Description:** The authors reason that future decisions should remain adjustable because forecasts and the reservoir state will be updated. They optimize through the end of the horizon but authorize only the immediate-period release from the selected sequence.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** The current time period ends and new hydrologic information becomes available. **Inputs:** selected expected decision sequence, updated forecast, and updated reservoir storage. **Judgment/procedure:** Apply only the immediate release; advance the state using mass balance; regenerate the forecast and rerun optimization, risk assessment, and SMAA-TOPSIS. **Branches:** Future decisions are replaced by recourse decisions after each update. **Output:** A rolling sequence of current-period release decisions and associated risks.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2.4, six-step recourse procedure, pp. 7–8; results and Table 4, pp. 17–18. This is a modeled receding-horizon procedure; no field authorization or human override protocol is reported.

### E07-R5: Use a rolling one-day decision horizon

**Description:** The authors separate a 15-day forecast horizon from a one-day implementation horizon. They reason that the release decision should use all currently available future information but be recalculated the next day with an updated forecast, allowing operations to adapt as uncertainty changes.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Each daily simulation time step. **Inputs:** Current storage and releases, current 15-day ensemble hindcast, other release requirements. **Judgment/procedure:** Simulate all future days and members, select the maximum required first-day release, implement it only for the current day, then advance one day and repeat. **Branches:** Subsequent forecast updates can change the next day's decision; the paper does not describe a separate manual override branch. **Output:** Daily flood-control release and updated simulated storage/downstream flows.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit on pp. 9 and 12, Section 3.3.1. The rolling process is a simulation procedure; live operational update frequency and operator authorization are not evaluated.

### E10-R5: Recompute and adapt through flood

**Description:** A one-step release is selected from the current allocation and then recomputed as new information arrives.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Each 6-hour operation interval until flood ends. **Inputs:** Current state, s1, physical FLWL/MWL/spill constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** Calculate release via routing and constraints; advance to next operation. **Output:** Release/storage trajectory.

**Evidence boundary:** §2.4, p. 7, Fig. 4. Human correction of release fluctuations is mentioned (p. 9).

### E17-R2: Select MPC for scalable control

**Description:** MPC re-optimizes release each timestep, can consume ensemble forecasts directly, avoids policy-training overfit, and scales better than SDP for large samples.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Need thousands of policy evaluations across sites/parameters. **Inputs:** Current storage/inflow and finite-horizon ensemble forecasts. **Judgment:** Use scenario-based convex MPC despite possible forecast-uncertainty sensitivity. **Output:** Repeated release sequence and accumulated storage.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2, 5–6. Alternative trace/tree MPC feedback is left for future work; scalability is a methodological choice, not proof of best operational policy.

### E21-R7: Roll decisions as forecasts update

**Description:** The operation ensemble is generated for different leading times and rolling executed as forecast information is updated. During the 2020 case, rigid conventional schedules were not always applicable, so reservoirs discharged flexibly in real time.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** New forecast/observed-flow information changes expected flood timing or magnitude. **Inputs:** Updated forecasts, current levels/releases, prior scheme, system state. **Judgment/procedure:** Recompute/revise candidate cooperation and adjust actions while respecting current constraints and consultations. **Branches:** Long-lead preparation transitions to short-lead quantitative control; local reservoir actions may change as peak encounters are reassessed. **Output:** Updated release, impounding, level, and generation schedule.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 5–7, especially ICDSS description p. 7. Update frequency, stopping criteria, and safeguards against unstable policy oscillation are not reported.

### E33-R7: Optimize benefit within risk and physical constraints

**Description:** Once flood risk is constrained, the authors choose hydropower generation as the objective and use rolling forecasts so only the current decision segment is implemented before re-optimization.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** real-time operation with updated forecasts. **Inputs:** current storage, forecast inflow scenarios, risk constraint, storage/release/routing/change limits. **Judgment/procedure:** maximize total hydropower over the forecast decision variables, implement the current decision horizon, advance one time interval, and repeat. **Output:** updated release/storage trajectory.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.3 and 3.5, pp. 4, 6–7; Eqs. (6)–(11); Fig. 6. The paper reports the optimization algorithm but not a human authorization or fallback procedure.

### E34-R7: Use recourse to correct forecast-driven decisions

**Description:** Even a scenario set can miss actual flows. Rolling updates provide a mechanism to correct earlier release/storage choices when new information arrives.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a new time period and updated forecast. **Inputs:** implemented current release, updated state, new forecast/scenarios. **Judgment/procedure:** implement only the current period’s decision, update information, rerun the models, and compare with a fixed no-recourse expected-value strategy. **Output:** adaptive operation sequence and evidence about recourse value.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.4, pp. 7–8; Section 4.3, pp. 11–12; Tables 3–4. “Recourse” is a model update mechanism; no operator authorization procedure is reported.

### E35-R7: Use feedback to correct forecast errors

**Description:** Only the first control value is trusted; after actual inflow/state information arrives, the remaining open-loop plan is discarded and recomputed.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** every one-hour receding step. **Inputs:** actual inflow, updated forebay, new DSF/PSF tree. **Judgment/procedure:** implement root release, update state, rebuild optimization, and repeat through 96 h. **Output:** closed-loop operation trajectory.

**Evidence boundary:** Materials and Methods, p. 3; Section 4.3, pp. 15–16; Fig. 1. The paper does not specify human override or emergency fallback.

### E43-R2: Select a tractable predictive control architecture

**Description:** MPC is chosen because it predicts future states, solves constrained release decisions, and replans at each control step. A simplified internal model is used to meet computational requirements, with SOBEK providing detailed system behavior and state updates.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** releases must account for delayed river response and multiple reservoirs. **Inputs:** inflow forecasts, current states, structure equations, objectives, constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** use an internal linear state-space approximation with a high-resolution hydrodynamic simulation outside the optimization loop. **Branches:** model losses are neglected; free-flow structure assumptions apply. **Output:** executable predictive controller.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–7, Equations (1)–(9), Figures 2–3. Rationale is explicit; transfer to other basins is limited by case-specific rainfall-runoff and hydraulic calibration.

## RP07-D Share current decisions; let future decisions branch with information

Two-stage or scenario-tree structure: one action until uncertainty resolves, then branch. Strength: Established. 7 steps, 6 papers.

### E03-R2: Choose a two-stage rolling-horizon decision structure

**Description:** Current inflow is treated as sufficiently predictable over the immediate decision interval, whereas future inflow over the remaining forecast horizon is uncertain. A first stage for the next interval and a second stage for the remaining horizon allow the current release/storage decision to account for future risk. Rolling the window forward incorporates updated forecasts and decisions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Each real-time decision time during the flood season. **Inputs:** Current storage, near-term inflow, forecast horizon, forecasted Stage-2 inflow. **Judgment/procedure:** Optimize one immediate interval jointly with the remaining forecast horizon, then shift the window by one time step. **Branches:** The time step should reflect emergency-management timing and information accuracy; the paper notes 6 h or 1 day as practical examples. **Output:** A current release and carryover storage decision passed to the next rolling window.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–4, Section 2.1 and Figure 1; equations 1–4. The exact field implementation of forecast updating is not reported beyond the rolling-horizon description.

### E10-R3: Allocate storage across RFH and outside RFH

**Description:** Relative marginal risk indicates whether storage should be moved earlier or later, subject to FLWL/MWL bounds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Solve outer two-stage model. **Inputs:** s12, s3, stage risks and densities, storage bounds. **Judgment/procedure:** Apply KKT; if lower bound active, allocate more to later stage; if upper bound active, allocate all available to RFH; otherwise equalize marginal risks. **Output:** s12 and s3.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.3.2, pp. 5–6, Eqs. (11)–(16). KKT sufficiency follows stated convexity.

### E23-R3: Align branching with uncertainty resolution

**Description:** A tree should use one early control while forecasts are unresolved, then permit adaptive controls once observations resolve uncertainty. For a large basin, precipitation timing and inflow lag mean branching should be precipitation-dependent rather than based only on contemporaneous inflow distance.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** convert a full ensemble into an optimizable tree. **Inputs:** ensemble precipitation/inflow trajectories, observable precipitation timing, basin lag. **Judgment/procedure:** use precipitation-dependent distance metrics and multi-stage branch points. **Branches:** fixed tree or tolerance-based maximal reduction; average preserved sample spaces and smooth branch transitions. **Output:** scenario tree with probabilities and nodal control partition.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1637 and 1640–1641, Sections 1 and 2.3; pp. 1646–1647, Fig. 6. The exact general tree design beyond reported fixed binary case settings is not established.

### E23-R5: Formulate risk-aware multi-stage control

**Description:** A probability-weighted objective over scenario branches preserves a shared early decision and allows independent later decisions after branch points. Hard forebay constraints are enforced for every retained branch in the stochastic run; a chance-constraint variant is proposed conceptually for accepting limited violation probability.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** optimize outflow/storage over 10–15 days under scenario inflows. **Inputs:** states, disturbances, routing outputs, control limits, scenario probabilities, cost terms. **Judgment/procedure:** minimize expected stage/terminal cost subject to dynamics and hard constraints using a nodal partition matrix. **Branches:** hard bound for all branches versus optional chance constraint. **Output:** scenario control sequence and predicted states/flows.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1639–1641, Section 2.2; pp. 1647–1648, Section 3.4 and Fig. 7. Exact numerical objective weights and complete constraint parameterization are not legible/reported in the supplied narrative.

### E24-R1: Define the intertemporal flood trade-off

**Description:** Current and future decisions compete for storage/conveyance capacity. More current release can increase current downstream flood risk, while more retained water raises future risk; a two-stage abstraction makes this resource allocation explicit.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** periodically updated flood forecast over an event. **Inputs:** initial storage, upstream/intermediate expected flows, conveyance capacity, current/future periods. **Judgment/procedure:** represent current period as Stage 1 and remaining horizon until flood pool empty as Stage 2; use stage-average square-wave flows. **Branches:** conceptual two-stage model versus more complex real system. **Output:** linked current/future balance.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 04014041-2–3. Intrastage variability, multi-stage detail, and actual system complexity are intentionally simplified.

### E34-R6: Restrict current decisions to information available now

**Description:** Future scenario-specific states are not known when the current release is chosen. A common current release across scenarios is therefore required, while later decisions can differ after uncertainty is realized.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** multistage optimization with current and forthcoming periods. **Inputs:** current time, scenario trajectories, release variables. **Judgment/procedure:** impose nonanticipativity on current-period release; permit later wait-and-see decisions. **Output:** feasible stochastic decision policy.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.3.2, p. 7, Eq. (38). The paper does not claim nonanticipativity for every other variable beyond the stated release constraint.

### E35-R5: Resolve uncertainty through a scenario tree

**Description:** A fully shared average control can become infeasible in a storage-limited reservoir, while independent controls for all ensemble members are not implementable.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** optimize over ensemble forecasts with hard storage/release constraints. **Inputs:** PSF ensemble, probabilities, forecast horizon, tree-reduction settings. **Judgment/procedure:** partition scenarios at branch points so disturbances, states, and controls share a history before branching; preserve the probability-weighted mass of the original ensemble. **Output:** reduced tree and adaptive multistage decision structure.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.3, p. 7; Section 4.3, pp. 14–16; Fig. 6. The mass-conservative algorithm is referenced to prior work; implementation details are not fully reproduced.

## RP07-E Correction and fallback when the forecast is wrong

Correct by error sign, adjust gradually, or fall back to measurement-based rules. Strength: Provisional. 3 steps, 2 papers.

### E37-R7: Classify forecast bias for response

**Description:** If a forecast is materially wrong, continuing the FBO release schedule can create an unsafe level trajectory. Error sign indicates the direction of corrective discharge.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** forecast error is detected during FBO. **Inputs:** current forecast versus corrected/actual indication, error sign, current level/discharge, remaining lead-time. **Judgment/procedure:** false-negative/underforecast calls for enlarged discharge; false-positive/overforecast requires the opposite directional correction. **Output:** emergency adjustment plan.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 3.5, pp. 11–12; Fig. 7. Quantitative trigger thresholds and the positive-error trajectory are not fully specified.

### E37-R8: Prefer gradual correction when a jump is unsafe

**Description:** A single instantaneous release change can itself be operationally problematic, so the paper presents a multi-step correction based on updated forecasts.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** significant forecast error with remaining forecast lead-time. **Inputs:** error category, corrected forecast, current release, CO/FBO mode, allowable change. **Judgment/procedure:** either change discharge at once and return to CO, or adjust gradually over multiple steps while maintaining FBO; multi-step is preferred when a jump is large. **Output:** corrected release trajectory.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 3.5, pp. 11–12; Fig. 7. The remedy is explicitly preliminary/conceptual and lacks a fixed model for all situations.

### E46-R2: Prefer robust measurement-based rules under forecast uncertainty

**Description:** Forecasts may fail during severe storms, so a rule based on measurements is proposed as a simpler, more reliable fallback or alternative. Perfect-forecast optimization is retained only as a reference.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** future inflow is uncertain or monitoring/forecast data degrade. **Inputs:** measured level/inflow/rainfall and current storage. **Judgment/procedure:** use current measurements and simple hydrologic relations, accept some loss of ideal peak attenuation for robustness. **Branches:** proposed rule operation versus pseudo-optimization with perfect future inflow. **Output:** implementable rule framework.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–3 and 11–14. This is a design rationale; it does not prove operational reliability outside the validation cases.

## RP07-F Seasonal staging and multi-year adjustment

Split the flood season into stages, or adjust bounds period by period under nonstationarity. Strength: Established (at threshold). 3 steps, 3 papers.

### E29-R4: Set dynamic bounds to reflect flood timing and lead time

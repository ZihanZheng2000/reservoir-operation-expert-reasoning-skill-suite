# Evidence for unit 3.1-risk-and-acceptable-safety

Paper steps assigned to each small pattern of this unit, with their full card text. Step IDs: Exx = paper, Rn = reasoning step, En = execution step.

## RP04-A Define reservoir-side failure events

Define unsafe as maximum level or required flood pool exceeding a safety threshold. Strength: Established. 16 steps, 13 papers.

### E02-R6: Convert stochastic objectives into operational risk indicators

**Description:** The authors treat reservoir overtopping and downstream flooding as threshold exceedances. This allows each Pareto alternative to be compared using safety-relevant probabilities instead of only expected objective values.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** An alternative’s expected objective values do not by themselves express exceedance risk. **Inputs:** distributions of (Z_{max}) and (Q_{max}), safe level (Z_s), and safe discharge (Q_s). **Judgment/procedure:** Compute (P_Z=P(Z_{max}>Z_s)) and (P_Q=P(Q_{max}>Q_s)). **Branches:** Reservoir and downstream risks use separate loading/resistance pairs. **Output:** Alternative-specific overtopping and downstream-flooding risk estimates.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.3, equations (14)–(15), pp. 8–9; case thresholds (Z_s=848.00) m and (Q_s=4{,}200) m³/s, p. 14. These are model-based exceedance proxies, not observed damage probabilities.

### E04-R7: Define the operational threshold and comparison route

**Description:** To assess whether uncertainty changes flood risk, the study links runoff generation, reservoir flood routing, and risk analysis. It uses TGR flood-control criteria and a 10,000-year design flood associated with the 180.4 m upper threshold, then compares forecast-informed operation against a reference using observed data and regular routing.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Synthetic forecast inflows are available for the flood-season risk experiment. **Inputs:** FCWL 145 m, Jingjiang/Shashi stage criterion 44.5 m, release constraints, design-flow threshold, and routing policy. **Judgment/procedure:** Evaluate maximum storage/water level exceedance probability under the pre-release versus regular-routing setup. **Branches:** Release caps apply at 53,900 m³/s for Jingjiang protection, 79,000 m³/s for specified higher reservoir levels, and release capacity determines release above 180.4 m. **Output:** A case-specific modeled flood-risk comparison.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.3 and 3.4, pp. 717–719 and 724–725; Eqs. (14)–(15), Table 2. Threshold and release rules are study settings; the paper does not claim an authorization procedure for live operators.

### E06-R1: Frame the uncertain operating risk

**Description:** Flood forecasts guide reservoir operation, but forecast error, hydrograph timing/shape, discharge capacity, storage–level relations, and wind effects make targets such as highest water level and peak outflow random. The paper therefore defines flood-control risk as the probability that a load indicator exceeds a safe resistance.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A flood-control decision must be assessed under uncertain future inflow and system response. **Inputs:** Candidate uncertainty sources and safe water-level or safe-release thresholds. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat highest water level or downstream peak discharge as load and the corresponding safe value as resistance. **Branches:** The general framework allows either water-level or discharge exceedance; the TGR case evaluates highest-water-level exceedance. **Output:** A multi-uncertainty risk-analysis problem.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 1–2 and Section 4.1, Eq. (10). The paper does not model social/economic flood consequences; “risk” is an operational exceedance indicator.

### E06-R6: Propagate uncertainty through the operating policy

**Description:** Once a starting water level is given, the paper routes each simulated hydrograph using the continuity equation, storage–elevation relation, sampled hydraulic/storage/wind terms, and the current TGR rule-based pre-release operation. This translates input uncertainty into operational outcomes.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Simulated inflow and uncertainty samples are available. **Inputs:** Q_i,t, rule-model set discharge q_s,t, λ_j, v_j, e_j, h_pj, initial level, time step, and flood duration. **Judgment/procedure:** Iterate the discrete routing equation over the flood duration; use real-time forecasts in a pre-release module to release stored water before a large flood. **Branches:** Apply the TGR operation-rule branch corresponding to water level and forecast inflow; exact row mapping is partly unreadable in the supplied table transcription. **Output:** One routed operation per simulated hydrograph, including highest water level and downstream discharge.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–7, Section 3, Eqs. (6)–(8), Fig. 3, Table 1. The paper states N repeated operations and LHS sample size N_s = T·N, but case-specific N, T, and time-step values are not reported clearly in the supplied text.

### E08-R6: Convert distributions into risk and assess uncertainty interactions

**Description:** Flood risk is the cumulative probability that reservoir level or downstream flow exceeds its safety threshold. Comparing cases reveals which uncertainty drives modeled risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Interpret inferred distributions for decision support. **Inputs:** BN distributions, Hs and QCs, uncertainty cases and evidence levels L1–L3. **Judgment/procedure:** Sum probabilities above thresholds; compare BN with MC and compare one-/two-factor cases, forecast horizons, and evidence levels. **Branches:** Reservoir risk versus downstream risk; predictive versus diagnostic analysis. **Output:** Risk curves, posterior ranges, and factor attribution.

**Evidence boundary:** Eqs. (13)–(14), pp. 5–10, Figs. 7–16. Conclusions are case-bounded; apparent compensation is a modeled interaction.

### E16-R2: Define a flood-pool objective with drawdown guardrail

**Description:** Required flood pool is the maximum accumulated storage above TOCS, but aggressive releases can draw into conservation storage. A small drawdown penalty prevents policies from releasing maximally without forecast justification.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Compare policies on flood-space requirement while retaining water supply. **Inputs:** Relative storage (S), observed inflow (Q_t), release (R_t), TOCS, horizon H, weight (w). **Judgment:** Penalize positive and negative deviations with (w\in\{0.0001,0.001,0.01,0.1,0.5\}), usually prioritizing exceedance. **Output:** Required flood pool and drawdown trade-off.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–4, Eqs. (1)–(2). The TOCS offset and (w) are study constructs; actual water-supply value is simplified.

### E17-R3: Define benefit as flood-pool reduction

**Description:** A dynamic release policy can reduce static flood pool while potentially improving conservation storage. Required pool is the maximum accumulated storage above TOCS after actual releases.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Compare candidate parameters/policies. **Inputs:** Actual inflow, simulated storage, TOCS/design flood pool. **Procedure:** Calculate (max(0,X_{t_1:t_n})), then percent reduction relative to actual flood pool; assign 0% when required pool exceeds design pool. **Output:** FP reduction metric.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 6–7, Eq. (7). This is a storage/factor-of-safety proxy and does not model flood damage or all water-supply requirements.

### E20-R4: Choose output descriptors that represent flood-operation consequences

**Description:** The authors reduce each event's output hydrograph and storage trajectory to maximum released flow and maximum stored volume. These descriptors are selected because they are directly linked to downstream damage and dam-safety risk, while acknowledging that temporal evolution and gradients could also matter.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Full output distributions are too complex for direct calibration. **Inputs:** Released-flow and stored-volume time series. **Judgment/procedure:** Apply a maximum operator to derive event-level characterization variables. **Branches:** Other descriptors are possible; this study uses two. **Output:** QOmax and Vmax distributions for each parameter set.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.1 and 2.2, pp. 3, 6; Eqs. (3), (8), and (9). The limitation of omitting hydrograph/level trajectories and gradients is explicit on p. 14.

### E27-R4: Propagate error through deterministic routing/rules

**Description:** A deterministic water-balance/routing model maps Qin, storage–level relation, and forecast-based operating rule to Qout and Z at each time step. Random error series are treated as realizations added to representative floods, making output uncertainty explicit.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** evaluate forecast-rule consequences. **Inputs:** representative flood wave, generated error series, initial level, W=f(Z), rule g(R,Z). **Judgment/procedure:** superimpose errors, iterate water balance and rule time step by time step. **Branches:** representative floods; return-period hydrograph magnification. **Output:** inflow, outflow, water-level series and MWL sample.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4560–4561, 4565–4567, Eq. (1), Fig. 1 and Figs. 3–6. Evaporation/leakage/losses are ignored; model and rule details are case-specific.

### E27-R5: Use Monte Carlo MWL distributions as operator risk evidence

**Description:** Repeated error realizations produce an empirical MWL distribution. Assuming approximate normal MWLs, moments can estimate probabilities of crossing 330.0 m, 333.0 m, or 337.33 m for selected return periods.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** quantify risk for a flood magnitude and threshold. **Inputs:** 2,000 error series per representative flood, routed MWL samples, threshold levels, return-period hydrograph. **Judgment/procedure:** estimate mean/SD and normal tail probabilities; inspect concentration/variation across flood sizes. **Output:** distribution plots and Table 4 exceedance probabilities.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4567–4571, Figs. 7–11 and Table 4. Normal MWL assumption and finite sample support are modeling choices; no observed MWL validation is reported.

### E28-R1: Frame the forecast-enabled operating trade-off

**Description:** Forecast information can identify a flood before its lead time and permit a higher flood-season level, but forecast uncertainty can make that extra stored water unsafe. Risk is framed as flood load exceeding the reservoir’s regulating resistance.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** considering forecast-based prerelease or a raised flood-limited level. **Inputs:** current storage, forecast lead time, expected inflow, flood-control capacity. **Judgment/procedure:** weigh water-use/storage benefit against level-exceedance risk. **Branches:** use forecast information when accuracy is adequate; otherwise conventional storage-based rules remain relevant. **Output:** need for probabilistic forecast-risk assessment.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 1–2. The text motivates the trade-off; it does not provide a formal benefit optimizer.

### E30-R1: Define the uncertainty-bearing decision problem

**Description:** Inflow forecast, discharge, and storage-curve errors make actual levels differ from deterministic routing, creating safety risk in real-time decisions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a forecast-based flood-control program is used. **Inputs:** forecast inflow, release relation, storage–level relation, observations, flood duration. **Judgment/procedure:** treat uncertain level as the load and a selected safe/controlled level as the threshold. **Branches:** point-in-time risk and total process risk are both needed. **Output:** probabilistic risk model requirement.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–2. The paper motivates the factors; the exact empirical identification of each case uncertainty is not fully reported.

### E32-R5: Select inference direction and risk event

**Description:** Decision makers may need either forecast-to-consequence prediction or consequence-to-cause diagnosis. Risk is probability mass above a selected safe level/flow.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** uncertain operation needs a risk estimate or a suspected event needs explanation. **Inputs:** evidence on inflows/lateral flows, levels, or control-point flows; trained DBN; safety thresholds. **Judgment/procedure:** prediction infers downstream levels/flows from causes; diagnosis infers possible causes from observed effects; sum probabilities of states above the threshold. **Output:** stochastic outcomes, overtopping/flooding risk, or posterior causes.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–5, Eqs. (7)–(13), Experiments 1–6. Thresholds are selected values; no damage/loss function is included.

### E36-R4: Optimize an expected safety-oriented trajectory

**Description:** The operation should account for each scenario while seeking a policy that minimizes expected highest water level.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** scenario tree has been generated. **Inputs:** scenario probabilities, level/release variables, inflow tree, initial and target levels, limits. **Judgment/procedure:** minimize the probability-weighted maximum level over scenarios, subject to balance, level/release/capacity/fluctuation constraints and initial/end conditions. **Output:** scenario-specific optimal operation and water-level maxima.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.2.1–2.2.2, pp. 6–7; Eqs. (10)–(16). The source’s formulation uses scenario-indexed releases; its practical conversion to a single implemented release is not detailed.

### E36-R6: Define risk as maximum-level exceedance

**Description:** Consequences are difficult to pre-evaluate, so the paper simplifies risk to event probability: highest level (Z_{max}) exceeds safe level (Z_s).

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** scenario operation yields a distribution of maximum levels. **Inputs:** (Z_{max}) outcomes, (Z_s). **Judgment/procedure:** use (Risk=P(Z_{max}>Z_s)=\int_{Z_s}^{\infty}f(Z_{max}),dZ_{max}). **Output:** overtopping probability for a selected safe level.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.3.2, p. 8, Eq. (17). This is an explicit probability-only risk definition; consequences are excluded.

### E46-R3: Set target and safety levels by objective priority

**Description:** IL, SL, NL, and DL encode preparation, safety, conservation, and PMF capacity. The authors treat targets as given by decision makers, while optimizing rules to approach them.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** define a stage objective and feasible envelope. **Inputs:** dam safety, flood mitigation, conservation needs, channel conveyance, PMF. **Judgment/procedure:** set target levels and release bounds; preserve space above SL as backup. **Output:** level targets and constraints for optimization.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–3, 6–8, and case data on p. 8. Targets are assumed inputs, not optimized or independently elicited in this paper.

## RP04-B Define downstream and engineering consequences

Levee failure, release above safe channel capacity, dam-break losses, shortage and lost generation. Strength: Established. 10 steps, 9 papers.

### E10-R2: Formulate dependent flood-risk objective

**Description:** Storage should be allocated to minimize total downstream levee-failure probability, accounting for survival through earlier stages.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Optimize flood-control storage allocation. **Inputs:** Forecast releases/inflows, intermediate flow, FCC, Gaussian error σ, FLWL/MWL/end storage. **Judgment/procedure:** Use R1+(1−R1)R2+(1−R1)(1−R2)R3; treat catastrophic damage as fixed, so probability substitutes for economic loss. **Output:** Convex three-stage allocation problem.

**Evidence boundary:** Eqs. (1)–(7), pp. 3–5. Fixed damage and Gaussian error are assumptions.

### E20-R5: Convert outputs into thresholded damage and conditional failure risk

**Description:** Release damage is zero below kq, derived from downstream hydraulic/channel and exposure characteristics. Storage damage is negligible below kv (FCL), while above it the damage equals dam-break cost multiplied by the conditional probability of reaching crest/failure volume. The probability is estimated by routing many very rare synthetic floods.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Characterization variables need a common consequence scale. **Inputs:** QOmax/Vmax distributions, downstream damage curve, FCL/MCC thresholds, dam crest level, dam-break cost, conditional failure probabilities. **Judgment/procedure:** Use thresholded release damage Dq and conditional storage risk Dv = p(break|Vmax) × Costbreak; estimate p(break|Vmax) from synthetic routing. **Branches:** If monetary damage curves are unavailable, use exceedance-risk functions and the Pareto approach instead. **Output:** Damage functions and two expected-risk indices.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.1.3–2.2, pp. 6–7; Eqs. (10)–(16), Fig. 4; thresholds and case values in §3, p. 8 and Table 2. Damage-curve construction and failure assumptions are case-dependent.

### E24-R3: Translate conveyance gap into a safety resource

**Description:** Levee failure occurs when uncertain total flow exceeds conveyance. The gap between expected flow and capacity is a stage EFSM; larger EFSM lowers failure probability and expected damage.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** compare expected flow with threshold. **Inputs:** qthres, expected total flows, σt, catastrophic damage C and duration T. **Judgment/procedure:** define δ1 = qthres − r̄1 and δ2 = Tqthres − r̄2; integrate the error density above each margin to obtain expected damage. **Branches:** margin positive avoids some failures; negative/low margin implies high risk. **Output:** D1(δ1), D2(δ2), total EFSM Δ.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 04014041-3–4, Eqs. (10)–(14), Fig. 2. Damage is catastrophic levee-failure cost in the model; real damage heterogeneity is not represented.

### E25-R2: Represent engineering failure modes and stage risk

**Description:** Failure may arise from overtopping or internal geotechnical mechanisms. A continuous nondecreasing F(Q) between safe base capacity qlb and overtopping capacity qub captures uncertain failure, while pre-storm and future failure events are assumed independent for tractability.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** translate flow into downstream risk. **Inputs:** Q, qlb/qub, levee condition, F(Q), damage cost cf. **Judgment/procedure:** use convex, linear, or concave fragility curves and compute failure probabilities for pre-storm and storm releases. **Branches:** good/convex, fair/linear, poor/concave conceptual curves; overtopping-only sensitivity. **Output:** Pf(Qh), Pf(Qsi), expected cost terms.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5114–5116, Fig. 1, Eqs. (1)–(5). Curves are conceptual/professional-judgment forms; empirical levee calibration is not supplied.

### E25-R5: Assess convexity and parameter effects

**Description:** A convex failure probability function can guarantee convex expected cost; a linear/concave curve depends more on storm distribution and antecedent flow. More convex failure curves generally support greater hedging; water-supply losses reduce it.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** determine if an interior/near-optimal hedging rule is credible. **Inputs:** F curvature θ, storm probability distribution, Qa, storage capacity, spill-loss function. **Judgment/procedure:** inspect cost curve and vary θ, Qa, and k. **Branches:** convex → optimum more assured; linear → distribution-dependent; concave → usually no hedging unless antecedent flow is below qlb. **Output:** qualitative direction and sensitivity.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5122–5126, Figs. 5–6, Table 1. These are numerical-example relationships, not universal empirical laws.

### E26-R3: Define consequence risks and acceptable flood bound

**Description:** Separate upstream flood damage from exceeding conservation upper bound, downstream flood inundation from excessive future release, and water-shortage damage from insufficient future release. Flood risk must stay below acceptable probability while the other risks are minimized.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** score the conservation decision. **Inputs:** Vu, Ru2, D2, damage functions ωu/ωd/ωs, acceptable probability γa. **Judgment/procedure:** define Pu=Prob(V1≥Vu), Pd=Prob(R2≥Ru2), Ps=Prob(R2≤D2); impose Pd≤γa and minimize Lu+Ls. **Branches:** chance constraint bounds R2; physical release bounds also constrain. **Output:** constrained total risk objective.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2737–2739, Eqs. (6)–(16). Damage functions are later represented through weights; downstream chance constraint and upstream/shortage objective are model constructs, not observed damages.

### E35-R6: Balance flood safety, water supply, and operability

**Description:** The operation needs a single objective combining soft targets, with high penalties for downstream release above 200 m³/s and end-of-event water-supply deviation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** select releases at each optimization. **Inputs:** forebay, spillway release, downstream threshold, variable guide curve, weights. **Judgment/procedure:** penalize low forebay relative to maximum, total spill, squared spill above threshold, high forebay above guide curve, and consecutive release changes; enforce water balance and hard physical limits. **Output:** optimized control trajectory.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 3.3–3.5, pp. 12–13, Eqs. (16)–(27). Weights are case-specific and selected by trial and error.

### E42-R5: Separate flood and curtailment pathways

**Description:** IFU overestimation may cause flood-related threshold exceedance; underestimation may cause water deficits and curtailed generation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** quantify consequences of forecast uncertainty. **Inputs:** ensemble inflows, design flood Qrp, reservoir operation rules, power-generation model. **Judgment/procedure:** count ensemble events above Qrp for flood risk; optimize power generation under each inflow input and compare against observations for curtailment. **Output:** coupled risk measures.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 8–9 and 16–20, Sections 2.4–2.5 and 4.3. Flood risk is a threshold rate; curtailment is a generation deviation, not a direct economic loss.

### E43-R4: Set the prediction and safety envelope

**Description:** A 42 m Taungoo safety level is used below the 43 m danger level to retain buffer capacity. A 30-minute simulation step, 3-hour control step, and two-day horizon are selected to capture the approximately 41-hour travel time from the furthest reservoir and available forecast length.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** controller requires a safety target and horizon. **Inputs:** danger level, travel time, forecast availability, computation time. **Judgment/procedure:** leave a buffer below the danger level and choose a horizon long enough for system delay but short enough to remain computable. **Branches:** longer horizons may increase computation without improving performance. **Output:** safety and timing settings.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 7–8 and 13, Sections 3 and 5. These are study settings; no systematic horizon calibration is reported.

### E50-R5: Optimize storage subject to flood-release penalties

**Description:** The objective maximizes normalized mean storage across reservoirs while subtracting 1.0 for each release exceeding the maximum safe release. Normalization prevents large reservoirs from dominating. Differential evolution is used because policy search is noisy/discontinuous and has a broad solution space.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** choose policy parameters for Forecast and Perfect cases. **Inputs:** time-series storage/release, capacities, safe-release threshold, parameter bounds. **Judgment/procedure:** run approximately 30,000 evaluations with 10 random seeds; maximize the aggregate objective and retain the best parameters. **Branches:** objective may have different storage outcomes despite a higher aggregate value because it is aggregate/normalized. **Output:** optimized policy parameters and simulations.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit p. 5. Exact parameter bounds, mutation/crossover settings, and convergence tolerances are not reported in the supplied text.

## RP04-C One exceedance probability is not enough; add other measures

Add loss, tail (CVaR), entropy, duration, or resilience. Strength: Established. 10 steps, 7 papers.

### E06-R7: Compare threshold risk and entropy-based risk

**Description:** Traditional frequency risk shows whether a simulated maximum exceeds a selected safe level; entropy is added because the authors consider a single probability insufficient to represent whole-system risk and uncertainty contributions. The additive formulation permits a system value across risk types and uncertainty sources.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Routed outcomes need to be evaluated at design or safety levels. **Inputs:** Highest water-level/peak-discharge outcomes, threshold values, and binned probabilities. **Judgment/procedure:** Calculate exceedance counts divided by N, then calculate Shannon entropy over subintervals and sum across uncertainty/risk components. Interpret HR = 0 as deterministic/no modeled risk and larger HR as more uncertainty/risk. **Branches:** Analyze each uncertainty separately and all four together; evaluate 100-year and 1000-year criteria as well as other safe levels. **Output:** Risk curves, exceedance tables, component entropies, and total system entropy.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–6, Sections 4.1–4.2, Eqs. (9)–(13); pp. 7–12, Tables 6–8 and Figs. 11–12. The entropy measure is a model-based uncertainty/risk indicator, not observed damage or independently validated failure probability.

### E12-R3: Include process resilience in the objective

**Description:** Flood damage depends on magnitude and duration, so minimizing only peak values is incomplete.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Optimize flood-regulating stage. **Inputs:** Downstream flow time series, Qmin/Qmax, reservoir levels. **Judgment/procedure:** Define piecewise system performance and integrate it over the event; maximize resilience while minimizing maximum level and downstream peak. **Output:** Three-objective optimization problem.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.3.1, pp. 4–5, Eqs. (6)–(10). Resilience is a modeled index, not observed damage.

### E14-R6: Match objective complexity to flood scale

**Description:** For small floods, level and release objectives may suffice and risk/resilience indices can be near zero. Large floods require objectives for failure probability, failure duration, and loss severity in both protection areas.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Select optimization formulation for an event. **Inputs:** Flood scale, safety thresholds, scenario set, upstream/downstream priorities. **Judgment:** Use TSO for expected maximum level/release comparison; add five risk/resilience/vulnerability objectives in MRO for large events. **Output:** Objective vector and constraints.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3, 8–10, 20–27, Eqs. (12)–(22). The scale rule is demonstrated with two events and is not validated across many return periods.

### E38-R2: Replace a single failure probability with complementary risk views

**Description:** Failure probability does not show the consequences of extreme outcomes, and variance alone is inadequate for asymmetric/discrete simulated results. Mean represents a typical value, CVaR represents the tail, and entropy represents distributional disorder/dispersion.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** stochastic operation results are random and discrete. **Inputs:** simulated maximum occupied-storage values. **Judgment/procedure:** retain mean, tail CVaR, and information entropy together. **Branches:** indicator-wise rankings may disagree, so no single indicator is used as the final decision. **Output:** three risk-index vectors per scheme.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 2–3, 5; Eqs. (4)–(6). The interpretation of entropy as risk is the authors’ modeling choice and is not independently validated here.

### E45-R1: Diagnose stationarity and consequence gap

**Description:** Changing climate and human activity can make flood probabilities time-varying. A threshold-exceedance probability alone does not express the size of flood damage when the threshold is exceeded.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** annual maximum streamflow is nonstationary or consequence magnitude matters. **Inputs:** time-varying exceedance probabilities, loss distribution, planning horizon. **Judgment/procedure:** separate flood hazard probability from damage-loss severity. **Branches:** stationary and nonstationary probability formulas. **Output:** need for a risk measure that carries tail magnitude.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–2, Introduction and Equations (1)–(2). This is methodological motivation, not proof of a particular climate mechanism in the case.

### E45-R2: Choose CVaR and derive a horizon measure

**Description:** CVaR is selected because it averages losses beyond VaR. Annual CVaR values are combined with the probability of an exceedance in each year to form CVaR over n years under nonstationary probabilities.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** compare candidate FLWLs over a planning horizon. **Inputs:** loss function, confidence level, annual probabilities, annual CVaR. **Judgment/procedure:** set alpha plus cumulative risk equal to one and use the probability-weighted formula in Equation (6). **Branches:** stationary equal-probability formula versus nonstationary time-varying formula; independent annual-event assumption. **Output:** CVaR_n alpha threshold and candidate loss measure.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–3, Equations (3)–(6). Independence and Bernoulli occurrence assumptions are explicit; loss continuity and calibration details are limited.

### E45-R5: Add tail-loss protection

**Description:** Because B1’s CVaR_n alpha exceeds the stationary benchmark despite satisfying probability risk, the authors add CVaR as a second constraint. This limits the boundary increase when the tail-loss consequence grows.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** risk probability passes but CVaR exceeds beta_n alpha. **Inputs:** B1 candidate, nonstationary CVaR, stationary beta_n alpha. **Judgment/procedure:** enforce both cumulative risk and CVaR_n alpha constraints in the same adaptive optimization. **Branches:** if B1 CVaR is already below the benchmark, B1 remains applicable; otherwise FLWL is reduced. **Output:** B2 schedule.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 6–8, Equation (9), Figure 6 and Table 2. The conditional rule is explicit; practical loss valuation remains dependent on a constant unit cost.

### E45-R6: Interpret the stricter metric and claim scope

**Description:** The comparison with unconditional expected value suggests CVaR is more sensitive to FLWL changes at high confidence. The authors conclude that CVaR gives a more reliable constraint for this nonstationary adaptive-FLWL problem.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** choose a risk metric for adaptive operation. **Inputs:** B1/B2 expected loss, CVaR, generation, risk trajectories. **Judgment/procedure:** compare metrics and inspect whether the loss threshold is exceeded. **Output:** preference for CVaR-constrained adaptation in this case.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 7–8, Figure 7 and Conclusions. No observed damage validation or deployment study establishes universal superiority.

### E49-R3: Represent flood risk with complementary probability, loss, and resilience surrogates

**Description:** Risk probability alone can miss severity, while vulnerability/loss alone can allow frequent events; duration captures restoration/resilience. CVaR is chosen over VaR because it summarizes expected loss in the adverse tail.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** risk control must cover frequency, severity, and duration. **Inputs:** simulated water levels/releases, safety thresholds, loss-identification thresholds, scenario probabilities. **Judgment/procedure:** define threshold exceedance probabilities, loss ratios, CVaR at confidence level α, and maximum sequential exceedance duration. **Branches:** upstream uses water-level/storage loss; downstream uses release loss; event-scale emphasis changes. **Output:** six objective functions.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 2, 5–6, Eqs. (2)–(11), Fig. 2–3. Loss measures are surrogates; no direct depth-damage calculation is performed.

### E50-R6: Treat storage and release frequency as joint viability checks

**Description:** Higher storage alone can hide flood risk; frequent maximum-safe releases are undesirable even when they do not exceed damaging downstream thresholds. Therefore the paper compares both normalized storage and frequency of maximum releases, plus trajectories.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** evaluate policy performance and operational acceptability. **Inputs:** mean storage, May 1 storage, maximum-release occurrence, event trajectories. **Judgment/procedure:** inspect whether storage gains coincide with safe release behavior and spread releases over time. **Branches:** Baseline-TOCS may raise storage but force frequent releases; Forecast may spread releases. **Output:** storage/flood-control performance comparison.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 5–7, Figs. 4–5. Maximum-release frequency is a proxy for flood-control burden; the study does not model all downstream impacts.

## RP04-D Set an acceptable-risk benchmark

Use current-rule risk, design or protection standards, or a stationary benchmark as the pass line. Strength: Established. 11 steps, 11 papers.

### E01-R5: Require total modeled risk to be no greater than the current-rule risk

**Description:** The authors define risks (R_1)–(R_4) for the possible decision errors and hydrograph outcomes. Dynamic control is acceptable only if their sum does not exceed (R_0), the risk associated with the specified design flood regulated from the current FLWL.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A candidate upper level and decision policy must be screened for safety. **Inputs:** (R(E_1)), (R(E_2)), (R(Z,P)), decision alternatives, and baseline risk (R_0). **Judgment/procedure:** Compute the risk terms in Eqs. (8)–(12) and enforce (R_1+R_2+R_3+R_4\le R_0). **Branches:** One alternative is assigned zero risk under the paper’s formulation; other alternatives combine forecast-classification and hydrograph risks. **Output:** An accept/reject condition for the dynamic-control bound.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.4.1–2.4.2, pp. 126–127, Eqs. (6)–(15). This is a modeled risk constraint, not an observed downstream-loss guarantee.

### E07-R3: Set the critical threshold and lead-time tolerance

**Description:** The authors set the EFO storage threshold at 111,000 acre-feet, the maximum conservation storage under existing operations and 3 ft below the emergency-spillway crest, to maintain a safety margin. They compare forecasted exceedance risk with a tolerance curve: tolerance is 0% for the first five forecast days and increases afterward because near-term forecasts are treated as more reliable and later forecasts leave more response time.

## RP04-E Derive operating bounds from acceptable risk

Convert the risk limit into a safety margin, level ceiling, or the inflow quantile that must be accommodated. Strength: Established. 13 steps, 11 papers.

### E03-R4: Translate forecast uncertainty and acceptable risk into a safety requirement

**Description:** A future release can exceed the downstream conveyance threshold because forecast inflow differs from actual inflow. The authors therefore define flood risk as the probability that Stage-2 release exceeds (R_{2,​max}), and use the acceptable risk (r_a) to obtain a minimum safety margin (d_{​min}). A minimum Stage-2 release/probability requirement also gives an upper margin bound.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A prefill decision must be made with uncertain Stage-2 inflow. **Inputs:** Forecast-error distribution, (R_{2,​max}), (R_{2,​min}), acceptable risk (r_a), guaranteed release rate (P_e). **Judgment/procedure:** Evaluate the chance constraints and invert the forecast-error CDF to obtain (d_{​min}=H^{-1}(1-r_a)) (and (d_{​max}) from the minimum-release condition; under Gaussian error, (d_{​min}=rU^{-1}(1-r_a))). **Branches:** Larger uncertainty or lower acceptable risk increases (d_{​min}), narrowing the DC-FLWL feasible space; if forecast inflow is too large, use primary flood-control regulation. **Output:** Risk-feasible release and safety-margin bounds.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–9, Section 2.3 and equations 10–27; pp. 10–12, equations 34–35. The downstream release-equals-reservoir-release relation assumes short distance and negligible intermediate inflow/diversion.

### E05-R3: Define feasible storage–safety allocations before optimizing

**Description:** The stage-2 mass balance gives (W_1+d=RFCC), so residual flood-conveyance capacity is allocated between conservation storage and safety margin. The feasible region must also satisfy downstream flow, storage, and acceptable-risk requirements.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A candidate allocation must be physically and risk feasible. **Inputs:** (R_{2,max}), expected inflow ̄(I_2), (R_{2,min}), (W_{max}), and (d_{min}=rU^{-1}(1-r_a)). **Judgment/procedure:** Define point A by (d=d_{min}), D by (d=d_{max}=R_{2,max}-R_{2,min}), and bounds (0le W_1lemin(W_{max},RFCC-d_{min})). Determine the DC-FLWL inflow interval from (RFCC_A) to (RFCC_D). **Branches:** Large inflow makes flood control dominant; small inflow makes water supply dominant; increasing (r) shrinks the feasible zone, while increasing (r_a) expands it. **Output:** Feasible zone ABCD and candidate start/end segments for hedging curves.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2–3.1, pp. 4–6; Figures 3–4; equations (3), (4), (7). The normal-error formula and DC-FLWL interpretation are model assumptions.

### E11-R2: Convert forecast skill into an acceptable error bound

**Description:** A design flood probability must remain unchanged under uncertain forecast.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Set dynamic design-period flood. **Inputs:** Forecast skill Cp, variance of observed period volume, desired design exceedance Pdes. **Judgment/procedure:** Use σ²ε=(1−Cp)var(Vts), then choose δP,ts from the forecast-error quantile so Prob(εts≥δP,ts)=Pdes. **Output:** Error allowance and dynamic design volume.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.2, pp. 5–6, Eqs. (1)–(5). Normal error is assumed for illustration.

### E15-R3: Translate forecast uncertainty into a safety bound

**Description:** The forecast-stage release distribution follows from mass balance and the requirement that end storage equals the flood-limit storage. The acceptable risk rate (r_a) determines the percentile of forecast inflow that the current decision must accommodate.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Set current decision release with uncertain (I_f). **Inputs:** Current (V_t,I_t), forecast density (P_I), (T_f), (R_{lim}), (V_{lim}), (r_a). **Procedure:** Derive (V_{t+1}\le V_{lim}+R_{lim}T_f\Delta t-operatorname{Per}(P_I,1-r_a)T_f\Delta t) and a corresponding interval for (R_t). **Output:** Risk-aware storage/release bounds.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–7, Eqs. (3)–(16). Direction of (r_a) is explicit: smaller (r_a) lowers storage and raises discharge; variance in (P_I) increases its influence.

### E19-R3: Define PP as a conservative physical upper bound

**Description:** Starting with the atmospheric water balance, the authors assume all water vapor entering the basin converts to precipitation, with terminal PWV and outward vapor flux set to zero. This yields PP as initial PWV plus inward vapor flux (with evapotranspiration optionally included), a worst-case amount rather than an exact precipitation forecast.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A flood-control decision needs an estimate beyond the direct precipitation horizon. **Inputs:** Initial PWV, inward integrated vapor flux, evapotranspiration, atmospheric-water-balance terms. **Judgment/procedure:** Select conservative boundary assumptions and compute PP using Eqs. (1)–(6); neglect evapotranspiration in the multi-period approximation because it is typically minor. **Branches:** Include water-vapor, cloud-liquid, and cloud-frozen flux components; use multi-period accumulation when needed. **Output:** PP for each future interval/horizon.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.1.2, pp. 3–4, Eqs. (1)–(6). The all-conversion assumption is a safety-oriented modeling assumption; it is not observed precipitation and its conservatism depends on the specified system boundary.

### E19-R4: Convert PP into an operational water-level bound

**Description:** PP is physically interpretable but not sufficiently intuitive for operation because it lacks a fixed reservoir threshold. The authors therefore transform PP into potential floodwater volume and then into VCWL, the theoretical upper water-level limit compatible with a specified prerelease sequence.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** PP is available but operators need a level target/bound. **Inputs:** PP, runoff transformation, maximum water level, current inflow/outflow, prerelease sequence, forecast periods. **Judgment/procedure:** Use Eq. **Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.2.2, pp. 5 and Appendix A, p. 12, Eqs. (8)–(9). Exact dimensions/notation in Eq. (8) are partly damaged by OCR; implementation parameters beyond the reported coefficient/model are not fully given.

### E28-R6: Interpret accuracy sensitivity and reverse-use the risk curve

**Description:** If forecast accuracy is lower, routed-level dispersion and risk increase. The risk curve can therefore be read forward (risk for an accuracy) or backward (accuracy needed for an accepted risk).

## RP04-F Several safety objects or thresholds at once

Dam versus downstream, multiple reservoirs, or several thresholds interact and need an explicit combination rule. Strength: Established. 10 steps, 7 papers.

### E02-R3: Define the competing operating objectives

**Description:** The operating problem contains a structural conflict: lowering reservoir level protects the dam and upstream area but generally requires more release, whereas reducing release protects the downstream control point but stores more water. The authors retain both objectives instead of collapsing them into an assumed single weighted objective.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Candidate releases must balance two safety concerns. **Inputs:** scenario inflows, reservoir levels, releases, and scenario probabilities. **Judgment/procedure:** Minimize expected highest reservoir water level and expected peak reservoir discharge over the forecast scenarios. **Branches:** No single compromise is selected before the Pareto set is generated. **Output:** Two stochastic objective values for each candidate policy.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2 and equations (6)–(7), pp. 5–6; Introduction, pp. 1–2. Hydropower and water supply are mentioned as general competing goals, but the implemented optimization uses the two stated flood-control objectives.

### E20-R1: Frame flood operation as a conflict between two consequences

**Description:** Flood operation must limit downstream damaging discharges while preventing reservoir levels that endanger the dam. For small floods dam safety may be less salient, but extreme loads make dam safety a priority; the authors therefore treat the two concerns as a multiobjective calibration problem.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A flood-control policy must balance release and storage consequences. **Inputs:** Downstream channel/land-use exposure, dam/reservoir safety characteristics, inflow magnitude, operating policy. **Judgment/procedure:** Represent released-flow damage and stored-volume/dam-failure risk as separate objectives. **Branches:** Under extreme floods, dam safety may take precedence; exact priority is not fixed universally. **Output:** Two risk objectives for calibration.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§1–2, pp. 1–3. The priority discussion is qualitative; no universal risk-aversion rule is established.

### E21-R5: Let surplus upstream capacity compensate downstream tasks

**Description:** If upstream reservoirs have surplus capacity for the current flood type, they can hold part of the flood, allowing downstream reservoirs such as TGR to remain at a higher level and avoid unnecessary spillage while the entire basin's flood risk remains controlled.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Some reservoirs have unused reserved capacity and the downstream system needs flood-control space. **Inputs:** Flood classification, reservoir capacities/levels, forecast timing, downstream flood-control requirement. **Judgment/procedure:** Transfer part of the flood-control burden upstream by impounding there; accept a higher downstream level when system-level risk is still controlled. **Branches:** Compensation depends on relatively accurate forecasts and the joint-operation pattern; if flood types coincide, surplus may not exist. **Output:** Basin-level capacity compensation and benefit-preserving level trajectory.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 6–7, including TGR 153.03 m versus FLWL 145 m example. The article does not quantify a general admissible compensation boundary.

### E38-R1: Define the decision object and risk variable

**Description:** Joint operation must use the flood-control capacity of a reservoir group, but uncertainty can cause safety water-level or discharge exceedance. The paper therefore evaluates alternative schemes through maximum occupied storage capacity for each participating reservoir and the overall system.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a multi-reservoir scheme must be compared under uncertainty. **Inputs:** reservoir network, flood hydrographs, operation rules, safety-related storage/water-level quantities. **Judgment/procedure:** use maximum occupied storage as the common risk variable. **Branches:** subsystem and overall risk are both retained. **Output:** scheme-specific simulated risk distributions.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 2–4, Section 2 and Fig. 1. The paper also discusses water level/discharge risks, but the reported indicator tables use maximum occupied capacity.

### E38-R4: Resolve reservoir-level importance before system aggregation

**Description:** Risks at individual reservoirs should reflect different flood-control capacity and average annual runoff. AHP allows these attributes to generate reservoir weights, while a consistency ratio checks the judgment matrices.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** aggregate 14 reservoir indicators into a basin-level index. **Inputs:** flood-control capacity, average annual runoff, pairwise judgments. **Judgment/procedure:** construct hierarchical model, compare reservoirs on a 1–9 scale, calculate weights, and accept matrices when CR ≤ 0.1. **Output:** reservoir weights used for weighted mean, CVaR, and entropy.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 5–6 and 12–13, Eqs. (7)–(15), Fig. 5. Reported CRs are 0.049 and 0.054; the authors set the capacity/runoff attribute weights to {0.5, 0.5}.

### E39-R2: Distinguish single, union, and intersection hazards

**Description:** One threshold concerns maximum reservoir level and another downstream maximum streamflow. Their interaction can trade off: reducing release may lower downstream discharge while raising reservoir level.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** multiple safety thresholds may be crossed. **Inputs:** Zmax, Zmax^0, Qmax, Qmax^0. **Judgment/procedure:** define H1: Zmax > Zmax^0, H2: Qmax > Qmax^0, H3 = H1 or H2, H4 = H1 and H2. **Output:** hazard type whose probability is calculated.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–4, Eqs. (2)–(5). H3 is UHE and H4 is IHE; consequences are not modeled, only probabilities.

### E39-R3: Prefer continuous integration to conditional interval approximation

**Description:** MLTP partitions error and approximates conditional probability within each interval, while the authors argue that integrating the exact joint density over a domain is more precise and naturally handles UHE/IHE.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** risk probability must retain source dependence and complex hazard geometry. **Inputs:** fE,Y(e,y), hazard domain Ω. **Judgment/procedure:** calculate P(H) = ∬Ω fE,Y(e,y) de dy. **Branches:** MLTP remains a comparison method for single hazards. **Output:** analytical/numerical AMIT risk probability.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4, 7, 15–16, Eq. (6), Appendix 3. The “analytical” framework still relies on gridded routing/interpolation to derive boundaries in the case study.

### E39-R4: Build the hazard domain from physical boundary conditions

**Description:** A hazard boundary is the set of source combinations for which the routed maximum exactly equals the critical water level or discharge. The domain above the boundary is hazardous.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Ω is not known algebraically. **Inputs:** design flood frequencies/volumes, error range, runoff model, forecast-informed operation rules. **Judgment/procedure:** construct S1(e,y,Zmax) or S1(e,y,Qmax), intersect with the threshold plane, project to e–y, and classify above/below the curve. **Output:** Ω1 and Ω2; union/intersection domains Ω3 and Ω4.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–7, Figs. 1–2 and 7. The union is the union of single domains and the intersection is their overlap; exact curve geometry is case-specific.

### E40-R3: Set risk events and control mode

**Description:** Release above Qc threatens downstream safety; water level above Zc threatens upstream/dam safety. Release-control and water-level-control models expose different risk sides.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** evaluate an operating policy. **Inputs:** release/water-level trajectories, Qc, Zc. **Judgment/procedure:** define a scenario as failed if any time step crosses the relevant threshold; choose fixed release or fixed level as the control formulation. **Output:** upstream or downstream risk statistic.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–3, Eqs. (1)–(5), Section 3.3. The paper considers probability only, not consequence severity.

### E48-R2: Define the safety hierarchy and measurable criteria

**Description:** The authors distinguish the overall flood-control objective, reservoir flood-control safety, and downstream protected-region safety. For every reservoir they use highest water level, terminal water level, and used flood-control-capacity ratio; for each downstream protected region they use peak discharge, duration above safety discharge, and excess spill volume. All are treated as quantitative cost criteria.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a ranking model needs comparable outcome variables. **Inputs:** reservoir states and downstream routing outputs. **Judgment/procedure:** choose indicators that represent own-reservoir safety, subsequent storage capacity, and downstream damage exposure. **Branches:** criteria are repeated per reservoir/protected region. **Output:** hierarchical criterion system and decision matrix.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 3–4, Table 1, Fig. 1. The supplied text does not report a stakeholder elicitation or empirical validation of criterion completeness.

## RP04-G Aggregate risk over time

Distinguish point and whole-process risk, within and beyond the lead time, and weight by flood occurrence probability. Strength: Established. 9 steps, 8 papers.

### E28-R4: Define the risk event at the correct safety reference

**Description:** A flood-control risk must distinguish ordinary design operation from the extreme check condition. The authors factor risk into the probability of the selected extreme flood and the conditional probability that the routed stochastic level exceeds its corresponding critical level.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** evaluating a design or check hydrograph. **Inputs:** extreme-flood occurrence probability, critical storage/level, simulated level paths, flood duration. **Judgment/procedure:** calculate `R = P(flood event) × P(max Z > Zc | flood event)`. **Branches:** design uses DFWL and 0.10%; check uses CFWL and 0.02%. **Output:** RDF or RCF.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–5, Eqs. (14)–(19), definitions of design/check flood. The factorization is the paper’s risk definition; it does not establish damage consequences.

### E30-R5: Define point and whole-process risk

**Description:** Exceedance can occur at any time, and adjacent events overlap. A point risk alone can understate the chance of any exceedance, so the authors use a union probability with adjacent bivariate dependence and independence for other moments.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a controlled water level `HR` is selected. **Inputs:** mean level, error PDF, adjacent covariance, flood duration. **Judgment/procedure:** calculate `P[Ĥ(t)>HR]` and `P(∪At)` using univariate and adjacent bivariate-normal integrals plus inclusion–exclusion simplification. **Output:** point risk and integrated flood-process risk.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–5, Eqs. (18)–(29). The independence of nonadjacent moments is an explicit simplifying assumption, not an empirical finding.

### E31-R2: Define risks at point and process levels

**Description:** A highest-level risk can miss exceedance at another time, while summing point risks double-counts correlated events. The authors define point exceedance and total union risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a safe water level `ZR` is selected. **Inputs:** stochastic level process, flood duration. **Judgment/procedure:** use `P[Z(t)>ZR]` for each moment and `P(∪Yt)` for the complete flood; retain adjacent dependence in the union calculation. **Output:** point risks and total risk `PR`.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–5, Eqs. (8)–(9), (18)–(22). The treatment of nonadjacent dependence is a simplifying assumption.

### E33-R2: Split the horizon at each forecast boundary

**Description:** Risk methods limited to the forecast horizon cannot assess the remainder of the operation horizon. The authors consequently distinguish stage 1 (forecast lead-time) from stage 2 (beyond-forecast time).

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** forecast information ends before the operation horizon. **Inputs:** lead-time endpoint and scenario forecasts. **Judgment/procedure:** use forecast scenarios where information exists and design-flood routing afterward. **Branches:** stage-1 failures are counted across scenarios; stage-2 failures are computed through routing. **Output:** two risk components that can be integrated.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in Section 2.1, p. 2; Fig. 1; Eqs. (2)–(4). The causal sequence is a reconstruction from the stated method.

### E33-R4: Define failure probability over reservoir/scenario states

**Description:** A failure is any release or water level above its critical threshold in a scenario; the joint multi-reservoir risk combines stage-1 scenario failures and stage-2 routed failures.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** candidate storage/release trajectories must be assessed. **Inputs:** scenario trajectories, critical release/level thresholds, stage-1 end levels, design hydrographs. **Judgment/procedure:** count a scenario once when at least one exceedance occurs; evaluate stage-2 risk conditional on combinations of end water levels; combine stage risks into entire risk. **Branches:** threshold can be maximum release or maximum water level. **Output:** (R_{S1}), (R_{S2}), and (R_{TS}).

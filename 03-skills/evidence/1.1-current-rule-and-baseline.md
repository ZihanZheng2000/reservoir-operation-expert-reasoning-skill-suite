## RP01-A Fixed flood-control allocation causes a safety-benefit imbalance

A fixed flood-limited level, guide curve, or flood pool wastes water in small floods or under-protects in large floods. Strength: Established. 10 steps, 10 papers.

### E01-R1: Reframe fixed FLWL as a flood-control/conservation trade-off

**Description:** A fixed FLWL is intended to retain adequate flood storage, but applying it throughout the flood season can give too much priority to low-probability floods and reduce floodwater utilization. Forecast information makes a conditional, dynamic bound a plausible adjustment.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A fixed flood-season level is being used despite changing forecast and hydrologic conditions. **Inputs:** Current FLWL, flood-season operation objectives, forecast availability, and conservation/flood-control trade-off. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat the FLWL as an operating interval rather than an invariant level, subject to a safety-preserving upper bound. **Branches:** None stated at this conceptual step. **Output:** Need for a dynamic-control bound and forecast-informed decisions.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicitly stated in the abstract and Introduction, pp. 124–125. The claim is a design rationale, not evidence that every reservoir should use a dynamic FLWL.

### E03-R1: Reframe static FLWL as a forecast-responsive trade-off

**Description:** Static FLWL is designed from annual design storms or floods and ignores annual/seasonal inflow variation and real-time meteorological and hydrological information. This can produce unnecessary flood-season spills, post-season water shortage, or exposure when the actual flood season is delayed. Therefore the paper treats the active flood-limited level as a dynamic decision within lower and upper bounds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A fixed or static multistage FLWL is unable to use current forecasts or seasonal variation. **Inputs:** Flood-season inflow variation, existing FLWL practice, water-supply and flood-control purposes. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare the benefits of additional conservation with the consequence of losing flood-control storage. **Branches:** Use dynamic adjustment only within specified FLWL bounds; the baseline remains the primary/static flood-control regulation beyond the forecast horizon. **Output:** A need for DC-FLWL and a bounded dynamic storage decision.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit motivation in pp. 1–3, Introduction. The paper establishes the trade-off and proposed route; it does not claim that all reservoirs can use identical bounds or triggers.

### E05-R1: Reframe fixed flood-control storage as an adaptive trade-off

**Description:** Fixed flood-control space can be unused when no large flood occurs and can be insufficient for a large flood. The paper consequently asks how much storage may be conserved now while retaining later flood-control protection under forecast uncertainty.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A reservoir serves both flood control and water conservation and fixed allocation is potentially inefficient. **Inputs:** competing purposes, forecast information, expected future inflow, downstream flood-conveyance capacity, and water-use requirements. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat current conservation and future flood control as a hedging problem rather than a fixed-storage rule. **Branches:** If uncertainty or preference is outside a feasible range, one objective dominates and the problem reduces to a single objective. **Output:** A two-stage decision problem with explicit trade-off variables.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit motivation and questions in Sections 1–2, pp. 1–2. The adaptive framing is a research/modeling rationale; no real-time authorization protocol is reported.

### E07-R1: Diagnose the guide-curve trade-off

**Description:** The authors reason that static guide curves reserve flood space from climatological averages and can be overly conservative for flood protection, reducing water-supply storage. This is especially problematic when annual precipitation timing, climate, watershed conditions, and demands depart from historical averages; the 2012–2013 example illustrates water released for nominal flood space before a historically dry period.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A multipurpose reservoir must satisfy flood protection and supply/ecosystem needs under variable inflow timing. **Inputs:** Seasonal guide curve, storage conditions, historical climate/hydrology, operational objectives. **Judgment/procedure:** Identify where fixed seasonal storage targets force releases despite later dry conditions, while preserving the need for flood space. **Branches:** None stated as an algorithmic branch. **Output:** Requirement for an adaptive release decision that can retain water yet preserve flood capacity.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit motivation in pp. 1–6, Sections 1–2. The 2013 example is illustrative; the paper does not quantify a counterfactual EFO decision for that historical operation.

### E19-R1: Diagnose the fixed-FLWL trade-off

**Description:** A static FLWL reserves capacity for a potential large flood, but in flood seasons it can cause avoidable spills and later water shortages. Forecast-informed temporary level increases could improve floodwater utilization while retaining the ability to draw down before a flood.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A fixed seasonal flood-control level limits water-use benefits. **Inputs:** FLWL, flood-control requirement, forecast availability, supply/power objectives. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat the fixed level as a baseline constraint that may be adjusted conditionally when forecast evidence supports it. **Branches:** Raise/retain level during low-risk conditions; draw down when a flood is indicated. **Output:** Need for dynamic forecast-informed control.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §1.1, pp. 1–2. The paper does not redefine the legal safety standard or provide a general risk tolerance.

### E29-R1: Frame the static-FLWL limitation as a benefit–safety trade-off

**Description:** A fixed FLWL protects flood storage but can leave the reservoir underused if the flood season timing differs from expectation. Forecast-based dynamic control may store more water without exceeding the design risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a fixed flood-season level is underusing water. **Inputs:** flood-season timing, forecast horizon, available storage, safety and water-use objectives. **Judgment/procedure:** compare static protection with dynamic upper/lower bounds and prerelease/refill. **Branches:** dynamic control is intended for medium/small inflows below the downstream-risk threshold. **Output:** a forecast-enabled control route.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–2 and 4. The benefit argument is conceptual; it is not an optimization proof.

### E34-R1: Frame conservation as a coupled risk trade-off

**Description:** Keeping every reservoir below the designed FLWL preserves flood storage but causes frequent spillage and worsens water scarcity. Raising/retaining storage can conserve water, but forecast underestimation can threaten reservoirs and downstream regions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a receding flood and water-supply shortage risk coexist. **Inputs:** water demand, current storage, forecast inflow, flood-safety limits. **Judgment/procedure:** treat conservation benefit and flood risks as conflicting objectives. **Branches:** the paper distinguishes deterministic screening/soft-risk approaches from direct stochastic optimization. **Output:** a multi-risk decision problem.

**Evidence boundary:** Introduction, pp. 1–2. “Risk” is operationalized later as surrogate loss ratios, not observed damages.

### E41-R1: Make FLWL stage-specific

**Description:** A whole-season fixed FLWL either reserves too much capacity and sacrifices benefits or is too high for flood prevention. Seasonal flood magnitude and timing differ.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** balance flood safety and conservation over a season. **Inputs:** seasonal rainfall, peaks, runoff, flood control standard. **Judgment/procedure:** divide the season and allow dynamic bounds with original FLWL as lower limit. **Output:** stage-specific upper-bound problem.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–2 and 6–8, Sections 1 and 4.2.1; Table 2.

### E44-R1: Frame dynamic boundary need

**Description:** Static FLWL operation preserves flood-control storage but may spill water and create shortages later. Dynamic WLFS can trade flood safety against water conservation using hydrologic information.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** flood-season water level is fixed at FLWL. **Inputs:** flood risk requirements, water conservation and power objectives, forecasts. **Judgment/procedure:** permit controlled departures from FLWL only inside explicit risk and refill limits. **Branches:** upper and lower boundaries define the allowed dynamic range. **Output:** dynamic-boundary design problem.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–2, Introduction. The motivation is explicit; no general performance claim follows from it alone.

### E50-R1: Identify the fixed-rule conflict and forecast opportunity

**Description:** Seasonal rule curves preserve flood space based on historical timing/magnitude, but release wet-season inflows and reduce dry-season storage. Short-to-medium forecasts can make releases dynamically responsive to current and expected inflows.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** fixed seasonal flood pool causes avoidable opportunity cost under changing hydrology. **Inputs:** current storage/inflow, forecast trajectory, historical policy. **Judgment/procedure:** use forecast information to time releases instead of relying only on average historical patterns. **Branches:** forecast quality and reservoir constraints determine benefit. **Output:** need for formal FIRO policy.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 1–3. Climate and atmospheric-river motivation is contextual; the experiment itself uses historical 2013–2023 forecasts, not future climate simulations.

## RP01-B The rule cannot use available forecasts or their uncertainty

Forecasts exist, but the rule uses only the current state, treats forecasts as certain, or relies on a horizon that is too short. Strength: Established. 4 steps, 4 papers.

### E15-R1: Identify the fixed-rule limitation

**Description:** Conventional operation rules respond mainly to current state and do not exploit future forecasts or forecast uncertainty. The authors therefore seek a rule that can use water from smaller events and draw down before larger events.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Real-time flood-season operation with future inflow information. **Inputs:** Current inflow/storage, forecast horizon, conventional-rule behavior. **Judgment:** Make forecast and uncertainty part of the rule rather than only a post hoc evaluation. **Output:** Forecast-informed two-stage HPFOR route.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–3. The motivation is explicit; no claim is made that all conventional rules have identical limitations.

### E19-R2: Identify the forecast-horizon gap in PS-FIRO

**Description:** Precipitation and streamflow forecasts can trigger prerelease, but their horizon limits how early the reservoir can prepare. Vapor conditions are expected to rise before significant precipitation and can provide information beyond the precipitation/streamflow horizon.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Existing forecast horizon is too short for efficient retention/prerelease decisions. **Inputs:** Precipitation, streamflow, PWV, vapor convergence, lead times. **Judgment/procedure:** Use the earliest physically linked indicator for early warning and preserve later forecasts for operational calculations. **Branches:** Vapor is an early indicator; precipitation and streamflow take over as their forecasts become available. **Output:** Rationale for a vapor–precipitation–streamflow sequence.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§1.2–1.4 and §3.2.2, pp. 1–5. The article reports a 72-hour vapor and 48-hour precipitation/streamflow setup for this case, not a universal lead-time rule.

### E23-R1: Frame the operating decision under uncertainty

**Description:** Conventional offline rules do not directly use current forecasts. Online MPC can anticipate inflows, but deterministic forecast use can produce fragile pre-release decisions when the expected event fails to occur. Therefore the decision representation must account for forecast uncertainty.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** short-term flood-control decision with forecasted inflows. **Inputs:** current system state, forecast disturbances, constraints, objective. **Judgment/procedure:** compare proactive benefit against deterministic forecast-robustness risk. **Branches:** deterministic single trajectory versus ensemble/scenario formulation. **Output:** requirement for uncertainty-aware MPC.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicitly reasoned in pp. 1636–1637, Section 1. The statement that meteorological uncertainty is the largest component is a paper hypothesis/working assumption; hydrological model uncertainty is incompletely sampled.

### E26-R1: Frame conservation as a flood/shortage trade-off

**Description:** After a flood recedes, spare capacity could conserve water for dry periods, but the amount must remain compatible with flood-control reliability and future demand. Capacity-constrained pre-release rules do not explicitly model forecast-error risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** pre-refill/pre-release operation with surplus inflow and later shortage. **Inputs:** initial storage, inflow/demand forecasts, flood storage/conservation capacity, safe releases, forecast precision. **Judgment/procedure:** treat conservation as a risky decision balancing current/future phases. **Branches:** FRs prioritize flood control; CRs use capacity; HRs minimize weighted risk. **Output:** need for stochastic analytical HR.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2731–2736, Introduction and Fig. 1. The two-phase abstraction and relative storage below FLWL are deliberate simplifications.

## RP01-C The rule boundary omits system objects

The rule covers only one reservoir and ignores cascade coupling or downstream control points. Strength: Provisional. 2 steps, 2 papers.

### E21-R1: Frame the cascade as a coupled flood-control system

**Description:** Flood peaks from multiple tributaries may encounter in the mainstream before TGR, and upstream reservoirs can reduce pressure on a single downstream reservoir. Therefore, the operation target is the whole basin/cascade rather than independent local rule following.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Severe, spatially distributed flood affects a reservoir group. **Inputs:** Tributary/mainstream flood composition, reservoir locations, travel times, local and downstream control points. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat impounding/release actions as coupled through routing and flood-peak encounters. **Branches:** Mainstream and tributary reservoirs require different cooperation schemes; the appropriate role depends on flood type and location. **Output:** System-level coordination problem.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§“Challenges” and “Achievements,” pp. 1–4. Detailed routing uncertainty and exact decision equations are not reported in this article.

### E43-R1: Diagnose the control gap

**Description:** Local operators use present and desired reservoir levels for conservation and hydropower, but downstream Taungoo flood risk is not included. Coordinated control is therefore required.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** downstream flood risk is material during high inflow. **Inputs:** current levels, desired levels, downstream control level, multipurpose demands. **Judgment/procedure:** distinguish local storage objectives from system flood safety. **Branches:** retain current operation as a comparator. **Output:** need for coordinated predictive control.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit on p. 4, Section 2.1; the diagnosis is case-specific and does not prove that all local rules are inadequate.

## RP01-D The basis of the rule is outdated or too narrow

The manual is decades old, the flood regime is assumed stationary, or parameters were calibrated on a few events. Strength: Established (at threshold). 3 steps, 3 papers.

### E18-R1: Diagnose the WCM/forecast mismatch

**Description:** USACE WCMs prescribe reservoir-specific flood-control operations, but some are decades old and may omit changed watershed behavior, improved hydrologic understanding, and modern weather/streamflow prediction. This motivates examining forecast-informed adjustment rather than treating the historical rule curve as final.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A manual or operating plan may not reflect current watershed knowledge or forecast capability. **Inputs:** WCM age/content, watershed changes, available forecast science. **Judgment/procedure:** Identify whether forecast information could safely improve water management. **Branches:** The paper does not prescribe a universal threshold for “old” or “improved.” **Output:** Need for a site-specific FIRO viability investigation.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in Abstract and §1, pp. 1–2. The diagnosis and route are explicit; the decision thresholds are not reported.

### E20-R2: Diagnose the weakness of few-event or user-tuned calibration

**Description:** Calibration based on a limited number of recorded/design events may fit only those hydrologic conditions. Parameters supplied by users unfamiliar with mathematical programming can widen the gap between theoretical models and practice. A broad stochastic ensemble and automatic calibration are proposed to reduce this dependence.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A parametric RFCM must work across heavy/extreme floods. **Inputs:** Existing event-based calibration practice, parameter sensitivity, range of possible hydrologic loads. **Judgment/procedure:** Prefer automatic evaluation over a representative stochastic ensemble to event-specific tuning. **Branches:** Deterministic calibration remains possible but is recognized as less general; multiobjective aggregation or Pareto analysis is selected depending on commensurability. **Output:** Need for probabilistic parameter calibration.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§1–2.1, pp. 1–3. The paper does not quantify the bias from every alternative calibration strategy.

### E45-R1: Diagnose stationarity and consequence gap

**Description:** Changing climate and human activity can make flood probabilities time-varying. A threshold-exceedance probability alone does not express the size of flood damage when the threshold is exceeded.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** annual maximum streamflow is nonstationary or consequence magnitude matters. **Inputs:** time-varying exceedance probabilities, loss distribution, planning horizon. **Judgment/procedure:** separate flood hazard probability from damage-loss severity. **Branches:** stationary and nonstationary probability formulas. **Output:** need for a risk measure that carries tail magnitude.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–2, Introduction and Equations (1)–(2). This is methodological motivation, not proof of a particular climate mechanism in the case.

## EP01-A Assemble hydrologic, forecast, and operating data

Collect inflow, rainfall, forecast hindcasts, and operating records, aligned in time and space. Strength: Established. 8 steps, 8 papers.

### E04-E1: Assemble the TGR forecast-operation data

**Description:** They define the TGR case and flood-season frame, using observed and predicted inflow data from 2003–2009. The daily forecast is updated with a four-day horizon and is produced by a multiple-input single-output linear systematic model.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A case study of real-time flood-season operation is set up. **Inputs:** TGR inflow components (main stream, Wu River tributary, and intervening basin), Cuntan and Wulong station flows, areal rainfall from 40 precipitation stations, and the 2003–2009 forecast/observed data. **Procedure:** Use the stated daily rolling forecast and June 1–September 30 flood-season frame. **Output:** Aligned forecast and observed flows for error analysis.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 3.1, pp. 718–720; Fig. 5 and Table 2. Details of MLSM calibration and preprocessing are not reported in the supplied text.

### E07-E2: Assemble hindcast and common operational inputs

**Description:** The California–Nevada River Forecast Center generates a consistent 26-year retrospective HEFS record for Lake Mendocino inflow and downstream local flows. The record is hourly, has 61 members and a 15-day horizon, and is input to LMO at Lake Mendocino, West Fork, and Hopland; other sources provide unimpaired flows, Potter Valley Project transfers, losses, and release constraints.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Provide a long record for testing a forecast-informed alternative despite only a short operational forecast history. **Inputs:** 1985–2010 HEFS/ESP hindcasts, hydrologic observations/unimpaired flows, transfers, losses, water-supply operations, flood-control limits, ramping rates, and spillway rules. **Procedure:** Use the automated hindcast as a consistent realistic test sample; do not treat it as an exact reproduction of real-time forecaster refinements. **Output:** Daily simulation inputs for EFO and common inputs for comparators.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 6–8, Section 3.2. The hindcast assessment found underforecast bias in December–March but useful spread and upper-range reliability for 4–15 day leads; detailed hindcast methods are in supporting information and not fully present in the assigned text.

### E13-E1: Assemble case data and operating context

**Description:** Build the Lake Mendocino/Russian River forecasting and reservoir case used for fitting and policy simulation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Prepare synthetic forecasts for EFO. **Inputs:** 61-member HEFS hindcasts at LAMC, UKAC, HOPC; 1–14-day daily aggregates initialized daily from 1985–2010; gauge/full-natural-flow observations; GEFS v2 TMAX/TMIN/PRECIP and gauge MAT/MAP. **Procedure:** Adjust downstream observations for local flow and set negative observed full-natural-flow values to zero; retain the shared GEFS grid cell. **Output:** Aligned forecast/observation records and EFO inputs.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–5. Exact data-cleaning code and all missing-data handling are not reported in the supplied text.

### E14-E1: Assemble and classify the case data

**Description:** Collect rainfall and reservoir inflow records and divide flood events into C1/C2.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Fit cluster-specific flood forecasts. **Inputs:** Hourly rainfall at six stations and hourly Lishimen inflow, 1987–2020; 22 flood events; three weather indicators (3-h maximum rainfall, rainfall center, intensity). **Procedure:** Apply SOM to event attributes and assign 10 events to C1 and 12 to C2. **Output:** Two event classes and training/validation pools.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 11–14, Figure 4. The source reports no full SOM architecture or train/test randomization beyond the listed events.

### E19-E2: Assemble atmospheric, hydrologic, and reservoir data

**Description:** ERA5 total column water is used as PWV and ERA5 integrated vapor transport is calculated from six flux components. Observed precipitation is spatially averaged with Thiessen polygons; observed inflow, outflow, level, and other reservoir parameters are supplied by Jiaokou.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** PP and operations need synchronized vapor, precipitation, streamflow, and reservoir series. **Inputs:** ERA5 PWV/IVT, rain-gauge precipitation, inflow/outflow/level records, reservoir parameters. **Procedure:** Use hourly ERA5 at 0.25° and construct mean areal precipitation from gauges. **Output:** Time-aligned data for PP and operation calculations.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §2.2 and Table 2, p. 2. Gauge locations/counts, missing-data handling, spatial interpolation beyond Thiessen polygons, and exact IVT integration domain are not fully reported.

### E21-E1: Acquire and manage basin data

**Description:** ICDSS automatically collects and manages unstructured hydro-meteorological, operation, grid-load, regulation, and instruction data, plus semi-structured rules. The described network includes 1005 hydrological stations, 12,129 precipitation stations, and 37 ground-based radars; station failure rate was 1.7% over the prior three years.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Joint operation needs a current shared state and forecast basis. **Inputs:** Station observations, reservoir levels/releases, grid load, regulations/instructions, radar. **Procedure:** Automatically collect, organize, and expose the data to linked models and users. **Output:** Shared operational data context.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in “Data acquisition and management system,” p. 7. The paper does not report missing-data repair, quality-control thresholds, latency, or data-assimilation details.

### E43-E1: Assemble hydrologic and hydraulic inputs

**Description:** They combine DMH observations, 3-hour TRMM rainfall, SRTM DEM, surveyed river cross-sections, reservoir data, and operating information for the Sittaung basin.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** initialize model construction. **Inputs:** rainfall, evaporation, levels, discharges, catchment geometry, cross-sections, reservoir characteristics. **Procedure:** use TRMM for spatial/temporal rainfall coverage; derive catchments and tributary sections from DEM, slopes, and imagery. **Output:** basin model inputs.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4, 6, Figure 1. Daily observed data and limited spatial coverage are acknowledged; exact preprocessing is not fully reported.

### E50-E1: Set the multireservoir case and daily baseline data

**Description:** They model 14 reservoirs in the Sacramento, San Joaquin, and Tulare basins. The prior daily model is calibrated over 2009–2023; inflows are from California Data Exchange Center, while seasonal release patterns use daily medians from 1994–2023 and annual hydrology scaling.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** construct a common simulation across heterogeneous reservoirs. **Inputs:** observed daily inflows/releases/storage, capacities, TOCS, safe releases, ramping, seasonal demand proxy. **Procedure:** instantiate basin reservoirs and mass balance S_t=S_{t−1}+Q_t−R_t. **Output:** calibrated daily baseline simulator.

**Evidence boundary:** Evidence pp. 2–3. Baseline storage accuracy is average R²=0.865 across reservoirs over 2009–2023. Environmental/regulatory changes and informal operator forecast use are not represented.

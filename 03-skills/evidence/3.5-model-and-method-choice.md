# Evidence for unit 3.5-model-and-method-choice

Paper steps assigned to each small pattern of this unit, with their full card text. Step IDs: Exx = paper, Rn = reasoning step, En = execution step.

## RP08-A Reservoir system representation and computational trade-offs

Reservoir aggregation, channel storage, local linearization, routing-model fidelity, scenario-tree size, compact rules. Strength: Established. 11 steps, 10 papers.

### E13-R3: Select the synthesis space

**Description:** Directly fitting HEFS output (syn-HEFS) is computationally efficient and requires HEFS output plus hydrologic observations. Synthesizing GEFS meteorology and rerunning HEFS (syn-GEFS) retains a process-model pathway and can support manipulated forecast skill, but is much more expensive.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Choosing where forecast uncertainty is represented. **Inputs:** Available observations, HEFS architecture, computational resources, intended policy analysis. **Judgment:** Compare direct and forcing-space alternatives rather than assume the process route is superior. **Branches:** syn-HEFS versus syn-GEFS. **Output:** Two candidate generators with different applicability.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 5, 9–10, 19–20. The conclusion that syn-HEFS is often sufficient is case/model bounded.

### E17-R2: Select MPC for scalable control

**Description:** MPC re-optimizes release each timestep, can consume ensemble forecasts directly, avoids policy-training overfit, and scales better than SDP for large samples.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Need thousands of policy evaluations across sites/parameters. **Inputs:** Current storage/inflow and finite-horizon ensemble forecasts. **Judgment:** Use scenario-based convex MPC despite possible forecast-uncertainty sensitivity. **Output:** Repeated release sequence and accumulated storage.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2, 5–6. Alternative trace/tree MPC feedback is left for future work; scalability is a methodological choice, not proof of best operational policy.

### E23-R4: Select a routing representation that can support optimization

**Description:** Full dynamic hydraulics is accurate but too costly and lacks an adjoint for direct optimization. Simplified models are screened for accuracy and computational efficiency; the integrator-delay model gives the best stated accuracy/cost combination and can propagate outflow timing and nonlinear storage effects.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** downstream Pirapora flow must be predicted inside repeated optimization. **Inputs:** observed/simulated routing data, candidate SOBEK, kinematic-wave, integrator-delay, and Muskingum-Cunge models. **Judgment/procedure:** compare Bias, MAE, RMSE, NSE and visual gradient propagation, then choose the computationally feasible model. **Branches:** full dynamic model as reference; simplified model for optimization. **Output:** integrator-delay routing model.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1642–1644, Section 3.1, Tables 1–2. Selection is based on the reported assessment; no independent future-event validation of the chosen routing model is shown here.

### E30-R3: Represent hydraulic relationships locally

**Description:** Discharge and storage curves are nonlinear globally but approximately linear over a short level interval. Piecewise coefficients make the uncertain water-balance equation recursively solvable.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** advancing from time `t` to `t+1`. **Inputs:** deterministic level at each time, discharge/storage curves, short `Δt`. **Judgment/procedure:** fit `q̄(t)=μ_t H̄(t)+ν_t` and `V̄(t)=α_t H̄(t)+β_t` around the current mean level. **Branches:** coefficients change with time/level segment. **Output:** coefficients entering A(t), B(t), C(t) and error recursion.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–4, Fig. 2, Eqs. (8)–(12). The paper says `ΔH` is small and `Δt` short; fitting error and segment-selection diagnostics are not reported.

### E31-R3: Choose SDE plus local linearization

**Description:** A stochastic water balance propagates uncertain inflow, release, and storage; piecewise linear curves make the relation analytically recursive over short time intervals.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** uncertainty must be carried through routing. **Inputs:** water balance, mean level, discharge/storage curves, `Δt`. **Judgment/procedure:** write the balance SDE/difference equation, linearize `q̄=a_t Z̄+b_t` and `V̄=c_t Z̄+d_t`, and derive the `ω1–ω3` level-error recursion. **Output:** tractable level-error transition.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–5, Fig. 1, Eqs. (5)–(16). Linearization and normal assumptions are model choices; fitting errors are not reported.

### E35-R8: Treat branch/horizon selection as a performance-computation trade-off

**Description:** More branches capture more uncertainty but increase computational cost; longer horizons produce earlier, more conservative pre-release.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** configure TB-MPC for a case. **Inputs:** candidate branch counts 1–32, horizons 18–48 h, forecast scenario. **Judgment/procedure:** compare closed-loop peak release and CPU time; choose 16 branches and 48 h for later tests because 16–32 branch outputs overlap while cost rises. **Output:** selected operational configuration.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 4.3.1–4.3.2, pp. 15–17; Fig. 10–11; Table 2. There is no general branch-selection rule; the authors say it is user-defined.

### E41-R4: Use dense frequency coverage and fuzzy simulation

**Description:** The credibility of exceedance must account for possible flood frequencies, and the routing function is too nonlinear for a closed-form fuzzy program.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** evaluate a candidate IOWL under uncertain forecast error. **Inputs:** candidate frequencies, design hydrographs, d-level cut samples, routing model. **Judgment/procedure:** use 14 frequency levels, sample errors from cut sets, route each zoomed hydrograph, calculate credibility curve and area K. **Output:** K for each IOWL/stage.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–7, Section 3.3, Fig. 2–4; frequency list on p. 6. The authors state fine frequency division is important.

### E43-R2: Select a tractable predictive control architecture

**Description:** MPC is chosen because it predicts future states, solves constrained release decisions, and replans at each control step. A simplified internal model is used to meet computational requirements, with SOBEK providing detailed system behavior and state updates.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** releases must account for delayed river response and multiple reservoirs. **Inputs:** inflow forecasts, current states, structure equations, objectives, constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** use an internal linear state-space approximation with a high-resolution hydrodynamic simulation outside the optimization loop. **Branches:** model losses are neglected; free-flow structure assumptions apply. **Output:** executable predictive controller.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–7, Equations (1)–(9), Figures 2–3. Rationale is explicit; transfer to other basins is limited by case-specific rainfall-runoff and hydraulic calibration.

### E44-R2: Reduce system dimension at flood-control stations

**Description:** Direct risk analysis becomes difficult as reservoir count grows, so the system is aggregated into virtual reservoirs associated with flood-control stations, then later decomposed.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** cascade or parallel system has multiple reservoirs and control stations. **Inputs:** reservoir topology, station locations, interzone flows. **Judgment/procedure:** include each physical reservoir and intervening channel storage in the station-based aggregate; use recursive aggregation for series systems. **Branches:** series and parallel configurations have different inflow aggregation. **Output:** aggregation reservoirs with system-level storage, inflow, and release.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–4, Figure 2 and Section 2.1. The paper illustrates series aggregation and states applicability to parallel systems.

### E44-R3: Represent channel storage explicitly

**Description:** Flood routing changes storage and timing, so each channel segment is conceptualized as a channel storage reservoir (CSR) using Muskingum storage. This avoids treating channel routing as an unmodeled gap in the aggregate.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a channel segment lies between reservoirs and a control station. **Inputs:** Muskingum K and x, channel inflow/outflow, reservoir storages. **Judgment/procedure:** calculate CSR storage and add it to reservoir storage; use conservative estimates when precise hydraulic storage is unavailable. **Branches:** CSR may be omitted for comparison. **Output:** CSR-aware aggregate storage and boundaries.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–5, Equations (1)–(5), Figure 2. Authors note that hydraulic models could improve CSR estimates (p. 10).

### E50-R2: Use a compact risk-curve representation of EFO

**Description:** Prior EFO policies use ensemble forecasts and risk tolerance. The authors parameterize the curve with lead days of zero allowable risk and a slope after risk is tolerated, plus a TOCS multiplier, to enable cross-reservoir optimization and limit overfitting relative to 14 independent lead-time parameters.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** optimize a common, computationally manageable FIRO form across 14 reservoirs. **Inputs:** forecast ensemble storage exceedance risk, lead time, TOCS. **Judgment/procedure:** allow only the minimum release needed to bring forecast risk below the curve; raise TOCS if constraints remain feasible. **Branches:** risk curve applies to Forecast; Perfect collapses ensemble and its risk-curve parameters do not apply. **Output:** three policy parameters per reservoir and FIRO target release.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 2–4, Fig. 2. The “minimum release” and policy equation are clear in prose; exact parameter bounds are not reported in the supplied text.

## RP08-B Analytical derivation route

Derive optimal rules via KKT or equal marginal utility and state when they hold. Strength: Established. 5 steps, 5 papers.

### E03-R6: Use KKT conditions to identify the allocation regime

**Description:** Because the objective is stated to be convex and constraints are convex/affine, KKT conditions are treated as necessary and sufficient for the optimization. When storage, release, and minimum-margin bounds are nonbinding, optimality requires (f_1(W_1^*)=f_2(d^*)); when a bound binds, its shadow price explains why one objective receives all available RFCC or why storage is capped.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Solve the constrained allocation model for given forecast/risk conditions. **Inputs:** Loss functions, constraints, (W_1), (d), Lagrange multipliers. **Judgment/procedure:** Check which constraints bind and use stationarity/complementarity to determine whether marginal equality or a boundary solution applies. **Branches:** Lower-storage/flood-control boundary, interior hedging, upper-storage/conservation boundary, and minimum-safety-margin boundary. **Output:** Analytical conditions for piecewise optimal (W_1^*) and (d^*).

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 9–12, Section 4.1 and equations 31–35; Appendix A is referenced by the paper. The economic interpretation of multipliers is the authors' model interpretation, not evidence of observed operator judgments.

### E05-R4: Use marginal-utility equality or a boundary condition to select the rule

**Description:** Where storage and safety constraints are unbinding, optimality equalizes (f_1(W_1^*)) and (f_2(d^*)). At A, B, or D, the relevant boundary and marginal-utility equality determine when hedging starts or ends.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Select an optimum for a given (x), inflow, (r), and (r_a). **Inputs:** feasible-zone bounds and (f_1,f_2). **Judgment/procedure:** Solve equation (9) for the interior; compare marginal utilities at critical points to derive (x_D=x_{min}), (x_B=x_{max}), and (x_A). **Branches:** (x<x_{min}) yields zero excess storage and flood-control dominance; (x>x_{max}) yields maximum feasible conservation and conservation dominance; (x=x_A) separates the two nontrivial rule geometries. **Output:** Optimal (W_1^*), (d^*), and the preference-dependent hedging path.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2–3.2.1–3.2.3, pp. 4–8; equations (8)–(12); Figures 3 and 5. Equality is an analytical optimality condition; it does not imply that every operating decision is unconstrained.

### E24-R4: Establish marginal-value allocation logic

**Description:** Gaussian expected damage is decreasing and convex in EFSM, so each added unit gives diminishing benefit. With fixed total EFSM, KKT conditions are sufficient/necessary and imply equal marginal damage reduction unless a nonnegative margin bound is active.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** allocate Δ between stages. **Inputs:** D1, D2, Δ, stage uncertainty, nonnegativity constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** solve KKT; interpret λ1 as marginal value of total EFSM and λ2/λ3 as bound values. **Branches:** interior equal-marginal solution versus boundary allocation when δ1 or δ2 is zero. **Output:** optimal δ1, δ2 allocation condition.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 04014041-4–5, Eqs. (15)–(19), Table 1, Appendix III. The paper’s analytical sufficiency relies on the stated convex model.

### E25-R4: Derive the optimality condition

**Description:** Differentiating total expected damage yields current marginal cost and future expected marginal benefit. If no constraint binds, the optimum equalizes them; the overall cost must be convex in Qh for this stationary point to be a minimizing optimum.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** choose Qh inside feasible interval. **Inputs:** F and derivatives, storm probabilities/durations, storage relation dQsi/dQh=−tf/tsi. **Judgment/procedure:** set derivative to zero and compare current reliability-weighted marginal failure with expected storm-release marginal reduction. **Output:** Qh* condition and forecast-horizon implication.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5119–5121, Eqs. (9)–(15). The equality is theoretical and assumes differentiability/independence; constraints can eliminate an interior solution.

### E26-R4: Reduce to one conservation variable and derive optimality

**Description:** Water balance and boundary storage V2=0 allow R1/R2 to be represented through V1. The total risk becomes a one-dimensional function over [V1min,V1max], making KKT conditions interpretable.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** solve the two-phase stochastic model. **Inputs:** forecast means, σ errors, demand, Vu, Vs, release bounds, capacities, risk weights. **Judgment/procedure:** express Pu/Ps as normal tail integrals in V1 and solve dL/dV1 with lower/upper multipliers. **Branches:** interior marginal-risk balance; V1max if shortage-risk reduction dominates; V1min if flood-risk increase dominates. **Output:** V1* and implied release rule.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2740–2741, Eqs. (19)–(29). Analytical solution Eq. (29) is stated for V1*≤Vu and normal assumptions; formula OCR is partly corrupted, so exact symbolic transcription is not reproduced.

## RP08-C Choice of solution and search algorithm

Genetic and evolutionary algorithms, global solvers, Monte Carlo search. Strength: Established. 4 steps, 4 papers.

### E01-R7: Use Monte Carlo convergence to select the upper dynamic bound

**Description:** The authors reason that the bound should be found by repeated simulation because the relevant outcomes combine forecast uncertainty, hydrograph uncertainty, and alternative decisions. A candidate level is reduced until it satisfies the risk constraint, and the simulation-average bound is repeated until convergence.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A risk-constrained upper bound is needed before operation. **Inputs:** Simulated forecast series, candidate (Z), risk calculations, current FLWL (Z_0), and number of simulations. **Judgment/procedure:** Assume (Z), calculate the four risks, accept (Z) if Eq. (13) holds, otherwise reduce it; repeat until the change in the mean bound is below 0.05 m. **Branches:** Risk failure causes candidate-level reduction; convergence ends the search. **Output:** Dynamic bound ([Z_0,Z_u]).

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.5, p. 128, Eqs. (13) and (16), steps (1)–(5). The case uses 200 repetitions (Section 4, p. 130); the supplied text does not report a formal confidence interval for the resulting bound.

### E15-R6: Choose constrained evolutionary search

**Description:** The problem is non-linear, constrained, and many-objective; RSEA is selected to improve population diversity while maintaining convergence. Hard constraints must always hold, whereas output constraints can be ranked by violation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Solve the encoded HPFOR optimization. **Inputs:** Candidate parameter vectors, objective values, constraint violations. **Judgment/procedure:** Use region-associated subproblems, weights/neighborhoods, ideal/nadir normalization, reproduction, and population updates; dynamically repair rigid constraints and select lower violation for flexible ones. **Output:** Optimized parameter population and Pareto set.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 10–13, Algorithm 1, Table 2. RSEA superiority is relative to the tested algorithms and this problem; no global optimality is established.

### E22-R6: Use GA to search release/storage decisions for each scenario

**Description:** GA is selected for simplicity, robustness, and flexibility; grid search is combined with GA to improve parameter selection. Chromosomes are randomly generated, evaluated by fitness, and evolved by selection, crossover, and mutation until no further improvement is achieved.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A feasible multiobjective policy must be optimized for each inflow scenario. **Inputs:** Ensemble inflow, objective weights, constraints, encoded decision variables. **Judgment/procedure:** Evolve candidate policies and preserve high-fitness parents while mutation maintains diversity. **Branches:** Each ensemble member and each weight combination produces a separate run; ensemble mean can also be optimized. **Output:** Scenario-specific optimal release/storage policy.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.4.3–2.4.5, pp. 8–10. GA population size, crossover/mutation rates, termination tolerance, encoding, and constraint-handling details are not reported.

### E36-R5: Solve the deterministic equivalent globally

**Description:** The scenario model is high-dimensional and nonlinear; an optimizer is needed to obtain the scenario-wise release solution.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** stochastic model is formulated. **Inputs:** (T\times I) decision space, nonlinear curves, scenario constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** convert the scenario model to a nonlinear deterministic equivalent and solve with LINGO 17.0 Global Solver, which uses bounds/reduction and branch-and-bound. **Output:** optimal scenario trajectories/expected release guidance.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2.3, pp. 7–8; Section 3.2, p. 11; Table 3. The paper reports solver claims and CPU times but does not independently verify global optimality.

## RP08-D Methods for computing uncertainty and risk

SDE, Bayesian networks, maximum-entropy densities, mixture distributions, continuous integration. Strength: Established. 10 steps, 6 papers.

### E08-R3: Choose an interpretable dependency representation

**Description:** Routing equations explicitly define how storage, levels, outflow, lateral inflow, and downstream flow depend on one another, so the BN graph can encode those relations.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Represent the joint distribution without handling one high-dimensional PDF directly. **Inputs:** Reservoir balance and downstream routing equations. **Judgment/procedure:** Use expert knowledge for DAG structure; represent period-average flow variables and connect parent/child variables according to Eqs. (8)–(9). **Output:** BN structure.

**Evidence boundary:** §3.2.1, pp. 3–4, Fig. 3. Structure is expert-specified rather than structure-learned from data.

### E08-R4: Generate and discretize training evidence

**Description:** BN parameters require samples spanning uncertain inputs and feasible operation outcomes.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Learn conditional probability tables. **Inputs:** V0 distribution, LHS samples of reservoir/lateral inflows, routing constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** Solve an optimal flood-control model minimizing maximum outflow subject to water balance, level/outflow/discharge and ramping constraints; discretize continuous variables with equal-distance intervals and select K considering precision, samples, and cost. **Output:** Sampled V, H, q, QC and discretized training data.

**Evidence boundary:** §3.2.2, p. 4; case uses 1,000 samples (p. 5). The paper notes K sensitivity can be checked but does not give a complete selection protocol.

### E08-R5: Learn BN and select inference direction

**Description:** The BN can answer both forward risk questions and reverse diagnosis questions from observed consequences.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Need risk probabilities or likely causes. **Inputs:** Discretized complete samples and, for posterior queries, evidence variables. **Judgment/procedure:** Estimate CPTs by MLE; use prior inference without evidence, prediction given V0/Qmax/IUmax, or diagnosis given Hmax/QCmax using Bayes/joint-tree propagation. **Branches:** Prior, cause-to-effect, effect-to-cause. **Output:** Probability distributions of query nodes.

**Evidence boundary:** §§3.2.2–3.2.3, pp. 4–5. No claim that posterior inference itself authorizes a release.

### E30-R2: Select correlated SDE propagation

**Description:** Numerical simulation is flexible but sample-size dependent; FOSM/AFOSM/JC cannot readily retain uncertainty correlation or a non-explicit performance function. An SDE can propagate the uncertain water balance and derive a level distribution analytically.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** uncertainty must be carried through nonlinear reservoir routing. **Inputs:** water balance, uncertainty distributions, time dependence. **Judgment/procedure:** formulate stochastic storage balance and retain covariance between adjacent error terms. **Branches:** use local linearization to make the recursion tractable; assume other time points independent later in union-risk calculation. **Output:** recursive stochastic level-error representation.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–5, Eqs. (5)–(17), (21)–(29). The rationale is explicit; the approximation limits are assumptions.

### E32-R2: Choose DBN factorization and transfer links

**Description:** A DBN decomposes the high-dimensional joint distribution into static BNs plus links between adjacent time slices, making time-varying causal relationships explicit and computationally manageable.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** variables change through a flood and state at `t` depends on `t−1`. **Inputs:** variable dependencies, temporal transitions, topology. **Judgment/procedure:** factor the joint probability into node conditionals with current/previous parents; use a directed acyclic graph. **Branches:** prediction and diagnosis use the same posterior computation but condition on different evidence directions. **Output:** DBN model structure.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–4, Eqs. (1)–(4). Conditional independence is a structural assumption checked qualitatively; no independent causal validation is reported.

### E32-R3: Combine physical simulation data with expert structure

**Description:** The physical operation and routing models provide samples for learning, while explicit reservoir/river relationships are better encoded by expert knowledge than searched blindly.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a DBN must be constructed for a known flood-control topology. **Inputs:** historical flood, operation models, reservoir/river topology, flood propagation. **Judgment/procedure:** use LHS/physical routing to create training data; choose nodes/arcs from causal order and inspect conditional independence. **Output:** graph plus sample database.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–4, Sections 3.1–3.2. Expert structure and simulation samples are explicit; the credentials/process for eliciting expert knowledge are not reported.

### E32-R4: Make continuous risk variables learnable

**Description:** Continuous water levels/flows create difficult CPTs and may not be Gaussian. Equal-distance discretization supplies finite states and lets the DBN represent occurrence probabilities.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** learning/inference involves continuous state variables. **Inputs:** variable ranges, number of intervals `Mxi`, sample data. **Judgment/procedure:** divide each range into equal-width bins `Wxi=(xi,max−xi,min)/Mxi`; learn prior/CPT parameters by MLE. **Branches:** discretization precision trades risk accuracy against calculation load. **Output:** discrete node states, priors, and CPTs.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–5, Eq. (6). The paper does not report a principled bin-count selection or discretization-error bound.

### E36-R7: Infer the maximum-level density with POME

**Description:** A smooth density is needed for exceedance integration, but the scenario sample may be insufficient for direct distribution estimation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** estimate risk from scenario maxima. **Inputs:** moments of (Z_{max}), selected moment order, scenario outcomes. **Judgment/procedure:** use the maximum-entropy exponential-polynomial density and solve its Lagrange multipliers with Newton–Raphson; compare the resulting density with a normal curve and P–P plot. **Output:** (f(Z_{max})) and integrated risk.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.3.3, p. 9, Eq. (18); Section 3.2, pp. 11–12; Figs. 6–7. The paper says order 3 or 4 is appropriate but does not state the exact selected order in the supplied text.

### E39-R3: Prefer continuous integration to conditional interval approximation

**Description:** MLTP partitions error and approximates conditional probability within each interval, while the authors argue that integrating the exact joint density over a domain is more precise and naturally handles UHE/IHE.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** risk probability must retain source dependence and complex hazard geometry. **Inputs:** fE,Y(e,y), hazard domain Ω. **Judgment/procedure:** calculate P(H) = ∬Ω fE,Y(e,y) de dy. **Branches:** MLTP remains a comparison method for single hazards. **Output:** analytical/numerical AMIT risk probability.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4, 7, 15–16, Eq. (6), Appendix 3. The “analytical” framework still relies on gridded routing/interpolation to derive boundaries in the case study.

### E42-R2: Make mixture complexity and initialization data-driven

**Description:** Too few components harms fit; too many increase complexity. Arbitrary starting parameters can yield different EM results.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** configure the GMD before sampling. **Inputs:** error sample length, likelihood, candidate K, error clusters. **Judgment/procedure:** minimize AIC/BIC to choose K; use k-means++ to initialize centers/parameters; fit with EM. **Output:** IGMD for each lead time.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–7, Sections 2.1–2.2, Eqs. (2)–(12). In the case, AIC/BIC both select K = 3.

## RP08-E Construction choices for data-driven forecast models

Event clustering, MIC input selection, LSTM, model combination. Strength: Provisional. 4 steps, 2 papers.

### E14-R1: Classify event scale before fitting

**Description:** Small and large floods have different rainfall/flow characteristics and different relevance of risk, resilience, and vulnerability. The authors use two clusters to reduce model error given only 22 events.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Prepare a forecast model for heterogeneous flood events. **Inputs:** Short-term rainfall indicators and flood-event records. **Judgment:** Group events by similarity so model inputs and later objective interpretation reflect scale. **Branches:** C1 small-scale, C2 large-scale. **Output:** Two event classes for separate modeling.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3, 11–14, Figure 4. The two-cluster choice is case-specific and the paper says more return periods require future data.

### E14-R2: Select forecast information by association

**Description:** Forecast inputs should represent rainfall–runoff timing and avoid unnecessary variables. MIC captures linear or nonlinear association and selects precipitation lags above 0.3.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Define AI forecast inputs after clustering. **Inputs:** Antecedent/forecast precipitation at six stations and streamflow. **Procedure:** Calculate MIC for precipitation lags, retain candidate lags above 0.3, and use those in each cluster. **Output:** Cluster-specific input variables, interpreted as travel-time information.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–4, 12–14, Eq. (28), Figure 5. Threshold 0.3 and omission of streamflow/evaporation are study settings; selection rationale is partly constrained by data/validation limits.

### E14-R3: Combine heterogeneous deterministic models by distributional similarity

**Description:** No one AI model wins in all cases. The Cloud model is chosen because it compares forecast and observed distribution characteristics, assigns weights, and can retain complementary model behavior.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Produce a final deterministic inflow forecast. **Inputs:** LSTM, GRU, XGBoost forecasts and observed series divided into M segments. **Judgment/procedure:** Fit Gaussian Cloud characteristics (expectation, entropy, super-entropy), calculate overlap/similarity for each segment and model, average segment similarities, normalize to weights, and combine forecasts. **Output:** Cloud deterministic forecast.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–7, Eqs. (1)–(8), Figure 2. Exact segment boundaries and some model hyperparameters are not in the main text.

### E22-R2: Use NWP precipitation and LSTM for a fast ensemble inflow product

**Description:** Process models may be uncertain or slow for real-time use; LSTM is selected as a fast data-driven rainfall–runoff model. Feeding it ensemble precipitation should propagate meteorological uncertainty into ensemble reservoir inflow forecasts.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A real-time operation needs fast inflow scenarios at useful lead time. **Inputs:** Bias-corrected NWP precipitation ensembles, observed precipitation, temperature, potential evapotranspiration, discharge, prior LSTM state. **Judgment/procedure:** Train a sequential LSTM to map hydro-meteorological inputs to inflow hydrographs; use the same structure with ensemble precipitation to produce member forecasts. **Branches:** Deterministic precipitation → deterministic inflow; ensemble precipitation → probabilistic members/mean. **Output:** One-day-ahead ensemble inflow forecasts.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§1, 2.2–2.3, pp. 2–6. Model choice is justified by prior studies and speed; comparative architecture/uncertainty-calibration details are limited.

## RP08-F Methods for handling evaluation criteria

Network weights, sensitivity, PCA, entropy weights. Strength: Provisional. 4 steps, 2 papers.

### E47-R3: Use BP weights for inherent importance

**Description:** A trained network stores the nonlinear mapping in connection weights. The partial derivative shows that weighted input-to-hidden and hidden-to-output connections determine each criterion’s contribution; averaging across training cycles reduces random-initialization variation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** estimate a criterion’s inherent influence on closeness. **Inputs:** trained weights, hidden activations, repeated training results. **Judgment/procedure:** calculate Rj from absolute weighted paths and average over N training cycles. **Output:** relative importance ranking.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–5, Equations (8)–(13). Weight-based importance is model-dependent and not a causal effect estimate.

### E47-R4: Add one-at-a-time sensitivity

**Description:** Importance alone can miss a criterion that has modest average contribution but strongly changes output when perturbed. The sensitivity curve method changes one criterion by +/-5%, +/-10%, +/-15%, and +/-20% while holding others fixed.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** assess responsiveness of MCDM output to criterion changes. **Inputs:** original criterion vector, trained network, perturbation grid. **Judgment/procedure:** recalculate closeness for each one-variable perturbation and normalize the +20% change into Gj. **Output:** relative contribution ratio and sensitivity ranking.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–6, Equations (14)–(15) and Figure 4. One-at-a-time perturbations do not capture interactions among criteria.

### E48-R4: Diagnose correlation and choose PCA over deletion

**Description:** Correlated criteria repeat or interfere with information and can bias evaluation. Deleting correlated criteria may remove meaningful aspects and still leave residual correlation, so PCA is selected to transform the normalized criterion matrix into independent comprehensive criteria while retaining most information.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Pearson coefficients show widespread/high correlation. **Inputs:** alternative-by-criterion matrix, criterion means/standard deviations, correlation matrix. **Judgment/procedure:** compute Pearson correlation; perform PCA; retain the smallest number of components whose cumulative variance contribution exceeds 90%; interpret loadings. **Branches:** number of retained components depends on the threshold and eigenvalues. **Output:** independent principal-component decision matrix and loading interpretation.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 4–5 and 9–11. In the case, two components retain 96.7%; the paper reports linear independence of Y1 and Y2.

### E48-R5: Derive weights without unstable entropy conversion

**Description:** The entropy method uses criterion variation to determine weights. The authors use an improved conversion because the conventional conversion can fluctuate strongly when entropy is close to one.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** principal components require relative importance for ranking. **Inputs:** normalized principal-component matrix and entropy values. **Judgment/procedure:** normalize benefit/cost criteria, calculate entropy, and apply the improved entropy-to-weight formula. **Branches:** benefit and cost normalization differ; zero proportions use ln(f)=0. **Output:** weight vector for comprehensive criteria.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 5–6 and Eq. (7)–(11). The case weight vector is E=(0.528, 0.472), p. 11. The source does not report a separately elicited preference weight.

## EP06-A Derive analytical or piecewise rules

Use KKT, equal marginal utility, or heuristic steps to obtain piecewise hedging rules directly. Strength: Established. 18 steps, 7 papers.

### E03-E7: Optimize carryover storage and safety margin

**Description:** Minimize (L_1+L_2) over (W_1,d) subject to mass balance, storage bounds, minimum release, and (d\ge d_{​min}). The RFCC identity links the variables, and KKT conditions determine whether the optimum is interior or lies on a boundary.

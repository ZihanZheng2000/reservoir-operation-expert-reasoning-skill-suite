# Evidence for unit 3.4-trade-offs-and-selection

Paper steps assigned to each small pattern of this unit, with their full card text. Step IDs: Exx = paper, Rn = reasoning step, En = execution step.

## RP06-A Identify conflicting objectives

Safety and benefits trade against each other, or dam safety trades against downstream safety. Strength: Established. 21 steps, 21 papers.

### E01-R1: Reframe fixed FLWL as a flood-control/conservation trade-off

**Description:** A fixed FLWL is intended to retain adequate flood storage, but applying it throughout the flood season can give too much priority to low-probability floods and reduce floodwater utilization. Forecast information makes a conditional, dynamic bound a plausible adjustment.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A fixed flood-season level is being used despite changing forecast and hydrologic conditions. **Inputs:** Current FLWL, flood-season operation objectives, forecast availability, and conservation/flood-control trade-off. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat the FLWL as an operating interval rather than an invariant level, subject to a safety-preserving upper bound. **Branches:** None stated at this conceptual step. **Output:** Need for a dynamic-control bound and forecast-informed decisions.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicitly stated in the abstract and Introduction, pp. 124–125. The claim is a design rationale, not evidence that every reservoir should use a dynamic FLWL.

### E02-R3: Define the competing operating objectives

**Description:** The operating problem contains a structural conflict: lowering reservoir level protects the dam and upstream area but generally requires more release, whereas reducing release protects the downstream control point but stores more water. The authors retain both objectives instead of collapsing them into an assumed single weighted objective.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Candidate releases must balance two safety concerns. **Inputs:** scenario inflows, reservoir levels, releases, and scenario probabilities. **Judgment/procedure:** Minimize expected highest reservoir water level and expected peak reservoir discharge over the forecast scenarios. **Branches:** No single compromise is selected before the Pareto set is generated. **Output:** Two stochastic objective values for each candidate policy.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2 and equations (6)–(7), pp. 5–6; Introduction, pp. 1–2. Hydropower and water supply are mentioned as general competing goals, but the implemented optimization uses the two stated flood-control objectives.

### E03-R1: Reframe static FLWL as a forecast-responsive trade-off

**Description:** Static FLWL is designed from annual design storms or floods and ignores annual/seasonal inflow variation and real-time meteorological and hydrological information. This can produce unnecessary flood-season spills, post-season water shortage, or exposure when the actual flood season is delayed. Therefore the paper treats the active flood-limited level as a dynamic decision within lower and upper bounds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A fixed or static multistage FLWL is unable to use current forecasts or seasonal variation. **Inputs:** Flood-season inflow variation, existing FLWL practice, water-supply and flood-control purposes. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare the benefits of additional conservation with the consequence of losing flood-control storage. **Branches:** Use dynamic adjustment only within specified FLWL bounds; the baseline remains the primary/static flood-control regulation beyond the forecast horizon. **Output:** A need for DC-FLWL and a bounded dynamic storage decision.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit motivation in pp. 1–3, Introduction. The paper establishes the trade-off and proposed route; it does not claim that all reservoirs can use identical bounds or triggers.

### E05-R1: Reframe fixed flood-control storage as an adaptive trade-off

**Description:** Fixed flood-control space can be unused when no large flood occurs and can be insufficient for a large flood. The paper consequently asks how much storage may be conserved now while retaining later flood-control protection under forecast uncertainty.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A reservoir serves both flood control and water conservation and fixed allocation is potentially inefficient. **Inputs:** competing purposes, forecast information, expected future inflow, downstream flood-conveyance capacity, and water-use requirements. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat current conservation and future flood control as a hedging problem rather than a fixed-storage rule. **Branches:** If uncertainty or preference is outside a feasible range, one objective dominates and the problem reduces to a single objective. **Output:** A two-stage decision problem with explicit trade-off variables.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit motivation and questions in Sections 1–2, pp. 1–2. The adaptive framing is a research/modeling rationale; no real-time authorization protocol is reported.

### E07-R1: Diagnose the guide-curve trade-off

**Description:** The authors reason that static guide curves reserve flood space from climatological averages and can be overly conservative for flood protection, reducing water-supply storage. This is especially problematic when annual precipitation timing, climate, watershed conditions, and demands depart from historical averages; the 2012–2013 example illustrates water released for nominal flood space before a historically dry period.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A multipurpose reservoir must satisfy flood protection and supply/ecosystem needs under variable inflow timing. **Inputs:** Seasonal guide curve, storage conditions, historical climate/hydrology, operational objectives. **Judgment/procedure:** Identify where fixed seasonal storage targets force releases despite later dry conditions, while preserving the need for flood space. **Branches:** None stated as an algorithmic branch. **Output:** Requirement for an adaptive release decision that can retain water yet preserve flood capacity.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit motivation in pp. 1–6, Sections 1–2. The 2013 example is illustrative; the paper does not quantify a counterfactual EFO decision for that historical operation.

### E08-R1: Frame the DC-FLWL trade-off and uncertainty scope

**Description:** Pre-refill/pre-release can increase floodwater utilization but forecast error may cause FLWL exceedance. The paper focuses on initial water-level uncertainty and flood-forecast error as primary risk sources.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Consider operating above/around the design FLWL during a flood season. **Inputs:** Recession/rainless-day forecast, forecast inflows, current storage, downstream safety discharge. **Judgment/procedure:** Include the two uncertainty sources jointly; exclude discharge capacity and storage-curve uncertainty by scope choice. **Branches:** Initial-level only, inflow only, or both factors. **Output:** Defined stochastic risk problem.

**Evidence boundary:** §§1–2, pp. 1–3. Exclusions are explicit; extractor does not infer omitted factors are negligible.

### E10-R6: Evaluate by safety/utilization trade-off

**Description:** Compare FCC exceedance and generation across operation rules and perturbations to determine where stage-aware forecasts help.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Validate proposed rule in case simulation. **Inputs:** 12 floods, COR, Two-OR, forecast-error scenarios, RFH lengths, amplified floods. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare peak/volume types, error differences, RFH extension, and baseline rules. **Output:** Case-bounded performance findings.

**Evidence boundary:** §§3–5, pp. 7–11. No independent deployment validation.

### E12-R1: Define forecast-informed pre-release decision

**Description:** Pre-release increases flood space but its uncertainty creates conservation risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Forecast inflow is in early-flood discriminant range Qdown–Qup. **Inputs:** Forecast inflow, current storage, FLWL, minimum downstream demand. **Judgment/procedure:** Release now while requiring future inflow plus minimum-demand balance to restore FLWL if no flood occurs. **Branches:** Pre-release versus later detention when forecast exceeds Qup; potential restore if no flood. **Output:** Feasible early release.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.1–2.2, pp. 2–4, Eqs. (1)–(5). Exact Qdown/Qup calibration is case-specific.

### E18-R5: Form alternatives that trade storage/use benefits against flood protection

**Description:** At Lake Mendocino, the four FIRO alternatives are reasoned to carry more storage into the dry season while lowering the reservoir below the guide curve when major storms are predicted. At Prado, FIRO alternatives and buffer-pool elevations are evaluated for greater recharge while retaining flood-risk performance. At Yuba–Feather, flood-risk reduction is primary, with water supply secondary and environmental needs supported.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A site has competing flood, water-supply, recharge, or environmental objectives. **Inputs:** Forecasts, guide curves/buffer-pool assumptions, storage requirements, downstream capacity, objective priorities. **Judgment/procedure:** Construct candidate forecast-informed operating alternatives that create storage/release flexibility under predicted events. **Branches:** Objective priority and allowable storage assumptions differ by site; Yuba–Feather alternatives are not directly comparable to each other because assumptions differ. **Output:** Site-specific FIRO alternatives for simulation/comparison.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.1–2.3, pp. 4–8. The precise rules, forecast thresholds, and release triggers are not reported in this article; they reside in cited viability assessments.

### E19-R9: Quantify benefits and safety across a flood-event sample

**Description:** The study calculates water-level, storage, spill, outflow-peak, and duration statistics for 50 floods between 2011 and 2023. Higher final storage and lower spills indicate water-use benefit; lower peaks/durations and lower maximum levels indicate flood-control benefit, subject to comparator differences.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Representative events alone are insufficient to summarize inter-event behavior. **Inputs:** 50 event simulations under VPS-FIRO, PS-FIRO, and historical schemes; defined evaluation indexes. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare distributions and means, preserving the meaning of each index and noting initial-level confounding. **Output:** Aggregate performance evidence.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.3 and §4.3, pp. 5, 10–11, Figs. 10–11. The sampling/event-selection rule beyond “50 flood events occurring between 2011 and 2023” is not reported.

### E20-R1: Frame flood operation as a conflict between two consequences

**Description:** Flood operation must limit downstream damaging discharges while preventing reservoir levels that endanger the dam. For small floods dam safety may be less salient, but extreme loads make dam safety a priority; the authors therefore treat the two concerns as a multiobjective calibration problem.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A flood-control policy must balance release and storage consequences. **Inputs:** Downstream channel/land-use exposure, dam/reservoir safety characteristics, inflow magnitude, operating policy. **Judgment/procedure:** Represent released-flow damage and stored-volume/dam-failure risk as separate objectives. **Branches:** Under extreme floods, dam safety may take precedence; exact priority is not fixed universally. **Output:** Two risk objectives for calibration.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§1–2, pp. 1–3. The priority discussion is qualitative; no universal risk-aversion rule is established.

### E21-R2: Make competing objectives explicit

**Description:** Reservoir actions simultaneously affect flood protection, power generation, shipping, ecology, maintenance, and transmission limits. Water resources authorities seek flood mitigation, while transport and power organizations seek shorter abnormal operation and reliable generation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A proposed release/impoundment affects more than flood control. **Inputs:** Protection objectives, generation schedules, shipping requirements, ecological/maintenance constraints, transmission limits. **Judgment/procedure:** Balance requirements rather than optimize one objective in isolation. **Branches:** A flood-control action may be altered to maintain power/shipping within acceptable limits; exact priority rules are not specified. **Output:** Multi-department objective/constraint set.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §“Coordination of multiple departments,” p. 4 and Methods, pp. 7–8. The paper does not give a formal utility function or negotiated weighting scheme.

### E22-R3: Balance release-peak reduction against storage retention

**Description:** A flood operation should minimize peak release to reduce downstream damage, yet maintain storage to support water supply and hydropower. The authors formulate these as two conflicting objectives rather than a single flood-only target.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Lowering levels reduces release risk but can sacrifice stored water. **Inputs:** Release time series, storage time series, flood horizon, storage value, downstream damage concern. **Judgment/procedure:** Minimize maximum release (F1) and minimize the stated storage-deficit expression (F2) to increase storage. **Branches:** Weighting can emphasize release or storage; no single preference is assumed before grid search. **Output:** Multiobjective operating policy.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §2.4.1, pp. 7–8, Eqs. (7)–(8). The exact F2 expression is reproduced in OCR as minimizing Smax minus the sum of St; interpretation follows the authors' stated goal of maximizing storage.

### E26-R1: Frame conservation as a flood/shortage trade-off

**Description:** After a flood recedes, spare capacity could conserve water for dry periods, but the amount must remain compatible with flood-control reliability and future demand. Capacity-constrained pre-release rules do not explicitly model forecast-error risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** pre-refill/pre-release operation with surplus inflow and later shortage. **Inputs:** initial storage, inflow/demand forecasts, flood storage/conservation capacity, safe releases, forecast precision. **Judgment/procedure:** treat conservation as a risky decision balancing current/future phases. **Branches:** FRs prioritize flood control; CRs use capacity; HRs minimize weighted risk. **Output:** need for stochastic analytical HR.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2731–2736, Introduction and Fig. 1. The two-phase abstraction and relative storage below FLWL are deliberate simplifications.

### E28-R1: Frame the forecast-enabled operating trade-off

**Description:** Forecast information can identify a flood before its lead time and permit a higher flood-season level, but forecast uncertainty can make that extra stored water unsafe. Risk is framed as flood load exceeding the reservoir’s regulating resistance.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** considering forecast-based prerelease or a raised flood-limited level. **Inputs:** current storage, forecast lead time, expected inflow, flood-control capacity. **Judgment/procedure:** weigh water-use/storage benefit against level-exceedance risk. **Branches:** use forecast information when accuracy is adequate; otherwise conventional storage-based rules remain relevant. **Output:** need for probabilistic forecast-risk assessment.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 1–2. The text motivates the trade-off; it does not provide a formal benefit optimizer.

### E29-R1: Frame the static-FLWL limitation as a benefit–safety trade-off

**Description:** A fixed FLWL protects flood storage but can leave the reservoir underused if the flood season timing differs from expectation. Forecast-based dynamic control may store more water without exceeding the design risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a fixed flood-season level is underusing water. **Inputs:** flood-season timing, forecast horizon, available storage, safety and water-use objectives. **Judgment/procedure:** compare static protection with dynamic upper/lower bounds and prerelease/refill. **Branches:** dynamic control is intended for medium/small inflows below the downstream-risk threshold. **Output:** a forecast-enabled control route.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–2 and 4. The benefit argument is conceptual; it is not an optimization proof.

### E33-R1: Frame forecast uncertainty as an operational risk

**Description:** Real-time decisions are based on forecast inflows, but stochastic inflow can make an apparently optimal decision unsafe. Risk analysis is therefore needed to balance flood hazard and operational benefit.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a real-time decision must cover future periods. **Inputs:** forecast inflows, reservoir state, critical release or level. **Judgment/procedure:** treat forecast error as a source of possible failure. **Branches:** analytical methods are discussed, but the study chooses numerical simulation because multi-reservoir joint uncertainty is high-dimensional and nonlinear. **Output:** need for a scenario-based risk constraint.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in Introduction, pp. 1–2. The paper does not claim that all uncertainty sources are represented; the case model uses stochastic inflow uncertainty only.

### E34-R1: Frame conservation as a coupled risk trade-off

**Description:** Keeping every reservoir below the designed FLWL preserves flood storage but causes frequent spillage and worsens water scarcity. Raising/retaining storage can conserve water, but forecast underestimation can threaten reservoirs and downstream regions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a receding flood and water-supply shortage risk coexist. **Inputs:** water demand, current storage, forecast inflow, flood-safety limits. **Judgment/procedure:** treat conservation benefit and flood risks as conflicting objectives. **Branches:** the paper distinguishes deterministic screening/soft-risk approaches from direct stochastic optimization. **Output:** a multi-risk decision problem.

**Evidence boundary:** Introduction, pp. 1–2. “Risk” is operationalized later as surrogate loss ratios, not observed damages.

### E35-R1: Identify the short-term control conflict

**Description:** The reservoir must release enough water to protect a narrow downstream reach while retaining/recovering water for supply. Forecast errors can delay releases and cause excess downstream spillage.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** flood inflows arrive while storage is high or capacity is limited. **Inputs:** current forebay level, predicted inflow, downstream limit, water-supply target. **Judgment/procedure:** treat flood mitigation and end-of-event storage recovery as simultaneous objectives. **Branches:** guide curves/deterministic MPC are baseline approaches; stochastic MPC is considered when forecasts are uncertain. **Output:** need for uncertainty-aware receding control.

**Evidence boundary:** Introduction, pp. 1–3; Sections 3.1–3.2, pp. 7–9.

### E42-R5: Separate flood and curtailment pathways

**Description:** IFU overestimation may cause flood-related threshold exceedance; underestimation may cause water deficits and curtailed generation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** quantify consequences of forecast uncertainty. **Inputs:** ensemble inflows, design flood Qrp, reservoir operation rules, power-generation model. **Judgment/procedure:** count ensemble events above Qrp for flood risk; optimize power generation under each inflow input and compare against observations for curtailment. **Output:** coupled risk measures.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 8–9 and 16–20, Sections 2.4–2.5 and 4.3. Flood risk is a threshold rate; curtailment is a generation deviation, not a direct economic loss.

### E44-R1: Frame dynamic boundary need

**Description:** Static FLWL operation preserves flood-control storage but may spill water and create shortages later. Dynamic WLFS can trade flood safety against water conservation using hydrologic information.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** flood-season water level is fixed at FLWL. **Inputs:** flood risk requirements, water conservation and power objectives, forecasts. **Judgment/procedure:** permit controlled departures from FLWL only inside explicit risk and refill limits. **Branches:** upper and lower boundaries define the allowed dynamic range. **Output:** dynamic-boundary design problem.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–2, Introduction. The motivation is explicit; no general performance claim follows from it alone.

## RP06-B Allocate limited resources over time or across the system

Store now or keep for later; upstream reservoirs take over downstream tasks. Strength: Established. 13 steps, 7 papers.

### E03-R3: Allocate residual capacity between conservation and future flood safety

**Description:** With Stage-2 ending storage fixed at the lower FLWL bound, the expected RFCC is shared by Stage-1 excess storage (W_1) and Stage-2 safety margin (d). Storing more water now leaves less margin for an uncertain future release, while retaining margin reduces current conservation. This resource-allocation identity is the central justification for hedging.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** After the forecast and risk-feasible release limits are known. **Inputs:** (R_{2,​max}), expected Stage-2 inflow, (W_1), (d). **Judgment/procedure:** Apply (W_1+d=R_{2,​max}-\bar I_2) and compare the value of one more unit of carryover storage with one more unit of safety margin. **Branches:** With a perfect forecast, (d=0); with uncertainty, (d>0) and the allocation is split. **Output:** A feasible conservation/safety allocation problem.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–5 and 9–10, equations 6–7 and 29–30. This is a model identity under the stated ending-storage assumption, not an independently observed operational law.

### E03-R5: Define a weighted loss and marginal utilities for the two objectives

**Description:** The paper uses normalized deviations from target storage as water-conservation loss and the probability of excessive release as flood-risk loss. A weight (x) represents the decision-maker's relative emphasis on flood control, and exponent (m>1) makes the conservation loss convex. Marginal utilities (f_1(W_1)) and (f_2(d)) provide a common comparison for allocation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Multiple feasible allocations remain after hard constraints. **Inputs:** Target storage (W_N), lower/upper excess-storage bounds, risk function, (xin[0,1]), (m>1). **Judgment/procedure:** Minimize (L_1+L_2), then compare the marginal loss reduction from more Stage-1 storage with that from more Stage-2 safety margin. **Branches:** (x) near 0 favors conservation; larger (x) favors flood protection; binding bounds replace equality of marginal utilities. **Output:** A convex optimization objective and the marginal-utility relationships used to classify policies.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–7, Section 3.1 and equations 14–17; pp. 9–10 and 15–16. The selected (x) and (m) are decision parameters; the paper does not present an external calibration procedure for them.

### E03-R7: Classify cases by uncertainty, acceptable risk, and expected inflow

**Description:** The starting point of hedging depends on the relationship between (f_2(d_{​min})), (f_1(0)), and (f_1(W_{​max})). This yields three cases: small uncertainty/large acceptable risk, medium values, and large uncertainty/small acceptable risk. Within each case, RFCC or expected inflow determines whether all capacity goes to safety, is split by marginal equality, or is assigned to conservation above the required margin.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Known forecast-error spread, acceptable risk, and expected Stage-2 inflow. **Inputs:** (d_{​min}), critical (d_0,d_e), optional (W_{1h}), corresponding RFCC and inflow thresholds. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare marginal utilities and locate the forecast/inflow segment. **Branches:** Case 1 has three segments (flood control, hedging, conservation cap); Case 2 adds a minimum-margin conservation segment before hedging; Case 3 uses the minimum margin and then conservation because flood-control marginal utility is always lower over the feasible storage range. **Output:** A case-specific policy branch and thresholds.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 11–15, Sections 4.2–4.3, equations 35–46, Figures 3–5, Table 1. The classification is analytical under the paper's assumed error and loss functions.

### E05-R2: Encode the competing objectives in two linked stages

**Description:** Carryover storage (W_1) determines both conservation benefit in stage 1 and the remaining flood-control safety margin in stage 2. The objective weight (x) represents preference for conservation, while (1-x) weights flood control.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** The two objectives must be optimized jointly. **Inputs:** (W_1), stage-2 safety margin (d), forecast-error density (h(e)), uncertainty (r), and weight (x). **Judgment/procedure:** Minimize (x g_{w,1}(W_1)+(1-x)g_{f,2}(d,r)); use the convex conservation-loss exponent (m>1) and normal forecast-error form for the stated closed-form risk/marginal-utility expressions. **Branches:** Stage-1 flood risk is set to zero under deterministic near-future inflow; stage-2 conservation loss is one because (W_1=0) at the lower-FLWL endpoint. **Output:** Weighted loss functions and marginal utilities (f_1(W_1)) and (f_2(d)).

**Evidence boundary:** Model assumptions and equations (1)–(5), pp. 3–4. The weighting is a preference representation, not an empirically elicited stakeholder utility in the supplied text.

### E05-R5: Classify the hedging-rule type from the starting boundary

**Description:** The location of the rule's starting point reflects whether flood control or conservation has the larger marginal utility at the minimum safety margin. This yields three paper-specific rule types.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** (x) lies in the feasible preference interval. **Inputs:** (x_{min},x_A,x_{max}), marginal-utility comparisons, and feasible-zone geometry. **Judgment/procedure:** Type 1 for (x=x_A), beginning at A; Type 2 for (x_{min}<x<x_A), beginning on AD and starting conservation only at lower inflow; Type 3 for (x_A<x<x_{max}), beginning on AB and allowing conservation at the largest DC-FLWL inflow. **Branches:** As (x) decreases within Type 2, storage begins at smaller inflow and may end below (W_{max}); as (x) increases within Type 3, the start moves upward on AB and maximum conservation is reached more readily. **Output:** Rule type and its start/end segments for numerical search.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 3.2.3–3.2.4, pp. 7–9; Figures 3 and 5. The labels and geometry are the authors' definitions for this model, not universal reservoir rule categories.

### E10-R3: Allocate storage across RFH and outside RFH

**Description:** Relative marginal risk indicates whether storage should be moved earlier or later, subject to FLWL/MWL bounds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Solve outer two-stage model. **Inputs:** s12, s3, stage risks and densities, storage bounds. **Judgment/procedure:** Apply KKT; if lower bound active, allocate more to later stage; if upper bound active, allocate all available to RFH; otherwise equalize marginal risks. **Output:** s12 and s3.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.3.2, pp. 5–6, Eqs. (11)–(16). KKT sufficiency follows stated convexity.

### E10-R4: Allocate RFH storage to current action

**Description:** Within RFH, current versus second-stage risk determines detention or prerelease allocation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Outer allocation is available at each operation time. **Inputs:** s12, current/second forecast flows, risk densities. **Judgment/procedure:** For s12≥0, allocate detention under KKT; for s12<0, allocate prerelease; endpoint branches select stage with larger marginal risk, interior branch equalizes it. **Output:** s1 and s2, especially current s1.

**Evidence boundary:** §2.3.3, pp. 5–7, Eqs. (17)–(25).

### E21-R4: Allocate reserved capacity by flood type, role, and encounter timing

**Description:** The upper cascade reserves capacity for cooperation; surplus capacity can share downstream tasks when not all flood types need simultaneous protection. Release order depends on flood-season stage, reservoir classification, flood encounter situation, local task, and role relative to TGR.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A downstream/control reservoir needs help and upstream capacity is available. **Inputs:** Flood type, flood-season stage, reservoir class, current inflow/level, reserved/surplus capacity, travel time, downstream objective. **Judgment/procedure:** Select which reservoir's reserved capacity to use and when, so peaks are staggered and basin risk is reduced. **Branches:** Use different schemes for mainstream/tributary events; release capacity gradually when post-flood inflow declines; avoid releasing all capacity when simultaneous flood-type protection is not needed. **Output:** Ordered, coordinated capacity-release plan.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 3, 6, and Methods p. 7. Exact allocation algorithm/thresholds and formal risk proof are not reported.

### E21-R5: Let surplus upstream capacity compensate downstream tasks

**Description:** If upstream reservoirs have surplus capacity for the current flood type, they can hold part of the flood, allowing downstream reservoirs such as TGR to remain at a higher level and avoid unnecessary spillage while the entire basin's flood risk remains controlled.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Some reservoirs have unused reserved capacity and the downstream system needs flood-control space. **Inputs:** Flood classification, reservoir capacities/levels, forecast timing, downstream flood-control requirement. **Judgment/procedure:** Transfer part of the flood-control burden upstream by impounding there; accept a higher downstream level when system-level risk is still controlled. **Branches:** Compensation depends on relatively accurate forecasts and the joint-operation pattern; if flood types coincide, surplus may not exist. **Output:** Basin-level capacity compensation and benefit-preserving level trajectory.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 6–7, including TGR 153.03 m versus FLWL 145 m example. The article does not quantify a general admissible compensation boundary.

### E24-R1: Define the intertemporal flood trade-off

**Description:** Current and future decisions compete for storage/conveyance capacity. More current release can increase current downstream flood risk, while more retained water raises future risk; a two-stage abstraction makes this resource allocation explicit.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** periodically updated flood forecast over an event. **Inputs:** initial storage, upstream/intermediate expected flows, conveyance capacity, current/future periods. **Judgment/procedure:** represent current period as Stage 1 and remaining horizon until flood pool empty as Stage 2; use stage-average square-wave flows. **Branches:** conceptual two-stage model versus more complex real system. **Output:** linked current/future balance.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 04014041-2–3. Intrastage variability, multi-stage detail, and actual system complexity are intentionally simplified.

### E24-R5: Classify risk from uncertainty and total margin

**Description:** Because future-stage uncertainty is assumed larger (σ2 > Tσ1 in the text), the marginal value curves cross at K1 and K2. Comparing Δ with K1 and 2K2 yields distinct release/storage policies.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** an expected flood must be classified operationally. **Inputs:** total EFSM Δ, σ1/σ2, T, qthres and expected volume. **Judgment/procedure:** compute K1 where f1(K1)=f2(0), K2 where f1(K2)=f2(K2), then compare ranges. **Branches:** high risk Δ<K1 → all margin Stage 1; medium K1≤Δ≤2K2 → equalize with δ1≥δ2; low risk Δ>2K2 → equalize with δ2>δ1. **Output:** risk class and EFSM allocation regime.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 04014041-5–6, Eqs. (20)–(24), Fig. 3. Threshold derivation depends on Gaussian uncertainty and σ2>Tσ1; classifications are not universal outside assumptions.

### E25-R1: Identify the current/future trade-off

**Description:** A pre-storm release creates room for future storms but can cause immediate downstream damage and water-supply loss. The useful question is how much space to create given uncertain storm volume, duration, and failure response.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** reliable short forecast before an oncoming flood. **Inputs:** initial storage, antecedent inflow, forecast period, possible storm volumes/durations/probabilities, reservoir capacity. **Judgment/procedure:** compare marginal current release risk with expected future risk reduction. **Branches:** no hedging versus positive Qh; optional water-supply penalty. **Output:** single decision variable Qh and feasible range.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5113–5116, Sections 1–2.2. Antecedent inflow is treated as perfectly known and storm hydrographs as square waves, both simplifying assumptions.

### E26-R5: Turn KKT solution into three operational zones

**Description:** The current release curve intersects feasible lower/upper release bounds, defining SWA/EWA trigger points and MWA. Relative to A, low-flood/high-shortage conditions conserve maximally; intermediate conditions use R1*; high-flood/low-shortage conditions release at Ru1.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** operate once thresholds are calculated and A observed/forecast. **Inputs:** expected availability A, demand D1, SWA/EWA/MWA, R1*, release bounds. **Judgment/procedure:** apply piecewise rule: Zone I R1=D1; Zone II R1=R1*; Zone III R1=Ru1. **Branches:** A below SWA, within trigger range, or above EWA. **Output:** current release and conservation amount.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2736–2737, Fig. 3 and Eq. (4). Zone labels and directional explanations are explicit; exact threshold computation is tied to model parameters and not a universal rule.

## RP06-C Safety first: define the safe domain, then optimize benefits

Safety is a non-compensable constraint; benefits are pursued only once it is met. Strength: Established. 14 steps, 11 papers.

### E12-R2: Represent forecast uncertainty by its distribution

**Description:** Maximum/minimum-error rules are safe but optimize rare extremes; distribution-based rules target the most probable forecast and assess the remaining risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Need an operating rule under uncertain forecasts. **Inputs:** Forecast errors. **Judgment/procedure:** Fit maximum-entropy error distribution; identify mode δ0 and 99.99% interval δmin–δmax. **Output:** Forecast scenarios for optimization/evaluation.

**Evidence boundary:** §2.1, pp. 3 and 10–12. Distribution fitting details/data are limited in supplied text.

### E18-R7: Select an implementable pilot strategy when performance is favorable

**Description:** For Lake Mendocino, the selected approach both improves water supply reliability/flood protection outcomes and can be implemented with standard USACE decision tools while allowing growth as forecast skill and models improve. Prado results indicate higher buffer pools improve recharge and FIRO slightly lowers emergency spillway flow and releases over downstream channel capacity. Yuba–Feather alternatives perform favorably against baseline, subject to their differing assumptions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Multi-metric comparison has identified favorable candidate behavior. **Inputs:** Baseline comparisons, tool compatibility, forecast/model improvement pathway, site constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** Retain a strategy only where benefits and flood-risk outcomes are favorable and implementation can fit USACE practice. **Branches:** A site may need instrumentation/model improvements (Prado); a favorable preliminary result may still require deeper assessment (Yuba–Feather). **Output:** Pilot recommendation or next-stage viability action.

## RP06-D No single optimum: keep a noninferior set

Do not collapse to one score too early; aggregate only when objectives are commensurable. Strength: Established. 11 steps, 7 papers.

### E02-R5: Construct an uncertain Pareto front

**Description:** Because future inflows are uncertain and the two objectives conflict, the authors seek noninferior trade-offs rather than a single deterministic optimum. They use the constraint method in two directions to obtain distributions for both objective values.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Decision makers need alternatives spanning the safety trade-off. **Inputs:** stochastic program, scenario tree, feasible constraints, and a chosen number (K) of fixed constraint values. **Judgment/procedure:** First optimize expected peak discharge with expected highest water level fixed across a range; then optimize expected highest water level with the resulting discharge values constrained. **Branches:** The first stage varies the reservoir-level objective; the second reverses the optimization/constraint roles. **Output:** An expected noninferior set and probability distributions for (Q_{max}) and (Z_{max}).

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Convert the multi-objective formulation to an actionable candidate. **Inputs:** Objective values, constraints, reference/weight information. **Procedure:** Run NSGA-III, retain non-dominated solutions, then select a compromise by AHP-Entropy. **Output:** Pareto front and compromise release/level trajectory.

## RP06-E The final choice depends on preferences and ranking rules

Weights, TOPSIS, fuzzy optimum, or multi-agency consultation, made transparent and testable. Strength: Established. 15 steps, 11 papers.

### E02-R7: Make a preference-sensitive stochastic selection

**Description:** The Pareto set still requires a preferred alternative, but criteria performance values and criteria weights are uncertain. The authors therefore use SMAA-TOPSIS, combining TOPSIS closeness to ideal/anti-ideal points with stochastic sampling, and compare it with SMAA-2.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A release must be selected from multiple noninferior alternatives under uncertain criteria and preferences. **Inputs:** stochastic decision matrix, criteria weights or feasible weight region, and decision-maker preference information. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat (Q_{max}, Z_{max}, P_Q, P_Z,) and end level (Z_e) as cost criteria; use LHS Monte Carlo to estimate rank acceptability and holistic acceptability, then use the latter for final selection. **Branches:** Run without weight information; with deterministic AHP weights; and with uniform or normal uncertain weights. **Output:** Rank probabilities, holistic acceptability, decision-error risk, RUD, and a selected alternative.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.4.1–2.4.4, pp. 8–11; sections 3.3.2–3.3.3, pp. 13–17; Figures 6–9 and Tables 2–3. Three flood-control experts provide AHP preference weights in the case study; the paper does not describe their identities or elicitation protocol beyond this statement.

**Description:** Correlated criteria repeat or interfere with information and can bias evaluation. **Retained details:** **Trigger:** a single operational candidate is needed from a Pareto/noninferior set. **Inputs:** objective matrix and objective weights/preferences. **Judgment/procedure:** normalize benefit/cost objectives, apply weights, calculate ideal/nadir distances, choose highest closeness. **Branches:** selected policy changes with preference weights; the displayed case uses homogeneous weights. **Output:** compromise solution R (RMOO) and C (CCP).

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit p. 7 and p. 11, Eqs. (17)–(22), Table 4. Equal preference is a demonstration, not evidence that all agencies share these preferences.

## RP06-F Formulate objectives to fit the problem

Flood scale, site purpose, and operating stage decide what the objective function contains. Strength: Established. 10 steps, 9 papers.

### E12-R3: Include process resilience in the objective

**Description:** Flood damage depends on magnitude and duration, so minimizing only peak values is incomplete.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Optimize flood-regulating stage. **Inputs:** Downstream flow time series, Qmin/Qmax, reservoir levels. **Judgment/procedure:** Define piecewise system performance and integrate it over the event; maximize resilience while minimizing maximum level and downstream peak. **Output:** Three-objective optimization problem.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.3.1, pp. 4–5, Eqs. (6)–(10). Resilience is a modeled index, not observed damage.

### E14-R6: Match objective complexity to flood scale

**Description:** For small floods, level and release objectives may suffice and risk/resilience indices can be near zero. Large floods require objectives for failure probability, failure duration, and loss severity in both protection areas.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Select optimization formulation for an event. **Inputs:** Flood scale, safety thresholds, scenario set, upstream/downstream priorities. **Judgment:** Use TSO for expected maximum level/release comparison; add five risk/resilience/vulnerability objectives in MRO for large events. **Output:** Objective vector and constraints.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3, 8–10, 20–27, Eqs. (12)–(22). The scale rule is demonstrated with two events and is not validated across many return periods.

### E16-R2: Define a flood-pool objective with drawdown guardrail

**Description:** Required flood pool is the maximum accumulated storage above TOCS, but aggressive releases can draw into conservation storage. A small drawdown penalty prevents policies from releasing maximally without forecast justification.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Compare policies on flood-space requirement while retaining water supply. **Inputs:** Relative storage (S), observed inflow (Q_t), release (R_t), TOCS, horizon H, weight (w). **Judgment:** Penalize positive and negative deviations with (w\in\{0.0001,0.001,0.01,0.1,0.5\}), usually prioritizing exceedance. **Output:** Required flood pool and drawdown trade-off.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2–4, Eqs. (1)–(2). The TOCS offset and (w) are study constructs; actual water-supply value is simplified.

### E17-R3: Define benefit as flood-pool reduction

**Description:** A dynamic release policy can reduce static flood pool while potentially improving conservation storage. Required pool is the maximum accumulated storage above TOCS after actual releases.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Compare candidate parameters/policies. **Inputs:** Actual inflow, simulated storage, TOCS/design flood pool. **Procedure:** Calculate (max(0,X_{t_1:t_n})), then percent reduction relative to actual flood pool; assign 0% when required pool exceeds design pool. **Output:** FP reduction metric.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 6–7, Eq. (7). This is a storage/factor-of-safety proxy and does not model flood damage or all water-supply requirements.

### E18-R3: Match assessment objectives to site characteristics

**Description:** The three pilots were selected and interpreted as distinct cases: water-supply reliability at Lake Mendocino, groundwater recharge at Prado, and flood-risk management at Yuba–Feather, with different reservoir sizes, locations, climatology, and watershed conditions. Therefore, the authors reason that alternatives must be adapted from one pilot to another.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A portfolio contains reservoirs with different authorized purposes and physical/forecast settings. **Inputs:** Site objectives, reservoir/watershed characteristics, downstream controls, existing WCM. **Judgment/procedure:** Define site-specific performance objectives and candidate alternatives. **Branches:** An alternative that works at Lake Mendocino does not directly apply to Prado; Yuba–Feather requires a coordinated two-reservoir system view. **Output:** A tailored assessment design for each pilot.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.1–2.4, pp. 4–8. System-level coordination is described for Yuba–Feather; detailed alternative-construction rules are in cited viability reports and are not reproduced here.

### E20-R7: Separate calibration score from per-event operating objective

**Description:** The RFCM itself minimizes a weighted penalty P for released flows and stored volume in each flood event. The calibration methodology separately scores a parameter set through expected damage over the entire ensemble. This separation lets the method evaluate any parametric RFCM while recognizing that model penalties and risk indices answer different questions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A model's internal optimization objective must be evaluated and calibrated. **Inputs:** RFCM objective/constraints, candidate parameter set, ensemble output distributions, damage functions. **Judgment/procedure:** Do not conflate per-event penalty minimization with the across-event risk criterion; use the latter to calibrate parameters. **Output:** Parameter set selected by Ir, with per-event RFCM operations generated by MILP.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.2–2.4, pp. 5–8 and Appendix A. The article does not provide a formal proof that the chosen calibration criterion is optimal for all RFCM forms.

### E35-R6: Balance flood safety, water supply, and operability

**Description:** The operation needs a single objective combining soft targets, with high penalties for downstream release above 200 m³/s and end-of-event water-supply deviation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** select releases at each optimization. **Inputs:** forebay, spillway release, downstream threshold, variable guide curve, weights. **Judgment/procedure:** penalize low forebay relative to maximum, total spill, squared spill above threshold, high forebay above guide curve, and consecutive release changes; enforce water balance and hard physical limits. **Output:** optimized control trajectory.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 3.3–3.5, pp. 12–13, Eqs. (16)–(27). Weights are case-specific and selected by trial and error.

### E36-R4: Optimize an expected safety-oriented trajectory

**Description:** The operation should account for each scenario while seeking a policy that minimizes expected highest water level.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** scenario tree has been generated. **Inputs:** scenario probabilities, level/release variables, inflow tree, initial and target levels, limits. **Judgment/procedure:** minimize the probability-weighted maximum level over scenarios, subject to balance, level/release/capacity/fluctuation constraints and initial/end conditions. **Output:** scenario-specific optimal operation and water-level maxima.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.2.1–2.2.2, pp. 6–7; Eqs. (10)–(16). The source’s formulation uses scenario-indexed releases; its practical conversion to a single implemented release is not detailed.

### E46-R3: Set target and safety levels by objective priority

**Description:** IL, SL, NL, and DL encode preparation, safety, conservation, and PMF capacity. The authors treat targets as given by decision makers, while optimizing rules to approach them.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** define a stage objective and feasible envelope. **Inputs:** dam safety, flood mitigation, conservation needs, channel conveyance, PMF. **Judgment/procedure:** set target levels and release bounds; preserve space above SL as backup. **Output:** level targets and constraints for optimization.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–3, 6–8, and case data on p. 8. Targets are assumed inputs, not optimized or independently elicited in this paper.

### E46-R4: Choose rule parameters by stage-specific trade-off

**Description:** Each stage has a distinct objective function and weighting factor. The first stage trades level accuracy against smooth release; the second trades peak-release reduction against avoiding unnecessary surcharge above NL; the third minimizes end-of-operation level error.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** multiple feasible rules satisfy safety but differ in smoothness or conservation. **Inputs:** simulated end-stage levels, release changes, maximum releases, penalties, PMF levels. **Judgment/procedure:** vary wI and wII and number of post-peak steps, then inspect empirical target-level coverage and maxima. **Output:** selected wI = 0.2, wII = 2, and l = 2 for the case.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–7 and 8–11, Tables 2, 4, and 6. Weight selection is case-based; no formal uncertainty quantification is reported.

## EP08-A Build the criteria system and decision matrix

**Description:** They map each alternative to five cost criteria: peak release, maximum level, downstream-flooding risk, overtopping risk, and end water level.

### E47-E3: Construct BP training samples

**Description:** They calculate ideal and anti-ideal alternatives, sample criteria between them with one shared uniform random number per sample, add ideal and anti-ideal points, calculate Euclidean distances and closeness coefficients, and normalize inputs to [0,1].

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** train a nonlinear evaluator without external labels. **Inputs:** alternative criterion matrix, benefit/cost directions, k random samples. **Procedure:** apply Equations (1)–(6); use 30 samples for the case. **Output:** normalized 10-input samples and closeness targets.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** approximate the criterion-to-closeness mapping. **Inputs:** normalized training samples and targets. **Procedure:** update weights by back-propagation until tolerance or cycle limit; forecast closeness for verification samples. **Output:** trained weights and fitted mapping.

### E47-E5: Calculate importance and sensitivity

**Description:** From trained weights, they calculate mean relative importance Rj over training cycles. They then perturb each criterion one at a time by nine relative changes and calculate relative contribution ratio Gj from the +20% closeness change.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** quantify inherent influence and response sensitivity. **Inputs:** trained network, original criterion vector, perturbation grid, repeated weight estimates. **Procedure:** apply Equations (12)–(15), rank criteria, and plot sensitivity curves. **Output:** Rj, Gj, and rankings.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–6 and 10–11, Table 3 and Figure 4. The sensitivity method ignores joint perturbations and network uncertainty.

### E47-E6: Apply comprehensive discrimination threshold

**Description:** They calculate Fj = Rj Gj and Pj = log10(Fj/mean Fj). Criteria with Pj < -1 are deleted; Pj > 0 indicates above-average order. For Pubugou, Zch-Zmax, Qf, Zf, Wex, and Qmax are selected, while Ze-Zid, Wab, T, Sl, and Qsd are deleted.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 6–7 and 10–12, Table 3. The paper recommends lowering the threshold stepwise until MCDM rankings change in actual applications.

**Description:** After routing, they calculate 12 cost criteria: Zmax, Ze, and Rv for each of the three reservoirs, plus Qmax, T, and W for Leshan. These values form the alternative-by-criterion matrix.

## EP08-C Select a compromise from the noninferior set

Use TOPSIS, fuzzy optimum, or minimum composite risk to choose one plan. Strength: Established. 8 steps, 7 papers.

### E02-E4: Trace the uncertain Pareto front

**Description:** They generate ten noninferior alternatives by applying the two-step constraint method to the two objectives.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A feasible multiobjective stochastic model produces competing solutions. **Inputs:** solved model, lower/upper objective bounds, and (K=10) fixed constraint values. **Procedure:** Optimize expected peak discharge while stepping the expected maximum level constraint, then optimize expected maximum level while constraining the corresponding discharge values. **Output:** Ten alternatives with distributions for (Q_{max}) and (Z_{max}).

**Evidence boundary:** Figure 5 displays the time-period-1 uncertain Pareto front. Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests are reported as accepting the Gaussian assumption for (Q_{max}) and (Z_{max}) in that case. Sections 2.2.3 and 3.3.2, pp. 6–7 and 14.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.1.2–2.1.3 and §4, pp. 4–5, 7, 10–14; Eq. (17). The equal-weight choice is not stakeholder-validated and is not necessarily appropriate for every reservoir.

**Description:** The non-dominated combinations of Iq and Iv are identified using the Pareto dominance approach; among Pareto points the balanced solution is the one closest to the origin.

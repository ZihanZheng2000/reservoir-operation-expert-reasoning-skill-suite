# Evidence for unit 3.2-feasibility

Paper steps assigned to each small pattern of this unit, with their full card text. Step IDs: Exx = paper, Rn = reasoning step, En = execution step.

## RP05-A Hard limits of facilities and operation

Level, storage, outlet capacity, release change, and ramping limits exclude impossible actions. Strength: Established. 8 steps, 8 papers.

### E02-R4: Preserve physical and operational feasibility

**Description:** The authors constrain each scenario trajectory so that candidate releases respect mass balance, water-level limits, release limits, release-capacity limits, release-fluctuation limits, and initial/end conditions. The end water level is described as a soft constraint because storage should be restored for possible subsequent floods but may not be strictly attained.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A stochastic objective solution must still be physically operable. **Inputs:** initial storage/level, scenario inflows, reservoir and outlet curves, time-dependent bounds, and target end level. **Judgment/procedure:** Enforce hard operating bounds and identify the terminal target as generally soft. **Branches:** Time-dependent limits can vary through the flood season. **Output:** Feasible scenario-dependent release and level trajectories.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2.2, equations (8)–(13), pp. 6–7. The text does not report a separate feasibility-repair or fallback procedure.

### E15-R6: Choose constrained evolutionary search

**Description:** The problem is non-linear, constrained, and many-objective; RSEA is selected to improve population diversity while maintaining convergence. Hard constraints must always hold, whereas output constraints can be ranked by violation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Solve the encoded HPFOR optimization. **Inputs:** Candidate parameter vectors, objective values, constraint violations. **Judgment/procedure:** Use region-associated subproblems, weights/neighborhoods, ideal/nadir normalization, reproduction, and population updates; dynamically repair rigid constraints and select lower violation for flexible ones. **Output:** Optimized parameter population and Pareto set.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 10–13, Algorithm 1, Table 2. RSEA superiority is relative to the tested algorithms and this problem; no global optimality is established.

### E22-R4: Bind optimization to physical and operational constraints

**Description:** Candidate policies must remain viable for actual Dez operation. Water balance, minimum/maximum release and storage, and consecutive-release fluctuation limits constrain the search; the fluctuation limit is intended to prevent downstream-channel damage.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A multiobjective optimum may be physically infeasible or unsafe. **Inputs:** Previous/current storage and inflow, release bounds, storage bounds, area-volume-elevation curve, rating curve, release-change limit. **Judgment/procedure:** Reject or constrain policies that violate Eqs. (9)–(12), including 6-hour balance intervals and α=1665 m³/s. **Output:** Feasible release/storage trajectories.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.4.2 and p. 8. Numerical settings include Qmax=6844.5 m³/s, Smin=415 MCM, Smax=2698.5 MCM, and critical level 352 m. Full operational procedures and uncertainty margins are not reported.

### E35-R5: Resolve uncertainty through a scenario tree

**Description:** A fully shared average control can become infeasible in a storage-limited reservoir, while independent controls for all ensemble members are not implementable.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** optimize over ensemble forecasts with hard storage/release constraints. **Inputs:** PSF ensemble, probabilities, forecast horizon, tree-reduction settings. **Judgment/procedure:** partition scenarios at branch points so disturbances, states, and controls share a history before branching; preserve the probability-weighted mass of the original ensemble. **Output:** reduced tree and adaptive multistage decision structure.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.3, p. 7; Section 4.3, pp. 14–16; Fig. 6. The mass-conservative algorithm is referenced to prior work; implementation details are not fully reproduced.

### E37-R8: Prefer gradual correction when a jump is unsafe

**Description:** A single instantaneous release change can itself be operationally problematic, so the paper presents a multi-step correction based on updated forecasts.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** significant forecast error with remaining forecast lead-time. **Inputs:** error category, corrected forecast, current release, CO/FBO mode, allowable change. **Judgment/procedure:** either change discharge at once and return to CO, or adjust gradually over multiple steps while maintaining FBO; multi-step is preferred when a jump is large. **Output:** corrected release trajectory.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 3.5, pp. 11–12; Fig. 7. The remedy is explicitly preliminary/conceptual and lacks a fixed model for all situations.

### E46-R5: Preserve safety under extreme events and constraints

**Description:** PMF simulations and constraints prevent overtopping while historical events tune mitigation and recovery. Release rules are bounded by channel capacity, monotonicity, outlet limits, and Taiwan operating regulations.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** candidate rule could exceed SL/DL or induce a release hazard. **Inputs:** PMF/historical inflows, current levels, outlet capacities, legal limits. **Judgment/procedure:** penalize or exclude unsafe trajectories; use barrier functions for non-bound constraints. **Output:** feasible conservative rules.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–7, Equations (8)–(10), (19), and case setup pp. 8–10. The rules remain model-calibrated; deployment authorization is not studied.

### E48-R3: Generate feasible alternatives under event-specific operational logic

**Description:** A nearly hundred-year flood and small Houziyan flood-control capacity motivate restricting Houziyan to discharge-capacity operation to avoid dam-failure risk. Shuangjiangkou is allowed discharge-capacity or maximum-peak-reduction operation; Pubugou is allowed discharge-capacity, regular, or maximum-peak-reduction operation. These combinations yield six feasible alternatives.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** the event and reservoir characteristics constrain admissible operation models. **Inputs:** event inflows, reservoir capacity, relative safety priorities, operation-model choices. **Judgment/procedure:** assign model options sequentially from upstream to downstream and enumerate admissible combinations. **Branches:** reservoir-specific model availability; Houziyan is restricted. **Output:** six feasible cascade alternatives.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit p. 8 and Table 2. The text says the sequence follows importance weights between reservoir and downstream safety but does not provide the numerical weights.

### E50-R3: Preserve physical and downstream safety constraints

**Description:** A policy that raises storage but exceeds safe release, ramping, or capacity limits would be operationally unacceptable. The baseline and FIRO models therefore retain maximum safe release, ramping, capacity, and TOCS constraints.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** candidate policy changes storage/release decisions. **Inputs:** mass balance, safe release, ramping rates, reservoir capacity, TOCS. **Judgment/procedure:** constrain or penalize infeasible releases and prevent unrealistic daily changes. **Branches:** observed-record estimates stand in for infrastructure/ramping limits. **Output:** feasible or penalized policy trajectories.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 3–5 and Discussion. Maximum release and ramping assumptions are empirical and may not match actual infrastructure/policies.

## RP05-B Is there enough time

Can drawdown or prerelease finish within the lead time; longer lead time permits a higher ceiling. Strength: Established. 8 steps, 7 papers.

### E01-R3: Use lead time and release capacity to define an admissible upper level

**Description:** The reservoir must be able to lower its level to the current FLWL before a large inflow arrives. Therefore, the upper bound is linked to effective forecast lead time, downstream safety discharge, and reservoir release capacity.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A candidate upper level could prevent timely drawdown. **Inputs:** Candidate storage (V_u), current-FLWL storage (V_0), future inflow over (T_c), forecast lead time (T_c), and downstream safety discharge (Q_s). **Judgment/procedure:** Use the capacity-constrained mass-balance relation in Eq. (5) to limit the candidate upper level. **Branches:** The level may be allowed above the current FLWL only when pre-release capacity can restore the lower level in time. **Output:** A physically feasible upper-bound candidate.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2, p. 126, Eq. (5). The precise operational scheduling of releases within the lead time is not fully specified beyond the module and case constraints.

### E07-R4: Resolve excess risk with a constrained prerelease

**Description:** When the risk test fails, the authors reason that only enough forecast members need to be brought below the threshold to meet the tolerance. They select the lowest-ranked members among those exceeding the threshold, derive release schedules for those members, and retain the most demanding first-day release so all forecast-day risk tests are met while respecting physical and downstream limits.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** For any forecast day, (r_t>R_t). **Inputs:** Excess members, their forecast storage exceedance, forecast horizon, release limits, Hopland 8,000 cfs limit, and ramping constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** Select the smallest set of excess members needed to meet tolerance; distribute each member's volume difference across days through the forecast day; cap infeasible schedule entries at the forecast release limit and redistribute the adjusted volume to preceding days; take the maximum day-1 release over selected members and forecast days. **Branches:** No excess risk means no schedule for that forecast day; a schedule above a limit is adjusted and redistributed. **Output:** A current-day flood-control release that reduces forecasted risk to tolerance.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit computational logic on pp. 9–12, Sections 3.3.1 and Figs. 5–9. The 8 February 1986 example reports 14/61 members above threshold at day 8, (r_8=23.0\%), (R_8=9.8\%), 8 risky members selected, and a 529 cfs day-1 release for that horizon; the all-horizon maximum in the example is 1,240 cfs at day 14. The paper does not provide a formal optimality proof for the heuristic schedule.

### E09-R4: Stress-test forecast failure and capacity

**Description:** A forecast failure with the next flood arriving immediately is the adverse case for safe release. If all stored water cannot be removed within τ, retaining it would increase flood risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Dry/delayed-flood assumption fails. **Inputs:** ΔWtotal, qA, g(Z), qfl, τ. **Judgment/procedure:** Release at min(qA,g(Z)); compare required time t with τ; deduct unreleased volume from ΔWtotal to obtain ΔWsafe. **Branches:** If t≤τ, no added original risk under the model; if t>τ, reduce safe volume. **Output:** Safe added storage and quantified burden.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.4, 3.4, pp. 6–7, 11–13, Tables 5–6. All 50 tested scenarios exceeded lead time in the case.

### E17-R4: Vary the parameters that govern pre-release feasibility

**Description:** Maximum release, ramping rate, and lead time determine whether a policy can ramp releases early enough. Normalizing release/ramping by peak inflow allows comparison across sites.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Explain heterogeneous MPC performance. **Inputs:** (R_{max}), (RR), (LT), event peak inflow, site flood pool. **Judgment:** Sample these three factors globally; treat infrastructure/forecast properties as screening variables. **Output:** Parameter-response surface.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–7. Ranges are chosen for cross-site consistency and do not represent engineering feasibility at every site.

### E19-R4: Convert PP into an operational water-level bound

**Description:** PP is physically interpretable but not sufficiently intuitive for operation because it lacks a fixed reservoir threshold. The authors therefore transform PP into potential floodwater volume and then into VCWL, the theoretical upper water-level limit compatible with a specified prerelease sequence.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** PP is available but operators need a level target/bound. **Inputs:** PP, runoff transformation, maximum water level, current inflow/outflow, prerelease sequence, forecast periods. **Judgment/procedure:** Use Eq. (8) to convert PP to floodwater via a runoff coefficient or hydrological model, then Eq. (9) to derive VCWL from storage changes. **Branches:** Use conservative runoff coefficient 0.75 or Xinanjiang model; use VCWL trend to decide whether higher levels can be retained or drawdown is required. **Output:** Time-varying VCWL and an operational water-level constraint.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.2.2, pp. 5 and Appendix A, p. 12, Eqs. (8)–(9). Exact dimensions/notation in Eq. (8) are partly damaged by OCR; implementation parameters beyond the reported coefficient/model are not fully given.

### E19-R6: Establish a common capacity-constrained FLWL upper bound

**Description:** Both FIRO schemes need an upper bound because the reservoir must be able to return to FLWL within the forecast horizon. The capacity-constrained prerelease formula uses forecast horizon, inflow, and maximum prerelease outflow; the authors select 66 m from Table 3 for the case.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Comparing FIRO schemes and setting an initial flood-regulation level. **Inputs:** FLWL storage, forecast lead time, inflow, maximum prerelease outflow, time interval, reservoir storage-elevation relation. **Judgment/procedure:** Compute candidate upper bounds with Eq. (7), inspect discrete Table 3 combinations, and choose 66 m because the reservoir can complete prerelease within 48 h for the stated inflow/outflow range. **Branches:** Bound changes with inflow and release capacity; reported selection is case-specific. **Output:** Initial water level/upper bound for both FIRO schemes.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.2.1, p. 4, §4.2, pp. 6–7, Eq. (7), and Table 3. The exact operational approval/risk criterion behind choosing 66 m is not separately reported.

### E29-R4: Set dynamic bounds to reflect flood timing and lead time

**Description:** Segmentation captures different inflow regimes; longer lead time provides more prerelease opportunity, so the upper bound can be higher without the modeled increase in flood-control risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** defining a real-time FLWL policy for the flood season. **Inputs:** flood-season segmentation, lead time, risk constraint, current operation/design levels. **Judgment/procedure:** use Scheme I with preflood/main/postflood bounds or Scheme II with one whole-season pair; increase upper bounds with lead time. **Branches:** lower bound is 145 m in the schemes; Scheme I has distinct subperiod bounds. **Output:** bound table for the operation model.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–5, Table 1. The bounds are taken from the earlier FLWL model; this paper does not rederive them.

### E35-R2: Establish a horizon reference under perfect information

**Description:** Before testing imperfect forecasts, the authors determine the minimum horizon that can release early enough under ideal information.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** choose a useful forecast horizon. **Inputs:** 100-year flood hydrograph, initial high forebay level, candidate horizons 6–48 h. **Judgment/procedure:** compare closed-loop peak releases and forebay traces; select a horizon at which responses stabilize and remain below 200 m³/s. **Branches:** horizons 6/12 h delay release; 18 h or longer give similar safe responses. **Output:** evidence that forecast horizon affects control even without forecast error.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 4.1, pp. 13–14; Fig. 7. The “approximately infinite horizon” interpretation is an assumption from the cited MPC rationale, not a proof.

## RP05-C Is there enough storage space

Can remaining storage absorb the design flood, and can released water be refilled. Strength: Established. 9 steps, 8 papers.

### E05-R3: Define feasible storage–safety allocations before optimizing

**Description:** The stage-2 mass balance gives (W_1+d=RFCC), so residual flood-conveyance capacity is allocated between conservation storage and safety margin. The feasible region must also satisfy downstream flow, storage, and acceptable-risk requirements.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A candidate allocation must be physically and risk feasible. **Inputs:** (R_{2,max}), expected inflow ̄(I_2), (R_{2,min}), (W_{max}), and (d_{min}=rU^{-1}(1-r_a)). **Judgment/procedure:** Define point A by (d=d_{min}), D by (d=d_{max}=R_{2,max}-R_{2,min}), and bounds (0le W_1lemin(W_{max},RFCC-d_{min})). Determine the DC-FLWL inflow interval from (RFCC_A) to (RFCC_D). **Branches:** Large inflow makes flood control dominant; small inflow makes water supply dominant; increasing (r) shrinks the feasible zone, while increasing (r_a) expands it. **Output:** Feasible zone ABCD and candidate start/end segments for hedging curves.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2–3.1, pp. 4–6; Figures 3–4; equations (3), (4), (7). The normal-error formula and DC-FLWL interpretation are model assumptions.

### E09-R3: Quantify allowable added storage

**Description:** Water above FLWL is decomposed into utilization-period and flood-control-period volumes. Their sum depends on dry duration, inflows, discharge, safety margin, and lead time.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Construct an operating scenario. **Inputs:** d, τ, qm(t), qin(t), qfl(t), downstream safety qA, discount β, discharge capacity g(Z). **Judgment/procedure:** Compute ΔWFU, ΔWFC, qc=min(βqA,g(Z)), and ΔWtotal. **Branches:** Vary d and τ; longer values increase planned storage in this case. **Output:** Planned over-FLWL volume and corresponding level.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.3, 3.3, Eqs. (7)–(10), pp. 6, 10–11. These are modeled volumes, not authorization limits without further operational checks.

### E11-R3: Derive period-specific D-PSL

**Description:** Storage must support the design-period inflow after accounting for releasable volume and reservoir capacity.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** For each period forecast. **Inputs:** Capacity K, maximum safe release volume rts, forecast/design flood volume. **Judgment/procedure:** Compute S0,ts=K+rts−fdes,ts and express as water level; keep level no higher than D-PSLts. **Output:** Candidate D-PSL for each period.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.2, pp. 6–8, Eqs. (6)–(9). Detailed notation is supplied in the text; operational gate behavior is not.

### E11-R4: Select controlling period

**Description:** A single forecast period may miss a controlling accumulation pattern.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Candidate levels from several windows available. **Inputs:** D-PSL1d, D-PSL2d, D-PSL3d, D-PSL5d. **Judgment/procedure:** Choose the minimum candidate to satisfy all considered periods. **Output:** Event-specific D-PSL.

**Evidence boundary:** §2.2, pp. 7–8, Eq. (8). Conservative minimum is paper-specific.

### E12-R1: Define forecast-informed pre-release decision

**Description:** Pre-release increases flood space but its uncertainty creates conservation risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Forecast inflow is in early-flood discriminant range Qdown–Qup. **Inputs:** Forecast inflow, current storage, FLWL, minimum downstream demand. **Judgment/procedure:** Release now while requiring future inflow plus minimum-demand balance to restore FLWL if no flood occurs. **Branches:** Pre-release versus later detention when forecast exceeds Qup; potential restore if no flood. **Output:** Feasible early release.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.1–2.2, pp. 2–4, Eqs. (1)–(5). Exact Qdown/Qup calibration is case-specific.

### E15-R3: Translate forecast uncertainty into a safety bound

**Description:** The forecast-stage release distribution follows from mass balance and the requirement that end storage equals the flood-limit storage. The acceptable risk rate (r_a) determines the percentile of forecast inflow that the current decision must accommodate.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Set current decision release with uncertain (I_f). **Inputs:** Current (V_t,I_t), forecast density (P_I), (T_f), (R_{lim}), (V_{lim}), (r_a). **Procedure:** Derive (V_{t+1}\le V_{lim}+R_{lim}T_f\Delta t-operatorname{Per}(P_I,1-r_a)T_f\Delta t) and a corresponding interval for (R_t). **Output:** Risk-aware storage/release bounds.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–7, Eqs. (3)–(16). Direction of (r_a) is explicit: smaller (r_a) lowers storage and raises discharge; variance in (P_I) increases its influence.

### E16-R7: Bound water-supply and feasibility conclusions

**Description:** Low drawdown weight is attractive only if remaining inflow can refill the reservoir. Release constraints and authority to draw into conservation storage determine whether the simulated benefit is feasible.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Decide whether a low flood-pool policy is operationally acceptable. **Inputs:** Maximum drawdown, remaining water-year inflow, refill date June 1, release/ramping assumptions, agency authority. **Judgment:** Compare drawdown against refill opportunity and disclose physical/political constraints. **Output:** Site-specific applicability boundary.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 8–10. Refill test is simplified and Oroville-specific; no downstream delivery/environmental constraints are included.

### E24-R6: Convert allocation into operational targets and boundary actions

**Description:** Optimal EFSM determines expected downstream flow targets, reservoir releases after subtracting intermediate flow, and carryover storage. If storage would be below 0 or above K, capacity constraints replace the unconstrained allocation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** implement a class-specific hedging decision. **Inputs:** δ1/δ2, expected upstream/intermediate flows, qthres, initial storage, capacity K. **Judgment/procedure:** set r̄1=qthres−δ1 and r̄2=Tqthres−δ2; schedule r̄u,t=r̄t−q̄d,t; compute s1 and clip/resolve at s1=0 or K using Eqs. (28)–(29). **Branches:** unconstrained; lower-storage bound; upper-storage bound; negative Δ/extreme flood invokes storage and evacuation rationale. **Output:** stage targets, release schedule, carryover storage.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 04014041-6–7, Eqs. (25)–(29). The paper does not specify real-time authorization, gate operations, or emergency fallback implementation.

### E25-R3: Determine whether hedging can affect forecasted storms

**Description:** Storage and release mechanics imply three categories: small storms fit safe capacity, large storms can have reduced release after hedging, and overwhelming storms remain certain failures. Thus large-storm probability is necessary for useful hedging.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** assess candidate forecast ensemble. **Inputs:** Vsi, tsi, k, S0, Qh, Qa, qlb/qub. **Judgment/procedure:** compare storm release against qlb/qub with and without Qh using category boundary volumes. **Branches:** small → no future-risk reduction; large → hedging potentially valuable; overwhelming → pre-release cannot avoid storm failure. **Output:** category counts a, b, n−a−b and reduced cost form.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5117–5119, Sections 2.3, Figs. 3–4, Eqs. (7)–(8). Boundaries depend on square-wave and storage assumptions.

## RP05-D Downstream and system conditions limit local action

Travel time, channel storage, and uncontrolled local inflow mean locally feasible is not system-feasible. Strength: Provisional. 5 steps, 2 papers.

### E43-R4: Set the prediction and safety envelope

**Description:** A 42 m Taungoo safety level is used below the 43 m danger level to retain buffer capacity. A 30-minute simulation step, 3-hour control step, and two-day horizon are selected to capture the approximately 41-hour travel time from the furthest reservoir and available forecast length.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** controller requires a safety target and horizon. **Inputs:** danger level, travel time, forecast availability, computation time. **Judgment/procedure:** leave a buffer below the danger level and choose a horizon long enough for system delay but short enough to remain computable. **Branches:** longer horizons may increase computation without improving performance. **Output:** safety and timing settings.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 7–8 and 13, Sections 3 and 5. These are study settings; no systematic horizon calibration is reported.

### E43-R5: Interpret residual risk and model limits

**Description:** MPC can reduce peaks but cannot eliminate violations caused by uncontrolled catchments, full storage, and conduit limits. The authors therefore avoid treating the simulated improvement as universal and recommend ensemble forecasts, measurement-noise treatment, and multiobjective methods for future work.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** simulated safety level is still exceeded. **Inputs:** controlled versus uncontrolled inflows, storage and outlet capacities, forecast assumptions. **Judgment/procedure:** attribute residual risk to physical and information limits rather than controller failure alone. **Branches:** deterministic forecasts versus ensemble forecasts; weighted versus Pareto multiobjective control. **Output:** bounded interpretation and improvement agenda.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 11–14, Figures 5–6, Discussion. The paper does not report real-time deployment or independent field validation.

### E44-R3: Represent channel storage explicitly

**Description:** Flood routing changes storage and timing, so each channel segment is conceptualized as a channel storage reservoir (CSR) using Muskingum storage. This avoids treating channel routing as an unmodeled gap in the aggregate.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** a channel segment lies between reservoirs and a control station. **Inputs:** Muskingum K and x, channel inflow/outflow, reservoir storages. **Judgment/procedure:** calculate CSR storage and add it to reservoir storage; use conservative estimates when precise hydraulic storage is unavailable. **Branches:** CSR may be omitted for comparison. **Output:** CSR-aware aggregate storage and boundaries.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3–5, Equations (1)–(5), Figure 2. Authors note that hydraulic models could improve CSR estimates (p. 10).

### E44-R4: Link pre-discharge storage to flood risk

**Description:** Risk depends on storage remaining after pre-discharge and on the allocation of that storage among reservoirs. A two-stage risk analysis using forecast-derived end storage is used; conservative bias from forecast error is accepted as safety-oriented.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** forecast inflow exceeds a flood-event standard. **Inputs:** forecast inflow, safety outflow, lead time, aggregate storage, flood frequency. **Judgment/procedure:** draw down reservoir and then last-CSR storage; estimate risk as a probability-weighted relationship between end storage and flood risk. **Branches:** CSR-aware and CSR-free pre-discharge rules; aggregated risk uses the allocation producing the largest risk for a given storage/frequency. **Output:** maximum permissible pre-discharge storage and risk constraints.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–7, Equations (6)–(12). The Gaussian unbiased forecast assumption and conservative interpretation are stated; actual forecast bias is not independently verified.

### E44-R5: Trade hydropower against safety and refill

**Description:** The upper WLFS boundaries are decision variables. They are raised where this improves annual hydropower, but only while aggregate flood-risk constraints, refill constraints, and reservoir physical limits remain satisfied.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** decompose aggregate storage into individual reservoir operation. **Inputs:** individual inflows, storage/release/power limits, risk-derived aggregate storage, refill target. **Judgment/procedure:** optimize annual average generation under constraints; use standard operating policy to evaluate generation. **Branches:** CSR-aware versus CSR-free and empirical versus optimized boundaries. **Output:** individual upper/lower WLFS boundaries.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 7–8, Equations (13)–(22), Figure 1. Optimization algorithm details are not specified in the supplied text.

## RP05-E Model feasibility is not implementability

Regulations, authority, standard tools, data, computing time, and departmental approval. Strength: Established. 10 steps, 6 papers.

### E17-R1: Frame a cross-site screening problem

**Description:** FIRO benefits depend on reservoir infrastructure, forecasts, and control formulation, so single-site demonstrations do not identify where FIRO is promising.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Assess FIRO transferability across reservoirs. **Inputs:** Site capacity/flood pools, release/ramping limits, forecast horizons/ensembles. **Judgment:** Evaluate a broad parameter space with a common control method. **Output:** Global sensitivity and threshold-screening objective.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–3. The authors explicitly target screening; the selected six sites and event record define the evidence boundary.

### E18-R2: Constrain the candidate approach to USACE practice

**Description:** Real-time USACE reservoir operations are nearly exclusively based on rule curves because of federal laws, statutes, regulations, and guidance. HEC-ResSim is required for USACE water-management teams, so a usable FIRO strategy must accommodate this institutional and tooling paradigm.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A proposed operational adjustment is intended for real-world USACE use. **Inputs:** Legal/regulatory constraints, rule-curve practice, required CWMS/HEC-ResSim environment. **Judgment/procedure:** Prefer an approach that can be represented and evaluated within the existing decision framework. **Branches:** Mathematical optimization is acknowledged as prior work, but this paper does not select it as its implementation focus. **Output:** USACE-compatible FIRO alternatives and simulations.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §1, p. 2. The paper does not specify all legal constraints or a formal method for resolving conflicts among them.

### E18-R4: Treat forecast skill and lead time as decision prerequisites

**Description:** FIRO depends on forecast information that is useful before the relevant phenomenon occurs. In the screening framework, forecast skill at a sufficient lead time is identified as a critical component, and poor forecast skill is an example of a Stage A exclusion condition.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Considering whether forecast-informed retention/release can be trusted for a site. **Inputs:** Forecast skill, lead time, precipitation/streamflow products, timing of flood-producing events. **Judgment/procedure:** Ask whether forecast skill is sufficient to support the intended operational flexibility. **Branches:** Poor skill can eliminate a site at Stage A; better skill may permit deeper assessment. **Output:** Forecast-suitability requirement passed to viability/screening.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.1.1, p. 9; forecast lead time is defined there by citation to AMS. The paper does not give a universal numerical skill or lead-time threshold.

### E18-R7: Select an implementable pilot strategy when performance is favorable

**Description:** For Lake Mendocino, the selected approach both improves water supply reliability/flood protection outcomes and can be implemented with standard USACE decision tools while allowing growth as forecast skill and models improve. Prado results indicate higher buffer pools improve recharge and FIRO slightly lowers emergency spillway flow and releases over downstream channel capacity. Yuba–Feather alternatives perform favorably against baseline, subject to their differing assumptions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Multi-metric comparison has identified favorable candidate behavior. **Inputs:** Baseline comparisons, tool compatibility, forecast/model improvement pathway, site constraints. **Judgment/procedure:** Retain a strategy only where benefits and flood-risk outcomes are favorable and implementation can fit USACE practice. **Branches:** A site may need instrumentation/model improvements (Prado); a favorable preliminary result may still require deeper assessment (Yuba–Feather). **Output:** Pilot recommendation or next-stage viability action.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 4–8. Selection criteria are described qualitatively; no formal optimization or acceptance rule is supplied.

### E18-R8: Scale from pilots through staged portfolio screening

**Description:** Assessing every one of more than 400 USACE WCM-governed reservoirs by the full pilot process would take decades. The authors therefore reason that a scalable, adaptable screening process should first remove sites with little potential, then score remaining sites by benefit and implementation effort, and finally engage prioritized offices in deeper assessment.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Portfolio-scale prioritization under limited time/resources. **Inputs:** Site eligibility, forecast skill, outlet controllability, existence of WCM/WCP, expected benefits/challenges, implementation effort, local-office knowledge. **Judgment/procedure:** Apply Stage A elimination, Stage B scored suitability/benefit-effort assessment, then Stage C detailed assessment and dialogue. **Branches:** Stage C can route to no implementation because benefits are minimal, more forecast research, full viability assessment, or direct WCM update where suitability is high. **Output:** Prioritized site and route recommendation.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§3–3.1.1, pp. 8–10; Figure 10 is cited. Exact scoring weights and Stage C decision thresholds are not reported in the paper.

### E18-R9: Codify and future-proof suitable adjustments in WCMs

**Description:** FIRO implementation at USACE sites is accomplished by updating the WCM, in coordination with division/district water-management teams and project teams. FIRO 2.0 adds adaptive WCP language and a forecast-skill assessment so future forecast/model improvements enter decisions only after improvement is confirmed sufficient.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A site has demonstrated or is assessing FIRO viability and an authorized operating plan can be updated. **Inputs:** Viability evidence, legal/regulatory process, forecast-skill assessment, proposed WCP language, stakeholder coordination. **Judgment/procedure:** Decide whether and how to codify flexibility, and condition future tool use on verified improvement. **Branches:** FIRO may not currently be viable at all reservoirs; some sites need more research before WCM revision. **Output:** Updated WCM/WCP or a deferred implementation/research recommendation.

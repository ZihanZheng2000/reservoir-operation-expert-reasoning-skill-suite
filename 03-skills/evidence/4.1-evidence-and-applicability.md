# Evidence for unit 4.1-evidence-and-applicability

Paper steps assigned to each small pattern of this unit, with their full card text. Step IDs: Exx = paper, Rn = reasoning step, En = execution step.

## RP09-A Improvement must be compared with the right baseline

Use current rule, no-forecast, and perfect-forecast comparators to isolate the true contribution. Strength: Established. 18 steps, 15 papers.

### E01-R8: Interpret gains only after checking case constraints and the baseline comparison

**Description:** The authors compare dynamic-control schemes against the current operation rule after imposing the Shashi downstream-stage and TGR drawdown constraints. The reported conclusion is conditional: TGR simulations show higher utilization and generation without increasing the modeled flood-control risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A candidate scheme has passed the risk condition. **Inputs:** 1-day/2-day forecast scenarios, uncertainty on/off scenarios, baseline operation, downstream stage constraint, utilization, spill, and generation outputs. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare schemes in Table 5 and retain the risk-preserving interpretation. **Branches:** Results differ when forecast errors are neglected versus incorporated and for 1-day versus 2-day lead time. **Output:** Case-bounded performance conclusion and planning/design recommendation.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 3.4.1 and 4–5, pp. 130–131; Tables 4–5. The authors explicitly state that real-time implementation remains a future challenge.

### E04-R7: Define the operational threshold and comparison route

**Description:** To assess whether uncertainty changes flood risk, the study links runoff generation, reservoir flood routing, and risk analysis. It uses TGR flood-control criteria and a 10,000-year design flood associated with the 180.4 m upper threshold, then compares forecast-informed operation against a reference using observed data and regular routing.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Synthetic forecast inflows are available for the flood-season risk experiment. **Inputs:** FCWL 145 m, Jingjiang/Shashi stage criterion 44.5 m, release constraints, design-flow threshold, and routing policy. **Judgment/procedure:** Evaluate maximum storage/water level exceedance probability under the pre-release versus regular-routing setup. **Branches:** Release caps apply at 53,900 m³/s for Jingjiang protection, 79,000 m³/s for specified higher reservoir levels, and release capacity determines release above 180.4 m. **Output:** A case-specific modeled flood-risk comparison.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.3 and 3.4, pp. 717–719 and 724–725; Eqs. (14)–(15), Table 2. Threshold and release rules are study settings; the paper does not claim an authorization procedure for live operators.

### E07-R7: Judge viability against operationally meaningful comparators

**Description:** The authors reason that EFO must be assessed against current practice and bounded by a perfect-forecast case. EO provides the current water-control-manual baseline, while PFO uses actual future unimpaired flows and 0% tolerance to represent a hypothetical maximum benefit.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Evaluate whether adaptive forecasting improves storage without increasing flood risk. **Inputs:** Common hydrology, transfers, losses, water-supply rules, release/ramping constraints, and uncontrolled-spillway rules. **Judgment/procedure:** Hold shared assumptions across EFO, PFO, and EO and compare storage, releases, spillway behavior, and downstream flows. **Branches:** EFO uses uncertain ensembles; PFO uses actual future flows; EO follows seasonal guide-curve rules. **Output:** Comparative evidence and a bound on potential forecast-skill benefit.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit on pp. 8 and 13, Sections 3.3–3.3.3. PFO is labeled unrealistic and hypothetical; comparisons do not constitute independent validation.

### E10-R6: Evaluate by safety/utilization trade-off

**Description:** Compare FCC exceedance and generation across operation rules and perturbations to determine where stage-aware forecasts help.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Validate proposed rule in case simulation. **Inputs:** 12 floods, COR, Two-OR, forecast-error scenarios, RFH lengths, amplified floods. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare peak/volume types, error differences, RFH extension, and baseline rules. **Output:** Case-bounded performance findings.

**Evidence boundary:** §§3–5, pp. 7–11. No independent deployment validation.

### E12-R5: Check safety and transfer limits

**Description:** A chosen rule must be safe under forecast errors and show benefit versus current/traditional approaches.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Validate recommendation. **Inputs:** Nierji design floods, observed 1969/2013 hydrographs, DBM forecasts, ±22%/19% errors, baselines. **Judgment/procedure:** Simulate level, downstream flow, flood duration/resilience and compare frameworks. **Output:** Case-bounded rule assessment.

**Evidence boundary:** §§3–5, pp. 7–12. Single-reservoir scope and multi-reservoir limitations are explicit.

### E14-R5: Evaluate forecast quality and forecast value separately

**Description:** An optimization can look good under forecasted inflows but be “fake” when judged against actual inflows. Thus forecast metrics, optimization objectives, and post-assessment under Perfect observed inflows are separate evidence layers.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Compare deterministic, ensemble, and operational performance. **Inputs:** Forecast/observation metrics, candidate release trajectories, observed inflows. **Judgment:** Do not infer operational value from forecast skill alone; use Perfect inflows for post-assessment. **Output:** Forecast-quality and forecast-value comparisons.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2, 12–17. “Perfect” is a simulated observed-inflow benchmark, not a deployable policy or true future-information condition.

### E16-R3: Compare distinct policy information pathways

**Description:** EFO, MPC, and cumulative control differ in flexibility, optimization timing, horizon, and use of ensemble information; benchmarks establish lower/upper performance bounds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Select candidate FIRO approaches. **Inputs:** 14-day 41-member HEFS/synthetic forecasts, climatology, perfect observed inflow, common constraints. **Judgment:** Compare policies under the same mass balance and release/ramping limits while preserving each method’s operational logic. **Output:** Comparable policy traces and flood-pool estimates.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–6. “Perfect” uses observed future inflow in MPC and is a benchmark, not deployable information.

### E17-R6: Compare HEFS with perfect and baseline references

**Description:** Baseline indicates no-forecast performance, perfect sets information potential, and HEFS tests available ensemble value. Similar HEFS/perfect thresholds indicate practical forecast capture for the event.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Interpret the magnitude of forecast-informed benefit. **Inputs:** Baseline climatology, perfect future inflow, HEFS ensemble, common MPC. **Judgment:** Attribute remaining gap to forecast quality and parameter feasibility, not only policy form. **Output:** Information-value comparison.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 6–12. The 1997 HEFS timing is useful despite underprediction; results may differ for less skillful events.

### E18-R6: Compare each candidate with the current baseline on multiple metrics

**Description:** The pilots use current operations as the comparator. Lake Mendocino alternatives are evaluated against 16 metrics; Prado evaluates 25 scenarios (five alternatives × five buffer-pool elevations) against current operations; Yuba–Feather compares two combined alternatives with baseline operations. The reasoning emphasizes comparison to baseline rather than direct ranking of alternatives built on incompatible assumptions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Candidate alternatives have been defined. **Inputs:** Simulated/assessed performance under baseline and FIRO scenarios, site-specific metrics. **Judgment/procedure:** Check whether benefits and flood-risk indicators improve or remain acceptable relative to current operations. **Branches:** Direct FIRO-to-FIRO comparison is limited at Yuba–Feather because starting assumptions differ. **Output:** Evidence for selecting, prioritizing, or rejecting a strategy.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.1.1–2.3.2, pp. 4–8; Figure 4, Figure 7, and Figure 9 are referenced textually. Metric definitions, weighting, and statistical procedures are not reported in this article.

### E19-R7: Separate the proposed comparison from the historical comparator

**Description:** To isolate the value of vapor, PS-FIRO and VPS-FIRO use the same operating rules except that PS-FIRO omits vapor; historical operation is a reference reflecting actual past responses. The authors recognize that historical operation often starts at lower levels, so direct comparison with FIRO can be inappropriate for some indicators.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Assessing whether vapor adds value. **Inputs:** VPS-FIRO, PS-FIRO, historical operation, initial levels, shared rules. **Judgment/procedure:** Use PS-FIRO as the primary benchmark for vapor's incremental effect and interpret historical differences cautiously. **Branches:** Compare FIRO schemes directly where rules/initial levels align; qualify historical comparisons where initial levels differ. **Output:** Attributable or bounded performance comparison.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.3, p. 5, and §4.3, p. 10. “Isolating” vapor is the study design rationale; perfect forecasts still limit real-world inference.

### E19-R9: Quantify benefits and safety across a flood-event sample

**Description:** The study calculates water-level, storage, spill, outflow-peak, and duration statistics for 50 floods between 2011 and 2023. Higher final storage and lower spills indicate water-use benefit; lower peaks/durations and lower maximum levels indicate flood-control benefit, subject to comparator differences.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Representative events alone are insufficient to summarize inter-event behavior. **Inputs:** 50 event simulations under VPS-FIRO, PS-FIRO, and historical schemes; defined evaluation indexes. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare distributions and means, preserving the meaning of each index and noting initial-level confounding. **Output:** Aggregate performance evidence.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §3.3 and §4.3, pp. 5, 10–11, Figs. 10–11. The sampling/event-selection rule beyond “50 flood events occurring between 2011 and 2023” is not reported.

### E22-R7: Compare ensemble scenarios with deterministic/no-forecast and observed operation

**Description:** The authors need to determine whether uncertainty-aware operation adds value beyond a deterministic approach. They compare optimized deterministic inflow operation, ensemble-member and ensemble-mean operation, and observed release/storage for the 2016 and 2019 floods.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** An ensemble-based policy has been optimized. **Inputs:** Ensemble member policies, ensemble mean policy, deterministic policy, observed operation, event inflows. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare peak releases, maximum storage, release fluctuation, and storage-capacity compliance. **Branches:** Member range reflects uncertainty; mean policy supplies a single operational summary; observed operation is a benchmark, not a controlled counterfactual. **Output:** Incremental performance evidence.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§2.2, 3.2–3.3, pp. 5, 11–15, Table 1 and Figs. 6–7. The paper does not provide a formal statistical significance or independent test set.

### E34-R4: Separate benchmark boundary construction from stochastic risk optimization

**Description:** The deterministic model first derives conservation bounds through aggregation/decomposition, then optimizes a single forecast; MOSP instead embeds risk objectives directly and does not derive dynamic storage bounds.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** need for a comparator and for a model that can handle uncertainty. **Inputs:** topology, forecast lead-times, routing, storage/outflow limits, forecast trace/scenarios. **Judgment/procedure:** use capacity-constrained prerelease logic for the benchmark; use direct expected risk objectives for MOSP. **Output:** deterministic benchmark solution D and stochastic formulation.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.2 and 2.3, pp. 4–7. The distinction between benchmark boundary constraints and MOSP’s direct risk objectives is explicit.

### E37-R1: Compare uncertainty structures of CO and FBO

**Description:** FBO introduces forecast-factor error, but CO’s observed inflow is also imperfect. The mode itself does not determine risk without considering the uncertainty pathway and operation response.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** select a flood-regulation mode for risk analysis. **Inputs:** forecast/observed inflow, initial level, storage/discharge relations, operation and decision factors. **Judgment/procedure:** separate hydrological, hydraulic, and decision-making uncertainties; identify the forecast-versus-observed inflow item as the main difference. **Branches:** CO has no forecast error term; FBO has forecast-factor error. **Output:** comparable risk-factor inventory.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.1–2.2, pp. 2–4; Fig. 1. The paper does not quantify every listed uncertainty.

### E37-R5: Compare CO/FBO risk under common flood scenarios

**Description:** FBO should be judged against CO using the same flood magnitude, initial level, thresholds, and repeated simulation design.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** risk comparison for a return-period flood. **Inputs:** typical flood hydrograph, error distribution, operation rules, initial level 325 m, permissible (Z_{a-max})/(Q_{a-max}), 10,000 replications. **Judgment/procedure:** perturb the relevant inflow or net-rainfall series, route actual storage/level, determine rule-based release, and count any level/release exceedance. **Output:** event count/frequency and scaled risk rate.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 3.4, pp. 9–11; Tables 4–5; Fig. 5–6. The comparator uses distinct CO observed-inflow error and FBO forecast-net-rainfall error models.

### E45-R3: Establish a stationary benchmark

**Description:** Conventional FLWL under stationary conditions provides the baseline generation, cumulative risk, and beta_n alpha CVaR threshold. This benchmark defines the maximum acceptable risk/loss for nonstationary adaptation.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** evaluate whether adaptation increases risk or loss. **Inputs:** observed 1882–2010 inflows, conventional operation, FLWL-performance relationship. **Judgment/procedure:** set FLWL to 145.0 m, derive annual generation and beta_n alpha at 98% confidence over 2020–2039. **Output:** Scheme A baseline.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–6, Table 1 and Scheme A description. The benchmark is model-based and depends on observed-data generation relationships.

### E50-R4: Design comparisons to isolate forecast value

**Description:** Baseline, Baseline-TOCS, Forecast, and Perfect are chosen to separate the effects of forecasts, TOCS increases, and perfect information. A gap between Forecast and Perfect indicates potential value from better forecast skill.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** aggregate storage benefit can arise from multiple mechanisms. **Inputs:** same baseline model and policy constraints, observed or forecast inflows. **Judgment/procedure:** compare controlled variants rather than attributing all Forecast benefit to forecasts. **Branches:** Baseline-TOCS raises TOCS by iteration; Perfect uses observed future data and no risk curve. **Output:** interpretable policy contrasts.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 5–6. The paper states most storage benefit during the dry 2013–2023 period arises from TOCS raising, while forecasts provide additional release-magnitude benefits.

### E50-R6: Treat storage and release frequency as joint viability checks

**Description:** Higher storage alone can hide flood risk; frequent maximum-safe releases are undesirable even when they do not exceed damaging downstream thresholds. Therefore the paper compares both normalized storage and frequency of maximum releases, plus trajectories.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** evaluate policy performance and operational acceptability. **Inputs:** mean storage, May 1 storage, maximum-release occurrence, event trajectories. **Judgment/procedure:** inspect whether storage gains coincide with safe release behavior and spread releases over time. **Branches:** Baseline-TOCS may raise storage but force frequent releases; Forecast may spread releases. **Output:** storage/flood-control performance comparison.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 5–7, Figs. 4–5. Maximum-release frequency is a proxy for flood-control burden; the study does not model all downstream impacts.

## RP09-B Robustness: sensitivity, stress, and holdout tests

Does the result depend on one flood, one parameter set, one record, or the number of scenarios. Strength: Established. 22 steps, 18 papers.

### E02-R9: Test how uncertainty affects reliability

**Description:** The authors evaluate whether forecast updating reduces uncertainty and whether larger forecast uncertainty or temporal correlation increases risk propagation. They also compare SMAA-TOPSIS with SMAA-2 to assess ranking clarity and decision-error risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** The framework’s value depends on how uncertainty changes selection reliability. **Inputs:** updated forecasts and state, uncertainty standard deviation (r), temporal correlation (q_{error}), and competing MCDM method. **Judgment/procedure:** Inspect standard deviations, risk of decision-making errors, RUD, and rank/holistic acceptability across experiments. **Branches:** Experiment 1 demonstrates the full recourse chain; Experiments 2 and 3 omit recourse to isolate forecast uncertainty level and correlation. **Output:** Case-bounded evidence about uncertainty propagation and method comparison.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 3.3.4–3.5, pp. 18–19; Table 1 and Figures 10–14. The experiments are numerical sensitivity/comparison analyses, not independent validation or deployment tests.

### E03-R8: Interpret sensitivity and case results within the model's claim boundary

**Description:** The authors vary the flood-control weight (x) and conservation-loss exponent (m), then apply the derived rules to Nierji data and compare policies. They use the results to judge whether dynamic adjustment can increase water conservation without lowering the modelled primary flood-control standard, especially in normal and wet years.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Assess how policy parameters and forecast quality alter the hedging rule and case outcome. **Inputs:** (x,m), forecast-error distribution, historical inflows, policy scenarios, simulated releases/levels. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare hedging curves, risk, end-of-season storage, and refilling statistics. **Branches:** Static MS-FLWL, balanced DC-FLWL, and conservation-only DC-FLWL-W; lower/higher uncertainty cases. **Output:** Case-specific evidence and sensitivity findings, not a universal operating guarantee.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 15–21, Sections 5–7, Figures 6–9, Tables 1–3. The study is an analytical/simulation case study; no independent deployment validation is reported.

### E08-R6: Convert distributions into risk and assess uncertainty interactions

**Description:** Flood risk is the cumulative probability that reservoir level or downstream flow exceeds its safety threshold. Comparing cases reveals which uncertainty drives modeled risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Interpret inferred distributions for decision support. **Inputs:** BN distributions, Hs and QCs, uncertainty cases and evidence levels L1–L3. **Judgment/procedure:** Sum probabilities above thresholds; compare BN with MC and compare one-/two-factor cases, forecast horizons, and evidence levels. **Branches:** Reservoir risk versus downstream risk; predictive versus diagnostic analysis. **Output:** Risk curves, posterior ranges, and factor attribution.

**Evidence boundary:** Eqs. (13)–(14), pp. 5–10, Figs. 7–16. Conclusions are case-bounded; apparent compensation is a modeled interaction.

### E09-R4: Stress-test forecast failure and capacity

**Description:** A forecast failure with the next flood arriving immediately is the adverse case for safe release. If all stored water cannot be removed within τ, retaining it would increase flood risk.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Dry/delayed-flood assumption fails. **Inputs:** ΔWtotal, qA, g(Z), qfl, τ. **Judgment/procedure:** Release at min(qA,g(Z)); compare required time t with τ; deduct unreleased volume from ΔWtotal to obtain ΔWsafe. **Branches:** If t≤τ, no added original risk under the model; if t>τ, reduce safe volume. **Output:** Safe added storage and quantified burden.

**Evidence boundary:** §§2.4, 3.4, pp. 6–7, 11–13, Tables 5–6. All 50 tested scenarios exceeded lead time in the case.

### E11-R5: Evaluate robustness and benefit

**Description:** The value of D-PSL depends on forecast skill, conveyance capacity, reservoir size, and flood pattern.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Assess whether relaxation helps without excessive risk. **Inputs:** Historical/simulated floods, capacities, skill levels, reservoir capacity ratios. **Judgment/procedure:** Simulate operation and compare selected D-PSL with FLWL and design levels; inspect overlap among period controls. **Output:** Conditional performance and limitations.

**Evidence boundary:** §§3–5, pp. 9–18. Larger simulated events and capacity changes alter outcomes.

### E13-R1: Diagnose hindcast coverage risk

**Description:** Short (roughly 10–35 year) hindcasts and small ensembles can omit rare forecast-error patterns. A policy calibrated and evaluated on one hindcast realization may not generalize to future forecast progressions, especially where a few atmospheric-river events dominate water and flood outcomes.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Designing or assessing FIRO under sparse extreme-event evidence. **Inputs:** Hindcast duration, ensemble size, observed events, policy dependence on forecasts. **Judgment:** Treat repeated plausible forecast realizations as necessary to assess robustness. **Output:** Need for synthetic forecast generation and out-of-sample testing.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in pp. 1–3. The paper argues for robustness testing; it does not establish that all unobserved forecast behavior is correctly represented.

### E14-R8: Test robustness under scenario quantity and measurement uncertainty

**Description:** More scenarios may represent uncertainty better but need not improve operation. The authors vary confidence level and add a 1,000-scenario stochastic process from observations to inspect objective ranges and compromise behavior.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Check dependence of conclusions on scenario coverage and observation error. **Inputs:** SE-Cloud scenario counts/confidence levels, observed inflows, MRO/TSO/Perfect solutions. **Judgment:** Examine hypervolume and objective changes rather than assume more scenarios are always beneficial. **Output:** Sensitivity evidence and bounded robustness interpretation.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 22, 25–27, Figure 17. Sensitivity is limited to two selected floods and specified confidence/scenario constructions.

### E16-R1: Diagnose robustness gaps

**Description:** Short hindcast records and limited extreme events cannot reveal all forecast-error sequences or scaled-event behavior. Synthetic ensembles and hydrograph scaling can expose overfitting and out-of-sample sensitivity.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Assess a forecast-informed policy intended for large floods. **Inputs:** Available hindcasts, synthetic forecast capability, historical event hydrographs, target return periods. **Judgment:** Test across forecast realizations and event magnitudes rather than a single hindcast. **Output:** Robustness study design.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 1–3. The framework is motivated explicitly; synthetic realism remains conditional on the generator’s properties.

### E16-R4: Train EFO under uncertainty and holdout branches

**Description:** EFO can overfit the forecast/event realization used in simulation-optimization. Training on 100 synthetic ensembles or withholding 1997 tests whether its risk curve generalizes.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Fit a risk curve to forecast-informed policy data. **Inputs:** Full 1990–2019 HEFS, each synthetic ensemble, or records with 1997 excluded. **Procedure:** Optimize 14 nondecreasing risk values under weighted objective; use median across random seeds/ensemble-trained policies as specified. **Branches:** HEFS full; synthetic; HEFS without 1997; synthetic without 1997. **Output:** EFO risk curves and policy variants.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 4–6, 8–10, Figure 8. Holding out 1997 has little effect in this setting; reasons are hypothesized and case-specific.

### E16-R5: Stress policies with scaled events and independent synthetic forecasts

**Description:** Scaling 1960–2019 annual maxima to 100-, 200-, and 300-year levels tests event magnitudes exceeding history; applying forecast scaling to HEFS and 100 synthetic ensembles tests forecast uncertainty at each magnitude.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Evaluate out-of-sample extreme-event robustness. **Inputs:** Four event hydrographs, log-normal annual-max fit, scaling factors, HEFS/synthetic forecasts. **Judgment:** Preserve event shape while increasing magnitude, explicitly acknowledging unchanged hindcast attributes as an assumption. **Output:** Flood-pool distributions and traces by policy/event/forecast type.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5–8. Scaling assumes fixed hydrograph shape and forecast-error attributes; it does not create independent climate-conditioned events.

### E20-R2: Diagnose the weakness of few-event or user-tuned calibration

**Description:** Calibration based on a limited number of recorded/design events may fit only those hydrologic conditions. Parameters supplied by users unfamiliar with mathematical programming can widen the gap between theoretical models and practice. A broad stochastic ensemble and automatic calibration are proposed to reduce this dependence.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A parametric RFCM must work across heavy/extreme floods. **Inputs:** Existing event-based calibration practice, parameter sensitivity, range of possible hydrologic loads. **Judgment/procedure:** Prefer automatic evaluation over a representative stochastic ensemble to event-specific tuning. **Branches:** Deterministic calibration remains possible but is recognized as less general; multiobjective aggregation or Pareto analysis is selected depending on commensurability. **Output:** Need for probabilistic parameter calibration.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§1–2.1, pp. 1–3. The paper does not quantify the bias from every alternative calibration strategy.

### E20-R8: Explore parameter behavior and inspect sensitivity before choosing a case policy

**Description:** The selected parameterization reduces the search to wq, PVfcl, and b, with M=6 fixed from prior sensitivity analysis and b searched over a stated range. The risk hypersurface and Pareto front reveal which parameters matter and how case characteristics shift the optimum.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A parametric RFCM needs a manageable, interpretable calibration search. **Inputs:** Feasible parameter ranges, prior sensitivity result for M, case damage/storage characteristics, Ir values. **Judgment/procedure:** Enumerate parameter combinations, inspect the risk surface, and compare the minimum and non-dominated solutions; use sensitivity patterns to interpret the influence of release versus storage penalties. **Branches:** Parameter importance differs by reservoir; wq is generally more influential, while b can become insensitive in regions. **Output:** Case-specific calibrated parameters and sensitivity interpretation.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §2.4, pp. 7–8, and §4, pp. 9–13; Tables 3–5 and Figs. 7, 10–11 are referenced textually. Exact search grid is not fully enumerated.

### E25-R5: Assess convexity and parameter effects

**Description:** A convex failure probability function can guarantee convex expected cost; a linear/concave curve depends more on storm distribution and antecedent flow. More convex failure curves generally support greater hedging; water-supply losses reduce it.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** determine if an interior/near-optimal hedging rule is credible. **Inputs:** F curvature θ, storm probability distribution, Qa, storage capacity, spill-loss function. **Judgment/procedure:** inspect cost curve and vary θ, Qa, and k. **Branches:** convex → optimum more assured; linear → distribution-dependent; concave → usually no hedging unless antecedent flow is below qlb. **Output:** qualitative direction and sensitivity.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 5122–5126, Figs. 5–6, Table 1. These are numerical-example relationships, not universal empirical laws.

### E26-R6: Test whether forecast precision changes the trade-off

**Description:** Forecast error affects both objective marginal risks and feasible zones. Better current-phase precision can permit more conservation; better future-phase precision changes shortage-risk balance and may reduce/increase current release depending on conditions.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** assess robustness to forecast quality. **Inputs:** σɛ1, σɛ2, I2, A, weights. **Judgment/procedure:** generate HR surfaces at three historical precision levels and compare SWA/EWA, release, and zone widths. **Branches:** current-phase and future-phase precision have different directions; nonmonotonic current release is possible. **Output:** sensitivity evidence and applicability condition.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 2747–2750, Figs. 6–7 and Conclusions. Trends are numerical/model-based and context-dependent.

### E31-R5: Compare uncertainty scenarios and model alternatives

**Description:** Isolating factors identifies which source matters most; combining them tests joint risk; comparisons test whether simplified risk definitions bias estimates.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** assessing model reliability or prioritizing data/forecast improvement. **Inputs:** uncertainty levels/correlations, safe-level grid, traditional model, MC comparator. **Judgment/procedure:** run Experiments 1–6; compare risk curves and distributions. **Branches:** IFE/OE/SOE separately, all together, correlated/independent, highest-level proxy, traditional model, MC. **Output:** critical-factor ranking and bias direction under tested settings.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 7–11, Table 2, Figs. 5–11. Results show IFE largest; this is a Foziling scenario conclusion.

### E32-R6: Judge uncertainty sensitivity and usefulness

**Description:** Increased uncertainty should broaden outcome probabilities and usually raise threshold risk. Isolating factors identifies drivers; combined and comparator experiments test whether the DBN gives useful risk information.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** checking robustness or prioritizing uncertainty reduction. **Inputs:** zero/one/two-SD scenarios, individual and combined uncertainty cases, static-BN/MC comparators. **Judgment/procedure:** compare probability distributions and exceedance risks across scenarios; inspect prediction and diagnosis outputs. **Output:** sensitivity findings, critical-factor insight, and decision-support interpretation.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 7–12, Figs. 6–18. The paper reports case-based trends and DBN/MC similarity; it does not independently validate the network or prove universal monotonicity.

### E35-R8: Treat branch/horizon selection as a performance-computation trade-off

**Description:** More branches capture more uncertainty but increase computational cost; longer horizons produce earlier, more conservative pre-release.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** configure TB-MPC for a case. **Inputs:** candidate branch counts 1–32, horizons 18–48 h, forecast scenario. **Judgment/procedure:** compare closed-loop peak release and CPU time; choose 16 branches and 48 h for later tests because 16–32 branch outputs overlap while cost rises. **Output:** selected operational configuration.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 4.3.1–4.3.2, pp. 15–17; Fig. 10–11; Table 2. There is no general branch-selection rule; the authors say it is user-defined.

### E38-R6: Test whether the selected scheme is flood-type dependent

**Description:** A scheme optimal for one hydrograph need not be best for another because flood sources and compositions differ. The authors therefore compare ranks across eight typical years and sum orders.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** decision must cover multiple design flood hydrographs. **Inputs:** scheme rankings for 1954, 1968, 1969, 1980, 1983, 1988, 1996, and 1998. **Judgment/procedure:** compare per-year precedence and use summed order as a cross-case robustness signal. **Output:** Scheme 5 and Scheme 6 each have summed order 25, lower than the other alternatives.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 14–15, Table 8 and Section 4. The summed order is a case-specific robustness heuristic; no uncertainty interval or independent validation is reported.

### E42-R6: Interpret lead-time/return-period dependence

**Description:** Longer lead times broaden error distributions; low-return-period design floods are more readily exceeded by ensembles.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** explain differences across lead times and design benchmarks. **Inputs:** lead time, return period, ensemble counts, CI/curtailment outputs. **Judgment/procedure:** compare risk rates and generation losses across four lead times and return periods. **Output:** case-level interpretation that IFU more strongly affects electricity curtailment than high-return-period flood risk.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 13–20, Figs. 7, 11–12, Table 3. The observed relationships are case results; the paper does not fit a universal scaling law.

### E46-R6: Validate generalization and identify forecast value

**Description:** Held-out typhoons test whether calibrated rules achieve stage targets; multi-peak Nari tests transitions and recovery. Pseudo-optimization quantifies the performance gap from lacking perfect forecasts.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** rules are calibrated and need out-of-sample assessment. **Inputs:** four unused typhoons, historical levels/releases, perfect-forecast comparator. **Judgment/procedure:** compare simulated maximum releases/levels and end-of-flood levels with records and pseudo-optimal results. **Output:** validation evidence and bounded claim about robustness.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 11–14, Figures 7–10 and Conclusions. Four-event validation supports case evidence but is not broad climate or deployment validation.

### E50-R2: Use a compact risk-curve representation of EFO

**Description:** Prior EFO policies use ensemble forecasts and risk tolerance. The authors parameterize the curve with lead days of zero allowable risk and a slope after risk is tolerated, plus a TOCS multiplier, to enable cross-reservoir optimization and limit overfitting relative to 14 independent lead-time parameters.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** optimize a common, computationally manageable FIRO form across 14 reservoirs. **Inputs:** forecast ensemble storage exceedance risk, lead time, TOCS. **Judgment/procedure:** allow only the minimum release needed to bring forecast risk below the curve; raise TOCS if constraints remain feasible. **Branches:** risk curve applies to Forecast; Perfect collapses ensemble and its risk-curve parameters do not apply. **Output:** three policy parameters per reservoir and FIRO target release.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit pp. 2–4, Fig. 2. The “minimum release” and policy equation are clear in prose; exact parameter bounds are not reported in the supplied text.

### E50-R7: Use attributes and diagnostics to qualify transferability

**Description:** Static reservoir attributes may help screen candidate FIRO sites, but weak correlations and equifinality mean they cannot predict benefits alone. Multiple seeds reveal different parameter combinations with similar objectives; LOOCV tests transfer to unseen years.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** move from case performance to site selection and policy reliability. **Inputs:** reservoir capacity/inflow/release ratios, forecast NSE, optimized parameters, training/held-out years. **Judgment/procedure:** compute correlations/regressions, inspect seed variability, and leave one water year out at a time. **Branches:** omit sites with fewer than 10 threshold releases from frequency analysis; scale TOCS bound when LOOCV failures appear. **Output:** attribute relationships, robustness diagnosis, and safer bound experiment.

## RP09-C Data and models must be credible first

Check synthetic forecasts, error models, and risk estimators before use. Strength: Established. 16 steps, 13 papers.

### E04-R5: Select a marginal family from empirical fit evidence

**Description:** The authors compare hydrologically used candidate distributions rather than imposing a normal marginal. They use fit diagnostics to select the generalized logistic (GL) distribution, noting that normality is rejected for X1–X3 in the main flood season.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Each reduction variable needs a marginal CDF. **Inputs:** Observed reduction series for each season and variable. **Judgment/procedure:** Estimate candidate parameters by L-moments; compare bias, RMSE, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov p-values; accept a fit when the reported p-value is above the selected significance level and consider bias/RMSE. **Branches:** α=0.05 generally; α=0.005 for main-season X2 because all candidate p-values were very small. **Output:** GL marginal distributions for subsequent copula fitting.

**Evidence boundary:** Sections 2.1.3.1 and 3.2, pp. 5 and 720–722; Tables 3–4 and Figs. 7–8. Fit selection is case-specific and does not prove GL is universally best.

### E13-R5: Check statistical parity before operational interpretation

**Description:** A synthetic sample should resemble HEFS in bias, rank consistency, spread/skill, and event-specific cumulative behavior. The authors use BSE diagrams, cumulative rank histograms, eCRPS distributions, a DTS two-sample test, and extreme-event comparisons before treating samples as fit for operational use.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Deciding whether synthetic outputs are credible inputs to EFO. **Inputs:** HEFS and synthetic ensemble forecasts over selected leads/seasons/events. **Procedure:** Compare aggregate metrics and rare-event mean/variance; interpret statistical indistinguishability at p = 0.1 where tested. **Output:** Evidence of parity and identified discrepancies.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 10–15. The tests compare synthetic and HEFS behavior; they are not independent real-world validation.

### E14-R1: Classify event scale before fitting

**Description:** Small and large floods have different rainfall/flow characteristics and different relevance of risk, resilience, and vulnerability. The authors use two clusters to reduce model error given only 22 events.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Prepare a forecast model for heterogeneous flood events. **Inputs:** Short-term rainfall indicators and flood-event records. **Judgment:** Group events by similarity so model inputs and later objective interpretation reflect scale. **Branches:** C1 small-scale, C2 large-scale. **Output:** Two event classes for separate modeling.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 3, 11–14, Figure 4. The two-cluster choice is case-specific and the paper says more return periods require future data.

### E19-R8: Test physical relation and operational mechanism before aggregate evaluation

**Description:** The authors first examine PP versus observed precipitation to establish an upper-bound relationship, then use representative Typhoon In-Fa and Muifa/Hinnamnor events to show how VCWL changes and how the three defenses operate, before calculating 50-event statistics.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A new indicator/rule needs physical and event-level plausibility checks. **Inputs:** PP/P time series, vapor/precipitation/streamflow peaks, level/outflow trajectories, representative floods. **Judgment/procedure:** Check PP remains above observed P, examine sequential peaks/time lags, and verify safe drawdown/regulation behavior in illustrative events. **Output:** Mechanism evidence supporting broader event statistics.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in §§4.1–4.2 and §5.1, pp. 5–11. This is a case demonstration and simulation check, not independent multi-basin validation.

### E19-R10: Check data/model adequacy and state operational limits

**Description:** Appendix A tests an Xinanjiang alternative for converting PP to VCWL; Appendix B validates ERA5 PWV against two nearby radiosonde stations. The discussion then acknowledges rule-based parameters, forecast uncertainty, and the need for future optimization.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** A proposed indicator depends on atmospheric data and a simplified runoff transformation. **Inputs:** Xinanjiang calibration/validation observations, ERA5 PWV, IGRA radiosondes, operational forecast uncertainty. **Judgment/procedure:** Check model skill and regional vapor representation, compare model-derived and coefficient-derived VCWL, and identify limitations before claiming operational applicability. **Branches:** Coefficient method is simpler; Xinanjiang produces smoother VCWL; real-time uncertainty/optimization remains unresolved. **Output:** Qualified implementation evidence and research agenda.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in Appendices A–B and §5.3, pp. 11–14. The appendix validations support components of this case, not deployment or universal transfer.

### E27-R5: Use Monte Carlo MWL distributions as operator risk evidence

**Description:** Repeated error realizations produce an empirical MWL distribution. Assuming approximate normal MWLs, moments can estimate probabilities of crossing 330.0 m, 333.0 m, or 337.33 m for selected return periods.

**Description:** MCDM requires alternatives, criteria, performance values, weights, ranking, and sensitivity analysis. Because selected criteria directly affect ranking, criteria must be examined before final MCDM.

**Description:** Because there is no external benchmark alternative, the authors compare rankings from TOPSIS, fuzzy matter-element, and fuzzy optimization models using all versus selected criteria.

Consistent rankings after PCA are interpreted as evidence that repeated information has been removed and the result is more rational.

## RP09-D Applicability depends on site, event, and institutions

Results from one reservoir, period, or basin do not transfer automatically. Strength: Established. 21 steps, 18 papers.

### E07-R8: Bound the conclusion by forecast skill and site conditions

**Description:** The authors interpret EFO's benefit as conditional on where the hindcast is reliable and on the reservoir's constraints. They note that the upper forecast range is most reliable at 4–15 day leads, that the risk-tolerance curve is sensitive to the training inflow sequence, and that larger events and additional hydraulic/damage modeling are still needed.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Decide whether the proof of concept can transfer to another reservoir or support stronger claims. **Inputs:** Hindcast bias/reliability, lead-time distribution of releases, event coverage, reservoir constraints, and objective function. **Judgment/procedure:** Treat transferability as an interaction between forecast skill/reliability and project-specific limits; require ESP forecasts and a sufficiently long hindcast for another evaluation. **Branches:** Further testing is needed for extreme events, other objectives, multiple reservoirs, and other sites. **Output:** A conditional transferability statement and research agenda.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit on pp. 16–18, Section 5. The paper reports five modeled events with Hopland flows above 15,000 cfs but does not simulate the 1964 flood of record; no universal safety claim is supported.

### E09-R5: Judge practical benefit and boundaries

**Description:** Dynamic storage benefit varies by actual flood and may be negative for small events when releases exceed inflow. A safe upper bound is therefore more useful than total planned volume.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Interpret scenario and historical event results. **Inputs:** Six historical floods, scenario tables, model assumptions. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare real-time added storage and inspect duration/lead-time trade-offs and worst-case safe values. **Output:** Case feasibility claim and need for broader risk/benefit evaluation.

**Evidence boundary:** §§4–5, pp. 13–15. Four of six historical events had positive added storage; two were negative. Single-reservoir scope is explicit.

The authors treat this as a robustness extension dependent on maintaining forecast-error relationships; the architecture is not specifically designed for nonstationarity.

## RP09-E Explain why the result occurs

Attribute improvements or failures to specific mechanisms rather than only reporting numbers. Strength: Established. 10 steps, 10 papers.

### E06-R8: Interpret contribution and design-criterion results within the case

**Description:** The authors read the near-equal single-uncertainty entropy values and small difference from the combined result as evidence that hydrograph shape is the main source and other effects may offset one another. They also compare computed entropy with design-criterion entropy and conclude that the TGR case conforms at 171 and 175 m.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Component and system risk results are available. **Inputs:** Tables 6–8, risk curves, and TGR design levels. **Judgment/procedure:** Compare component/combined entropy and threshold-specific values; use positive entropy for presence of modeled risk and the design-criterion comparison for conformity. **Branches:** Risk decreases as the safe level increases; at 180.4 m the simulated operation risk is zero. **Output:** Case-bounded interpretation of dominant uncertainty, offsetting effects, and design-level conformity.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 10–12, Section 5.3.2 and Conclusions. “Hydrograph shape is mainly responsible” is the authors’ interpretation of this TGR experiment; it should not be generalized without other cases or validation.

### E13-R6: Attribute operational differences cautiously

**Description:** Synthetic EFO runs generally align with HEFS but sometimes spill because pre-releases are too small or too late. The authors distinguish generator inability to reproduce HEFS type-1 overforecasts from a potential EFO vulnerability to plausible forecasts lacking those overforecasts.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Synthetic-driven operations diverge from the HEFS trace. **Inputs:** Storage/release traces, forecast samples, event timing, HEFS comparison. **Judgment:** Consider both model bias and policy sensitivity; do not assign all spills to one cause. **Output:** Conditional failure-mode interpretation.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 17–21, Figure 9, Table 1. The causal attribution is qualified (“suggests,” “likely”) and remains unresolved in some events.

### E15-R7: Select and interpret representative rules

## EP10-E Holdout, transfer, and generalization tests

Leave-one-year-out, held-out events, cross-reservoir thresholds, random seeds. Strength: Established (at threshold). 7 steps, 4 papers.

### E17-E7: Train and evaluate individual DT classifiers

**Description:** Map parameter combinations to target FP-reduction classes.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Determine conditions for each (FPT\in\{0,10,ldots,90\%\}). **Inputs:** Parameter sample and FP reduction. **Procedure:** Label better if reduction ≥ target, worse otherwise; split 70/30, train scikit-learn DT, cap depth at 3, inspect train/test accuracy, feature importance, root and deeper nodes. **Output:** Site/target-specific thresholds and factor maps.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 7, 11–13, Figure 7. Classifiers are highly accurate in the example due to threshold behavior; accuracy and exact tree settings for every case are not tabulated in the main text.

### E17-E8: Extract cross-reservoir thresholds

**Description:** Test whether parameter conditions generalize across the six sites.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Produce a screening rule rather than site-specific maps only. **Inputs:** All six-reservoir classification samples, HEFS/perfect labels, target FPT. **Procedure:** Aggregate samples, train DTs, extract thresholds from root/second/third nodes along the better-class rightmost path, compare feature importance and train/test accuracy. **Output:** Generalized (R_{max},RR,LT) thresholds.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 13–14, Figure 9. HEFS thresholds rise from approximately 22%/2%/2 days at 0% target to 42%/7%/6 days at 90% target; an example 20% rule is (R_{max}>23\%, RR>4\%, LT>2) days.

### E17-E9: Diagnose interactions and event dependence

**Description:** Compare parameter effects, forecast skill, and a supplemental event before making screening claims.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Explain threshold variation and generality. **Inputs:** Oroville heatmaps, 1997 and 2017 hydrographs, bias/eCRPS, default ratios, DT thresholds. **Procedure:** Inspect (R_{max})-(RR)-(LT) pairwise maps; compare HEFS/perfect; repeat Oroville analysis for February 2017; relate thresholds to event magnitude/shape and downstream constraints. **Output:** Qualified interpretation of maximum release dominance and event dependence.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 8–15, Figures 4–9 and SI references. 2017 (R_{max}) thresholds are within 5%–10% of 1997 in the example, but broader event/forecast-skill variation is future work.

### E46-E7: Validate and benchmark

**Description:** They test rules on Nari, Saola, Soulik, and Trami, compare simulated and historical outcomes, and run pseudo-optimization with perfect future inflow to estimate the value of forecasts.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** assess out-of-sample performance. **Inputs:** held-out hydrographs, historical releases/levels, calibrated rule parameters, perfect-forecast comparator. **Procedure:** run the stage rules and compare maximum release, stage-end level, and end-of-flood level. **Output:** validation figures and peak-reduction range.

**Evidence boundary:** pp. 11–14, Figures 7–10 and Conclusions. Simulated rules reach target levels more closely; no live operational test is reported.

### E49-E9: Test event-scale behavior and document limitations

**Description:** They apply RMOO/CCP analyses to roughly 100-year and 5-year forecast events to see how objective trade-offs change. The paper concludes that the 20-year case is medium-scale and that results depend on event magnitude.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** assess conditional applicability beyond the main event. **Inputs:** flood-scale scenarios and model. **Procedure:** inspect parallel-coordinate objective distributions and compare RMOO/CCP behavior. **Output:** event-scale interpretation.

**Evidence boundary:** Evidence p. 12–13, Fig. 12. Full input-generation and parameter details for these additional event scales are not reported in the supplied text; there is no independent validation or deployment trial.

### E50-E9: Analyze site attributes and policy equifinality

**Description:** They correlate storage surplus and reduction in maximum-release frequency with mean inflow/capacity, maximum inflow/safe release, safe release/capacity, and 4-day NSE. Sites with fewer than 10 threshold releases in either policy are omitted from frequency analysis. Ten random seeds are compared for objective and x1/x2/x3 variability.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** assess candidate-site screening and parameter robustness. **Inputs:** reservoir attributes, policy outcomes, optimized parameters across seeds. **Procedure:** compute correlations/regressions and inspect whether different parameters achieve similar objectives. **Output:** attribute relationships and equifinality diagnosis.

**Evidence boundary:** Evidence pp. 7–9, Figs. 6–9 and Tables S1–S2 referenced in text. Significant reported relationships include r=0.55 for peak inflow/safe release versus storage benefit and r=−0.79 for mean inflow/capacity versus release-frequency reduction; other relationships are weak/insignificant.

### E50-E10: Perform LOOCV and restrict the TOCS bound after failures

**Description:** They leave out each of 10 water years, train on the remaining years, and evaluate held-out years. Failures occur at PAR, EXC, and PNF (20 days total); a second LOOCV scales the TOCS upper bound using the ratio of largest 3-day inflow sums in 1994–2013 versus the training period. This eliminates max-release exceedances at a 4% mean-storage reduction per reservoir.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** test transfer to out-of-sample floods and mitigate observed failures. **Inputs:** annual forecast record, historical 1994–2013 inflows, optimized policy, largest 3-day inflow sums. **Procedure:** train/hold out each year; count exceedances/equalities and storage changes; rescale TOCS bound and repeat. **Output:** out-of-sample risk diagnosis and constrained-policy result.

**Evidence boundary:** Evidence pp. 8–10, Table 1 and Fig. 10. LOOCV still uses limited forecast years and policies can fail during clustered floods; the bound-scaling experiment is a model mitigation, not operational validation.

## EP10-F Verify the method itself

Use independent samples or another method to check risk estimates or evaluation results. Strength: Established. 8 steps, 6 papers.

### E19-E12: Validate hydrological and vapor-data components

**Description:** Appendix A calibrates Xinanjiang with 2012–2018 data and validates with 2018–2023 hourly observations; it reports NSE 0.94 and a floodwater relative error reported as 0.87% with the sign partly corrupted in OCR. Appendix B compares ERA5 PWV from 2011–2024 with the two nearest IGRA radiosonde stations (113 and 137 km away), reporting R² > 0.97 and RMSE < 4.0 mm.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** Simplified runoff conversion and reanalysis vapor input need component checks. **Inputs:** Model calibration/validation periods, hourly observations, ERA5 PWV, radiosonde PWV. **Procedure:** Fit/test Xinanjiang; compare coefficient- and model-derived VCWL/operations; validate ERA5 against nearby soundings. **Output:** Component-level accuracy evidence and a refined VCWL option.

**Evidence boundary:** Explicit in Appendices A–B, pp. 12–14. These validations do not validate VPS-FIRO deployment, forecast skill under operational conditions, or transfer to other basins; the relative-error sign is not fully reliable in the supplied OCR.

### E33-E7: Generate Monte Carlo verification samples

**Description:** Generate synthetic multisite inflow samples for verification.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** testing whether the proposed risk estimate is conservative enough. **Inputs:** downstream Huangzhuang main site, upstream Ankang and Danjiangkou slave sites, Pearson-III frequency curve, observed typical hydrographs, observed inter-site relations. **Procedure:** sample (N=10{,}000) maximum 7-day flood volumes at the main site; select typical observed flood processes; magnify by (K_{main,j}=W^D_j/W^O_j); successively choose corresponding slave-site processes to preserve flood encounter properties. **Output:** 10,000 multisite inflow scenarios and derived design-hydrograph samples.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2.1, pp. 3–4; Section 3.4.1, p. 5; Table 1. Table 1 reports means, CV, CS, and relative errors; several simulated statistics differ from observed values.

### E33-E8: Verify risk against the reference statistic

**Description:** Compare failure counts under the traditional statistic with failure counts under decisions produced using the two-stage model.

**Retained details:** **Trigger:** after generating verification samples and optimized decisions. **Inputs:** (N) samples, regular-operation failures (f), optimized-decision failures (f_r). **Procedure:** compute (R_{accepted}=100f/N); compute (R_r=100f_r/N); accept feasibility if (R_r\le R_{accepted}). In the case, (R_{accepted}=1.00%) (100/10,000) and (R_r=0.97%) (97/10,000). **Output:** verification finding that the assessed risk was not an underestimate in this test.

**Evidence boundary:** Section 2.2.2, p. 4; Section 3.4.2, p. 6. This is a finite stochastic experiment and does not establish independent or deployed validation.

### E39-E7: Compare AMIT with MLTP

# Advice to the Basin Commission: Flood-Season Operating Level Proposal

## Bottom line

- **Keep the current rule as the legal operating basis for now.** None of the three schemes is ready to adopt on the evidence in the table.
- **Do not take Scheme B forward in its present form.** It is the only scheme whose central estimate makes the city's flood risk worse (1.0% to 1.3% a year, about 30% higher). Its generation, irrigation and navigation gains cannot pay for that. City flood safety is a constraint to meet, not a benefit to trade.
- **Take Scheme A forward as the lead candidate, with Scheme C as the comparator.** Approve neither yet. Both go to a second-phase evaluation with uncertainty intervals, a dam-safety check, and agreed limits on the losses to irrigation and navigation. Both should also be compared with a forecast-based (dynamic) version of the higher level. My provisional preference is A, because it gives the largest generation gain (+3%) without raising the central flood-risk estimate. Its losses to irrigation (−2 points) and navigation (+2 days) are small, may be within the estimation error, and can be mitigated or compensated.
- **If A passes, adopt it through a conditional, time-limited trial.** It should run in shadow mode before real operation, with reversion triggers and a review after the trial seasons. The commission should not approve a permanent rule change on this study.

The three questions that decide this case:

1. **Does each scheme meet the governing flood-safety standard, for both the city and the dam, using the upper bound of its risk and not only the central estimate?** This decides whether B and possibly A are admissible.
2. **Can the study actually resolve the differences in the table?** The differences are 0.1 to 0.3 points of annual exceedance probability, 2 to 7 points of irrigation reliability, and 2 to 6 navigation days.
3. **Who bears the cost of the extra generation, and is that transfer acceptable or compensable?** A and C appear to gain generation partly at the expense of irrigation and navigation. B gains at the expense of the city's flood safety.

## 1. How to compare the schemes and handle the trade-offs

### 1.1 What each scheme actually trades

Change in each indicator relative to the current rule:

| Scheme | Generation | City exceedance probability | Irrigation reliability | Navigation shortfall days | Who gains / who pays |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | +0.3 bn kWh (+3%) | 0 | −2 pts (92→90) | +2 days (12→14) | Hydropower gains; irrigation and navigation pay a little |
| B | +0.5 bn kWh (+5%) | **+0.3 pts (1.0→1.3%)** | +3 pts (92→95) | −3 days (12→9) | Hydropower, irrigation and navigation gain; **the city pays in flood risk** |
| C | +0.2 bn kWh (+2%) | −0.1 pt (1.0→0.9%) | **−7 pts (92→85)** | **+6 days (12→18)** | Hydropower and the city gain a little; irrigation and navigation pay heavily |

**Dominance.** No scheme dominates the current rule, and none dominates another. A beats C on generation, irrigation and navigation but is worse on flood risk, by a difference too small to resolve (see 1.3). So on the central estimates all four are non-inferior. The choice therefore rests on safety screening and on explicit, stakeholder-owned preferences. A single score computed by the study cannot settle it.

**Mechanism.** The pattern is physically plausible, but the study should confirm it:

- B holds more water through the flood season. More storage remains for irrigation and for low-flow navigation releases, but less flood space is left for the city.
- A and C appear to raise generation by passing more water through the turbines or keeping a higher head during the season, while refilling less by the season's end. This explains the worse irrigation and navigation numbers.
- C combining more generation with less flood risk needs particular explanation. It may draw down earlier or deeper, gaining turbine flow and flood space at the expense of refill.

The commission should ask the study team to show this mechanism with representative wet, normal and dry years and their level trajectories, not only with aggregate numbers. A scheme whose gains cannot be explained should not be adopted.

### 1.2 Step 1: screen on safety first (no compensation allowed)

Before any benefits are weighed, each scheme must pass all of the following. The test uses the upper bound of the risk estimate, not the point value.

1. **City flood standard.** The upper bound (for example the 90% or 95% upper confidence limit) of the annual exceedance probability must be no higher than the more restrictive of:
   - the city's legally designated protection standard; and
   - the current rule's risk, unless the competent flood authority explicitly agrees to accept more.

   If the city's standard is a 100-year protection level (1% a year), the current rule sits exactly at the limit. B then fails outright, and A passes only if its upper bound is shown not to exceed 1.0%. That condition is unlikely with a point estimate of 1.0% unless the paired difference from the current rule is shown to be zero or negative. *Assumption: the protection standard is not given in the question; the commission must supply it.*

2. **Dam safety.** The table has no reservoir-side indicator at all. Starting from each scheme's raised flood-season level, the design flood and check flood (and the PMF where the dam's regulation requires it) must be routed. The routing should include closely spaced successive floods and should use realistic conditions: one gate out of service, discharge capacity at actual head, and level-measurement error. The resulting maximum levels must stay below the design and check flood levels. A higher starting level eats into surcharge storage one-for-one, so this check can bind even when the city indicator looks unchanged.

3. **Severity, not only frequency.** "Probability of exceeding the safe threshold" treats a marginal exceedance the same as a catastrophic one. For each scheme the study should also report:
   - the distribution of peak flow above the threshold;
   - the duration and volume of exceedance; and
   - preferably expected damage or a tail measure such as expected loss in the worst few percent of years.

   A scheme that keeps the same frequency but deepens the large-flood exceedances fails the spirit of the standard.

4. **Man-made rises downstream.** If a scheme depends on pre-releasing the extra storage when a flood is forecast, the pre-release itself must never push city flow above the safe threshold, or above what the natural flood would have produced.

### 1.3 Step 2: check whether the evidence can resolve the differences

Here is how much a 40-year record and the simulation can resolve:

- **Historical record alone.** At 1% a year, 40 years contain on average 0.4 exceedances. The chance of seeing no exceedance in 40 years is 0.99⁴⁰ ≈ 67% at 1.0% and 0.987⁴⁰ ≈ 59% at 1.3%. The record therefore cannot distinguish 0.9%, 1.0% and 1.3%. All of these figures must come from the probabilistic and simulated part of the study, and their credibility depends on the fitted flood-frequency model, which is itself fitted to 40 years.
- **Irrigation reliability.** If reliability were computed directly from 40 historical seasons, values would be multiples of 2.5%. 92%, 90%, 95% and 85% are not, which suggests they also come from synthetic sequences; the study should confirm this. With 40 years alone, the binomial standard error at 92% would be √(0.92 × 0.08 / 40) ≈ 4.3 points. A's −2 points would be well inside the noise, and even C's −7 points would be only about 1.6 standard errors if the comparison were unpaired.
- **Simulation sample size needed.** To tell 1.3% from 1.0% at about two standard errors with independent samples, the standard error of the difference must be about 0.15 points. That needs N ≈ 2 × 0.01 / 0.0015² ≈ 9,000 simulated years. To tell 0.9% from 1.0% it needs N ≈ 2 × 0.01 / 0.0005² ≈ 80,000 years. With paired simulation, where every scheme runs on the same synthetic years, the requirement falls sharply. If the schemes differ only in a fraction d of years, the standard error of the difference is about √(d / N). For d = 0.3% that gives N ≈ 1,300 years, and for d = 0.1% about 4,000 years. **The commission should ask how many synthetic years were run, whether the runs were paired, and what the confidence intervals of the differences are.**
- **Parameter uncertainty is a larger issue than sampling noise.** A 100-year flood estimated from 40 years carries wide uncertainty in its magnitude. That uncertainty affects all schemes in a similar way, so it matters less for the *ranking* than for the *absolute* test against the standard. It also matters most for B, and for A if the standard is binding. The study should propagate it, for example by bootstrapping or by Bayesian sampling of the distribution parameters, and report the upper bound of each scheme's risk.
- **Generation is the most robust number.** When all schemes are simulated on the same inflows, generation differences are mainly mechanical (head and turbine flow), so +3% and +5% are probably well resolved. They are still conditional on the generation model being correct.
- **Nonstationarity.** The 40-year record may not represent the future flood regime. A stress test with scaled floods, for example +10% and +20% on flood peaks and volumes, should show whether the ranking and the safety screen survive.

### 1.4 Step 3: weigh the benefits among the schemes that pass

- **Keep the indicators in their own units.** Do not collapse them into one weighted score: kWh or revenue, reliability, days, and flood risk are not commensurable, and a single score would let money buy flood risk. Present a side-by-side table like the one above, with confidence intervals.
- **Monetize only where defensible**, such as generation revenue, and where possible irrigation shortage losses and navigation delay costs. Use the money values to set the size of compensation, not to override safety.
- **For flood risk, show the break-even.** B's extra expected exceedances are 0.003 per year. At an electricity value p per kWh, B's 0.5 billion kWh a year breaks even on expected flood damage only if the damage per exceedance event is below 0.5×10⁹ p / 0.003 ≈ 1.7×10¹¹ p. Over 50 years the chance of at least one exceedance rises from 1 − 0.99⁵⁰ ≈ 39% to 1 − 0.987⁵⁰ ≈ 48%. This is shown to make the stakes visible. It is not a reason to accept B: expected-value trading is not appropriate for a protection standard.
- **If a ranking is needed among the schemes that pass**, elicit weights from the stakeholders, for example with swing weights in a facilitated workshop. Test whether the ranking holds when the weights are varied, when the indicators are taken at the ends of their confidence intervals, and when wet, normal and dry years are taken separately.
- **Ask for better alternatives, not only the three on the table.** The study should test:
  - **A dynamic (forecast-based) flood-season level.** The reservoir runs above the current flood-limited level only when forecasts show no significant flood, and must pre-release back down when they do. The check is whether pre-release can finish in time: the extra storage ΔV must satisfy ΔV ≤ (Q_safe − Q_inflow) × usable forecast lead time. For scale, 1,000 m³/s of net release sustained for 24 hours removes 86.4 million m³. That volume must be compared with the storage between the current level and the proposed level.
  - **A staged flood season.** The level stays at the current value in the main flood period and is raised in the late season to protect refill. This could combine B's refill advantage with A's flood neutrality.
  - **A hybrid of A and B** that keeps B's refill behaviour while holding city risk at or below the current level.

  Such variants often recover most of the generation gain without increasing flood risk. They could make the choice among A, B and C unnecessary.

### 1.5 Checks on the study itself before relying on it

- Every scheme, including the current rule, must be simulated under identical inflow sequences, starting levels, constraints and downstream routing.
- The current rule as simulated should reproduce historical levels and releases reasonably well.
- The simulation must be closed-loop: each scheme's releases feed back into its storage.
- Downstream routing must include uncontrolled tributary and local inflow to the city. If most city exceedances come from local inflow, the reservoir rule has less influence on city risk than the table implies.
- If any scheme relies on forecasts, it must be evaluated with realistic forecast errors (hindcasts from the forecast system that will actually be used), not with perfect foresight.
- Equipment and operating limits must be respected: release ramping, drawdown rates for bank and dam slope stability, and navigation-lock constraints.

## 2. Recommendation and conditions

**Provisional recommendation: keep the current rule in force. Take A forward as the lead candidate and C as a comparator into a second-phase evaluation together with forecast-based and staged-season variants. Do not take B forward unless it is redesigned so that its city flood risk does not exceed the current rule's.**

**Why A over C.**
- A gives more generation (+0.3 vs +0.2 bn kWh).
- A's losses to irrigation and navigation are much smaller (−2 pts and +2 days, against −7 pts and +6 days).
- A's flood risk is indistinguishable from C's on any plausible sample size (1.0% vs 0.9%).
- C's irrigation reliability of 85% means about one failed season in 7 instead of one in 12.5. That is a large burden on one stakeholder in exchange for a flood-risk gain that cannot be shown to be real.

**Why not B.** B is the only scheme whose central estimate moves city risk the wrong way. If the city's standard is 1% a year, B breaches it outright. Even if the standard were looser, B transfers risk to the city, a party that gains nothing from the transfer. Its attractive irrigation and navigation numbers show that holding more water helps conservation. That is a reason to look for a variant that keeps the refill benefit without the flood penalty, not a reason to accept B.

**Conditions for adopting A (or any successor scheme).**

1. **Flood-risk interval.** A paired-simulation confidence interval for A's annual city exceedance probability, with flood-frequency parameter uncertainty propagated, must have an upper bound no higher than the current rule's value and the legal standard. If the upper bound is above the standard, A must be modified (for example, by lowering the raised level or making the raise forecast-conditional) and rechecked.
2. **Dam safety.** Design and check floods (and the PMF if required) must be routed from A's highest permitted flood-season level, under realistic equipment assumptions. The results must show maximum levels within the dam's design limits, and the dam-safety authority must accept them.
3. **Severity.** A's distribution of exceedance magnitude, duration and damage must not be worse than the current rule's in large floods, for example in the worst 1–5% of simulated years.
4. **Irrigation and navigation limits.** Agree in advance with the irrigation district and the navigation authority the maximum acceptable loss, for example reliability no lower than a stated value in dry years and a cap on extra navigation days. Agree also a mechanism, such as a share of the incremental generation revenue, to compensate or to fund mitigation such as targeted late-season releases.
5. **Trial.** Before real operation, run A in shadow mode for at least one flood season beside the current rule. Then operate it under a temporary, documented deviation authorized by the competent authority for a fixed number of seasons, followed by a formal review.
6. **Reversion triggers.** Return immediately to the current rule if:
   - a significant flood is forecast or observed above an agreed trigger flow;
   - forecasts are missing, late, or diverging from observations (if the scheme relies on them);
   - a gate or outlet is out of service; or
   - the dam-safety authority instructs it.

   The flood-control authority holds the power to order reversion at any time. All decisions and level departures are logged for audit.
7. **Review.** After each flood season, review observed performance against the predicted indicators. Run a full re-evaluation after the trial period, and again when the record has been extended or the flood regime changes.

**What would change this recommendation.**

- *Toward rejecting A:* the upper bound of A's flood risk exceeds the standard; dam-safety routing fails; A's exceedances are more severe in large floods; or A's irrigation loss proves larger in dry years than the 2-point average suggests.
- *Toward C:* the city's flood risk is found to be well above an acceptable level under all schemes, so that a reduction in flood risk becomes the priority, and C's 0.1-point reduction is shown to be statistically real. Even then, C would need an irrigation mitigation plan.
- *Toward B (or a B-like variant):* a redesign brings its city risk to no more than the current rule's with a credible upper bound, while keeping most of its refill benefit. That would make it the strongest option, because it improves three objectives.
- *Toward keeping the current rule:* none of the variants passes the safety screen with credible intervals, or the confirmed generation gains are judged too small to justify the institutional cost and residual risk.

## 3. Who should take part, and how

The question does not name a country, so the institutions are described by function. The commission should map each one to the bodies that actually hold these roles in its jurisdiction.

| Party | Role in the decision | How they take part |
|---|---|---|
| **Basin commission** | Convenes the process and makes the decision (or recommendation) | Chairs a steering group; sets the terms of reference, timetable and decision rules; issues the final decision through the formal instrument (revision of the reservoir operating rules or regulation, and the annual flood-control operating plan) |
| **Flood-control authority with legal power over the reservoir's flood operation** (the flood-control and drought-relief command, or the water-resources authority) | Holds the safety veto on city flood risk; approves any change to the flood-season level | Sets the binding flood-safety acceptance criteria in advance; approves the trial and the final rule; holds the authority to order reversion |
| **Dam-safety regulator** | Confirms that the dam remains safe from the raised starting level | Reviews the design/check flood routing; must sign off before any trial |
| **City government and its flood-control department; emergency management** | Bear the flood risk | Must agree to the definition of the city safe threshold and the acceptable risk; review the severity results; take part in warning and emergency protocols, including notification of man-made releases during pre-release |
| **Hydropower company (reservoir owner/operator)** | Proposer and beneficiary; carries out operation | Supplies the generation model and operating data; funds the second-phase study and the compensation mechanism; operates under the trial rules and logs all decisions |
| **Grid dispatch authority** | Affected by changes in generation timing; constrains turbine scheduling | Confirms that the generation gains can be dispatched and absorbed; coordinates turbine schedules during floods |
| **Irrigation district (and the water-allocation authority)** | Bear the irrigation losses | Agree the acceptable minimum reliability (especially in dry years) and the compensation or mitigation; review the refill results |
| **Navigation authority** | Bears the navigation losses | Agree the acceptable number of shortfall days and minimum-flow conditions; coordinate on lock operation |
| **Hydrological and meteorological services** | Provide the flood forecasts and data | Certify forecast skill and lead time if any scheme relies on forecasts; supply hindcasts for evaluation |
| **Environmental/ecological agency** | Environmental flows (not in the table) | Confirm that environmental flow requirements still hold under each scheme |
| **Independent technical review panel** | Checks the study's quality | Reviews the risk modelling, uncertainty intervals, simulation sample size and dam-safety routing before the steering group decides |
| **Downstream public and local communities** | Affected by flood risk and man-made releases | Are informed through public consultation on the proposal and on any trial; are warned of operational changes |

**How the process should run.**

1. **Agree acceptance criteria before the analysis.** The steering group agrees the safety criteria, set by the flood and dam-safety authorities, and the limits on losses to other users, before the second-phase results are seen. This prevents the criteria from being negotiated after the fact around a preferred scheme.
2. **Joint technical working group.** Experts from the operator, the flood authority, the irrigation district and the navigation authority work with shared data and models. The independent panel reviews their work.
3. **Graded decision rules.**
   - Safety criteria are binding; the flood-control and dam-safety authorities can veto.
   - Benefit trade-offs among the schemes that pass are negotiated by consultation, using transparent indicators and weights and the robustness checks described above.
   - Compensation is agreed in writing.
4. **Formal approval.** The approved rule is issued through the legal operating instrument by the authority that holds that power. Until then the current rule remains the legal basis of operation.
5. **During the trial.** All parties receive regular reports. The warning protocol for releases, staff training, and version control of the operating rules must be in place before the trial starts. An after-season review is held with all parties.

# Study design: staged (seasonal) flood-limited water levels for a multipurpose monsoon reservoir in India

## Summary

The question is not "can a higher level be kept for part of the season?" That can almost always be shown in a simulation. The question is whether staging can be justified by a real, stable difference in flood hazard within the monsoon, and whether the staged rule keeps both the dam and the town 40 km downstream at least as safe as the current rule. The benefit only matters once both of those hold.

With the data on hand, the student can complete the seasonality analysis, the reservoir-side mass balance, a first estimate of benefits and a feasibility check on the forecasts. The data cannot show that the town is still safe. That needs the dam's design flood or design standard, the safe channel capacity or danger level at the town, the local inflow between the dam and the town, and sub-daily flood hydrographs. Irrigation demands and hydropower plant data are also needed to put a value on the benefit. My provisional judgement: the study can support the claim that staged levels are worth formally assessing. It can support a recommendation to change the rule only if the missing safety data are obtained.

I would treat the 8 years of forecasts as a secondary strand. They are too short to carry the safety case for a rule that will be used for decades. They can be used to test whether forecast-based drawdown could act as an extra safety margin, or justify a small band above each stage level. They cannot be the basis of the staged levels themselves.

## 1. The questions that decide the outcome

1. **Is there a real, stable seasonal difference in flood hazard?** Staging is justified only if floods in the early and late monsoon are clearly smaller (in peak and in multi-day volume) than in the main monsoon. The difference must exceed the sampling uncertainty of 45 years of data, hold in both halves of the record, and match a physical cause such as monsoon onset and withdrawal. If late-season floods from depressions or cyclones occur in this basin, a higher late-season level may not be justified at all. I do not know which basin this is, so the data must answer this.
2. **Does the staged rule keep annual flood risk at or below the governing standard and at or below the current rule's risk, for both the dam and the town?** This includes risk combined over the stages and the transitions between them. It must hold at the upper confidence bound, not only the central estimate.
3. **Is the benefit large enough to matter and to be resolved?** The extra storage is the stage-level rise times the reservoir area. It adds value only in years when the current rule would end the monsoon short of the conservation target, or spill water that the staged rule could keep. The number of such years in 45 sets how well the benefit can be estimated.

## 2. Research question and hypotheses

**Main research question.** Can a set of seasonal flood-limited water levels, fixed in advance for defined stages of the monsoon, raise irrigation supply and hydropower output compared with the current single level? The constraints are that the annual probability of exceeding the dam's safety levels, and of releases exceeding the safe flow at the town, must not rise above the current rule or the applicable design standard.

**Sub-questions.**
- **Q1 (hazard).** Does the 45-year record show statistically and physically robust within-season differences in flood peaks and 1-, 3- and 5-day flood volumes? Where do the stage boundaries fall?
- **Q2 (safety).** For each candidate set of stage levels, what are the annual risks at the dam and at the town, compared with the current rule? Combine the stages and include parameter uncertainty.
- **Q3 (benefit).** What are the gains in end-of-monsoon storage, irrigation reliability, hydropower energy and spill, how do they vary between years, and in which years do they occur?
- **Q4 (forecast extension, secondary).** Can the 1–5-day forecasts support drawdown back to the stage level within the effective lead time? How much margin would that add, and with what error?

**Hypotheses.** H1: off-peak stage floods have design quantiles meaningfully lower than main-season ones. H2: a staged rule exists that gives a positive benefit with annual risk no higher than the current rule. Both hypotheses can be falsified. Rejecting H1 is a valid result. It would mean staging cannot be justified on hydrological grounds.

The study should be built in two tiers. **Tier 1** uses the available data only. It can answer Q1, part of Q3 and Q4, and give reservoir-side risk under assumed design floods. **Tier 2** adds the safety data above and answers Q2 fully. A recommendation to change the rule needs Tier 2.

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 Baseline: the current rule as an executable comparator

- Code the current rule as a daily water-balance simulator, S(t+1) = S(t) + I(t) − R(t). Releases follow the rule, are capped by level-dependent discharge capacity, and include the gate sequence, refill logic and any drawdown requirements.
- Run it over the 45 years. Compare it with historical levels and releases if they can be obtained. Record the starting levels at the onset of each large flood. Operators are often above the nominal level when a flood starts, because the previous flood has not yet been evacuated. Any comparison between rules should begin from the same realistic starting states.
- Report the baseline's spill, end-of-monsoon storage, irrigation shortfall, energy, maximum level and maximum release. Check whether the current rule's losses come from the fixed level or from something else, such as release capacity or demand. If they come from something else, staging cannot help.

### 4.2 Seasonality and stage division (Q1)

- For each year, extract peaks and 1-, 3- and 5-day maximum volumes by 10-day period. Use the timing of annual maxima and of peaks-over-threshold events. Volumes matter because a reservoir's flood space is consumed by volume, not by the instantaneous peak.
- Find candidate boundaries with simple, transparent tools: the relative frequency of large events by period, circular statistics of flood dates, and change-point tests on the within-season series. Check the boundaries against monsoon onset and withdrawal dates in this basin.
- Keep to two or three stages, such as early monsoon, main monsoon and late monsoon. More stages mean more fitting to 45 years and a rule that is harder to operate.
- **Robustness.** Repeat the division on the first and second halves of the record. Test for trends in stage maxima to check whether the record is stationary. Shift the boundaries by ±10 days to see whether the results change. Early onset or delayed withdrawal in a given year can be handled by making the transition a condition rather than a date, for example "stay at the main-season level until the onset or withdrawal criteria are met". This makes the rule more robust.

**Resolution check.** Each stage has at most 45 stage-maximum values. The 10-year flood is expected to be exceeded about 4.5 times in 45 years, with a Poisson standard error of about √4.5 ≈ 2.1, or about ±47%. The 100-year flood is expected to be exceeded about 0.45 times. So the record cannot check design-level risks directly. Stage design floods must come from frequency analysis, with bootstrap confidence intervals. The stage difference counts as robust only if the confidence intervals of the off-peak and main-season quantiles are clearly separated, not just their central estimates.

### 4.3 Stage design floods and risk allocation

- Fit stage-specific frequency distributions for peaks and multi-day volumes. Compare candidate distributions, such as GEV and Pearson III, and bootstrap the quantiles.
- **Combining risk over stages is the subtle point.** The safety standard is annual. If each of three stages is designed for the annual design frequency p, the annual risk is 1 − (1 − p)³. For p = 1%, that is 1 − 0.99³ ≈ 2.97%, almost three times the standard. There are two correct approaches:
  - (a) Allocate the annual standard across stages so that 1 − Π(1 − p_i) ≤ p_design.
  - (b) Simulate the whole season and compute annual risk directly, then compare it with the current rule's annual risk under the same inflows.

  I would use (b) as the main test and (a) as a design-flood check.
- **Hydrograph shapes.** Use several historical large floods scaled to stage quantiles, matching both peak and volume. Include the least favourable shape and closely spaced successive floods. The most dangerous case for a raised level is often a second flood arriving before the first has been evacuated.

### 4.4 Candidate rules

- Search a grid of stage levels. A reasonable structure keeps the current level as the main-season level and as a lower limit, with higher levels only in the off-peak stages. Also include the transition rules: how the reservoir draws down before the main-season level applies, and how it fills after the main season ends.
- Keep structurally different alternatives:
  - (i) staging only in the late monsoon (filling toward the conservation level earlier);
  - (ii) staging in both the early and late monsoon;
  - (iii) staged levels plus a forecast-conditioned band (secondary; see 4.7).
- For each candidate, run two kinds of test:
  1. **Design-flood routing from each stage level**, including the dam's spillway design flood and check flood, routed from the raised starting level. Also run stress cases: one gate out of service, discharge capacity at the actual head, level-measurement error, and successive floods.
  2. **Continuous simulation** over the 45-year record and a long stochastic series, to get annual risk and benefit distributions.

### 4.5 Stochastic inflow generation

- 45 years is too short for annual risks near 1% or below. Generate long daily series that preserve seasonality, persistence and multi-day flood volumes, for example a seasonal k-nearest-neighbour bootstrap or a seasonal autoregressive model with fitted extremes. Add scaled flood events beyond the historical range for stress testing.
- Check that the synthetic series reproduce stage-maximum statistics, the timing of floods, and the persistence of wet spells. Persistence matters because back-to-back floods drive the risk to a raised level.
- **Monte Carlo resolution.** To estimate an annual probability p with relative standard error e, about (1 − p)/(p·e²) simulated years are needed. For p = 1% and e = 10%, that is about 9,900 years. For p = 0.1%, about 100,000. Compare the rules with the same synthetic years (common random numbers), so that the difference in risk is estimated much more precisely than either risk on its own.

### 4.6 Downstream: the town 40 km away

- **Travel time.** For a flood-wave speed of roughly 1–3 m/s (assumed), 40 km takes about 40,000 / 3 ≈ 3.7 h to 40,000 / 1 ≈ 11 h. That is shorter than the daily step. A daily model therefore cannot resolve the peak at the town. Major floods need a sub-daily routing model of the reach, such as Muskingum or similar, calibrated to any downstream gauge.
- Short travel time works both ways. Once a large release is made, it reaches the town within hours and cannot be recalled. Equally, local inflow from the intermediate catchment can raise the town's flow with little warning, independent of the dam. Add town flow = routed release + local inflow, using coincident historical local flows. Without them, the town's risk is understated.
- **Downstream failure measures:**
  - annual probability that flow at the town exceeds the safe capacity;
  - annual probability that it exceeds the danger level;
  - days per year above the safe capacity;
  - whether stage-transition drawdowns or prereleases create flows above what the natural flood would have produced (man-made rises).

### 4.7 Forecast strand (secondary)

- **Error by lead time.** For each lead time from 1 to 5 days, quantify error by flow magnitude. Check bias, spread and whether errors are non-normal. Also measure the correlation of errors across lead times. The 5-day volume error is a sum of daily errors. With equal daily spread σ and correlation ρ = 0.6 (illustrative), its variance is 5σ²(1 + 4 × 0.6) = 17σ², not 5σ². The standard deviation is underestimated by a factor of about 1.8 if errors are treated as independent.
- **Sample size.** 8 monsoons give roughly 8 × 120 ≈ 960 forecasts per lead time. These are serially correlated, and only a handful are large floods. The data can show skill for ordinary flows but cannot establish error behaviour in the tail that matters for safety. Confirm that the archive comes from the same forecast system and configuration that would be used in operation.
- **Feasibility of drawdown.** The volume to be released is ΔV = A × Δh. Illustration only, with assumed values: an area of 50 km² and a 1 m raise give ΔV ≈ 50 MCM. Drawdown at a net rate (release minus inflow) of 500 m³/s, which must stay below the town's safe flow, takes 50 × 10⁶ / 500 = 10⁵ s ≈ 28 h. So the forecast must reliably signal the flood more than about 1.2 days ahead, after allowing for decision and gate-operation time. The student should repeat this with the real area, safe flow and ramping limits. Use a conservative (upper) quantile of forecast inflow and a worst case in which the forecast misses the flood.
- **Information bounds.** Evaluate any forecast-based band in closed loop, out of sample (leave-one-year-out over the 8 years), and between a no-forecast bound and a perfect-forecast bound.
- **Role in the conclusion.** The staged levels must be safe without forecasts. Forecast-based drawdown should be presented as an added safety margin or a possible later extension, not as a condition of safety.

### 4.8 Benefits and trade-offs (Q3)

**Indicators:**
- end-of-monsoon storage, and the probability of reaching the conservation level by a key date;
- irrigation supply reliability and shortfall in the following dry season (needs demand data);
- annual energy and firm power (needs plant data);
- spill volume;
- refill failure in years with a late or weak monsoon.

**Mass-balance plausibility.** The extra conserved water each year cannot exceed ΔV in the stage concerned, and it adds value only in years when the current rule falls short. If simulation shows the current rule reaches the conservation level in, say, 30 of 45 years (to be determined), then about 15 years carry the benefit. The mean benefit is then estimated from about 15 values, and its uncertainty should be reported accordingly.

**Presentation:**
- One table showing every candidate against the current rule: risks with confidence intervals, and benefits.
- Remove any candidate that fails a safety test before weighing benefits. Safety is non-negotiable: a benefit cannot offset it.
- Among the remaining candidates, mark which are dominated and keep the non-dominated set. Then state a preferred option and explain the preference, for example the largest benefit with no increase in risk at the upper confidence bound. Avoid collapsing everything into a single weighted score.

### 4.9 Uncertainty beyond forecasts

Include the following in the risk simulation, and state which are excluded:
- the starting level at flood onset (drawn from simulated or historical distributions, not set exactly at the stage level);
- parameters of the flood-frequency fit;
- hydrograph shape;
- discharge capacity (gate availability);
- storage loss to sedimentation;
- the year-to-year timing of the transitions.

## 5. How to assess the effect on flood safety and the other objectives

**Flood safety: pass/fail screens, applied first.**
- **Dam:** with each stage level as the starting level, the spillway design flood and check flood routed through the reservoir must stay at or below the maximum water level allowed by the design. This must hold with sub-daily hydrographs, successive floods and one gate out of service.
- **Dam, annual risk:** the annual probability of exceeding the safety level under the staged rule, combined over stages and transitions, must be no higher than under the current rule. Use the upper confidence bound.
- **Town:** the annual probability that flow at the town exceeds the safe capacity must be no higher than under the current rule, and must meet the town's protection standard if one exists. Peak flows at the town during transitions must not exceed what the natural flood would have produced.

**Other objectives.** Compare benefits only among candidates that pass the screens, using the same inflows and starting states as the baseline:
- irrigation reliability and shortfall;
- energy;
- spill;
- refill.

**Mechanism.** Explain where the benefit comes from: which stage, which years, and whether it is mainly from avoided spill or from earlier filling. Show hydrographs of levels and releases for representative floods, not only averages.

## 6. What results would be sufficient to recommend a change

All of the following, not just some:

1. **Seasonality is real.**
   - The off-peak stage design quantiles, for peaks and multi-day volumes, are lower than the main-season ones, with confidence intervals clearly separated.
   - The stage division holds in both halves of the record and under ±10-day shifts.
   - The division has a physical explanation.
   - No significant late-season extreme-event mechanism has been ignored.
2. **Dam safety holds at every stage level.** The design and check floods routed from the staged levels stay within the allowed maximum level under the stress cases.
3. **No increase in flood risk.** The annual risks to the dam and the town are at or below the current rule's (and the standard), at the upper confidence bound, combined correctly over stages.
4. **The benefit is material and robust.**
   - The gain is positive in the 45-year simulation and in the long synthetic series.
   - It holds out of sample, for example when stage levels are chosen on one half of the record and tested on the other.
   - It is larger than its uncertainty.
   - It is consistent with mass balance: no greater than the added storage times the number of years in which it can be used.
   - Its size justifies the institutional cost of a rule change. Agree that threshold with the owner in advance; I cannot set it from the question.
5. **The rule can be operated.**
   - It is expressed as a simple table of levels and dates or conditions, with transition rules.
   - Drawdown at transitions is feasible within ramping limits and the town's safe flow.
   - It has a fallback: revert to the current level if a large flood is forecast or observed during a transition, or if forecasts are unavailable.
6. **The safety data exist.** Results 2 and 3 rest on the design-flood data, the safe flow at the town and local-inflow data, not on assumptions.

**What each outcome supports.**
- **Tier 1 only:** the study supports "staged levels are hydrologically justified and show potential benefit; a formal safety assessment is warranted". It does not support a rule change.
- **All six results met:** the recommendation is to propose the staged rule for review and a trial, not immediate adoption.
- **Result 1 fails:** the right conclusion is that staging is not justified at this site. A forecast-based approach would then be the alternative to investigate, and it needs a longer forecast record.

## 7. Path to adoption (beyond the thesis, but it shapes the design)

**Approval.** Changing the operating rule means revising the reservoir operation manual or rule curve. As far as I know, in India most such reservoirs are owned and operated by a state water resources or irrigation department, or by a power utility for hydropower projects. Under the Dam Safety Act, 2021, rule changes affecting safety come under the state dam safety organisation and committee, with the National Dam Safety Authority at national level. The Central Water Commission issues design-flood and reservoir-operation guidance and runs flood forecasting in many basins. The student should confirm who the actual owner and approving bodies are for this dam, and involve them early. Ask them to agree the acceptance criteria in Section 6 in advance, especially the design-flood standard and the town's safe flow. Until a revised manual is approved, the current rule remains the legal basis.

**Trial.**
1. Run the staged rule in shadow for one or two monsoons, alongside actual operation, with the current rule still governing.
2. If that goes well, move to a documented temporary deviation with clear reversion triggers: a forecast or observed large flood during a transition, missing or late forecasts, or a gate out of service. Name who decides on reversion and log every decision.
3. Review after each flood season.

**Warning.** The downstream district administration and disaster-management authority must be informed of the changed levels. They must be warned before any transition drawdown, because the town is only a few hours downstream.

## 8. Key assumptions and what would change the plan

**Assumptions:**
- The reservoir currently has a single flood-limited level through the monsoon.
- Gated outlets allow controlled drawdown.
- The town's flood risk is influenced mainly by releases plus some local inflow.
- The wave speed and the area and discharge figures used in the illustrations are placeholders.

**What would change the plan:**
- **No clear seasonal difference, or a late-season cyclone flood mechanism:** staging collapses, and only a forecast-based approach remains, which needs more forecast data.
- **Large local inflow or a town safe flow close to normal monsoon releases:** the town, not the dam, becomes the binding constraint. Staged levels may then give little room, and the study's focus moves to downstream coordination.
- **A strong trend in flood magnitudes:** stage levels must be set on recent or adjusted statistics, with a revisit trigger.
- **Releases or demand, rather than the flood-limited level, limit the current rule's performance:** staging will not deliver the benefit, and the research question should be re-scoped.

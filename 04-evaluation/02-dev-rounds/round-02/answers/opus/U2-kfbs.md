# Can 154 m serve as the operating upper limit above the 152 m flood-limited level?

## Short answer

- **Theoretical maximum under the stated assumptions: about 154.5–154.6 m.** In the 28 hours that are usable, the reservoir can release about 232 million m³ more than it receives. The 180 million m³ between 152 m and 154 m fits inside that, and on paper there is room for roughly 50 million m³ more.
- **154 m should not be adopted now as the actual operating upper limit.** It works only if everything goes as planned. Forecasts are issued every 6 hours, so in the worst case a flood is first seen only 30 hours before it arrives. In that case 154 m uses up all the margin (182 million m³ releasable against 180 needed). Several other plausible conditions also push the limit back to 154.0 m or below: inflow already rising during the drawdown, a slower decision process, gate ramping, or the need to wait for one confirming forecast before making a man-made release.
- **Provisional working limit: about 153.5 m.** This depends on the verifications listed below. At 153.5 m the operator can wait one 6-hour update to confirm a flood before acting, and can still absorb a moderate rise in inflow or delay. 154 m could be adopted later if the verifications show that the time and volume margins actually exist and that the dam stays safe even if a flood arrives with the reservoir still at 154 m.

## The questions that decide the case

1. **How much time is really available?** The usable time is the lead time at which a flood is reliably detected, minus the 8-hour decision, warning and gate time. The nominal 36 hours is the best case, not the guaranteed one.
2. **How fast can the reservoir be lowered without breaking the town's 5,000 m³/s limit?** That depends on the intervening flow that arrives with the storm and on the inflow during the drawdown.
3. **What happens if the drawdown is not finished?** Forecasts can miss a flood, arrive late, or misjudge its timing. The real test is whether the dam and the town remain within their design standards when a design or check flood starts above 152 m.

## 1. The theoretical calculation

**Allowable release during the drawdown.** The town's limit is 5,000 m³/s. The ungauged intervening catchment can add up to 1,200 m³/s in a storm. The storm that brings the reservoir flood also falls on this nearer catchment, which responds sooner, so I assume the full 1,200 m³/s coincides with the drawdown. The reservoir can then release at most 5,000 − 1,200 = **3,800 m³/s**. That is well below the 7,500 m³/s outlet capacity at 152 m, so the binding limit is downstream, not at the dam.

**Net release.** 3,800 − 1,500 (inflow before the flood) = **2,300 m³/s**, which is 8.28 million m³ per hour.

**Usable time.** 36 h lead time − 8 h for decision, warning and gates = **28 h**.

**Volume that can be released.** 2,300 × 28 × 3,600 = **231.8 million m³**.

**Time needed to empty 152–154 m.** 180 × 10⁶ / 2,300 = 78,300 s ≈ **21.7 h**. Adding the 8 h latency, the flood must be detected **29.7 h** before it arrives. Against the nominal 36 h, that leaves 6.3 h of slack.

**Equivalent level.** Between 152 m and 154 m the average storage is 90 million m³ per metre. Extending that rate linearly gives 152 + 231.8/90 ≈ **154.6 m**. The water surface widens with height, so the storage per metre above 154 m is probably larger, and the true level is likely a little lower (about 154.5 m). I need the storage curve to fix this figure.

So under the stated assumptions, 154 m sits about 0.5 m below the theoretical limit. That is the operator's case. The rest of this answer tests whether that margin survives realistic conditions.

## 2. Why 154 m is not yet an acceptable operating limit

Each line changes one assumption from the nominal case, except the last two lines, which combine changes. Levels are converted at 90 million m³/m.

| Case | Usable time | Net release (m³/s) | Releasable volume (10⁶ m³) | Equivalent ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal (36 h lead, 8 h latency, inflow 1,500, intervening 1,200) | 28 h | 2,300 | 231.8 | ≈154.6 m |
| Worst update timing: flood enters the 36 h window just after a forecast issue, so it is first seen at about 30 h | 22 h | 2,300 | 182.2 | ≈154.0 m (no margin) |
| Wait one update to confirm before releasing | 16 h | 2,300 | 132.5 | ≈153.5 m |
| Decision and warning take 12 h instead of 8 h (night, holiday, communication failure) | 24 h | 2,300 | 198.7 | ≈154.2 m |
| Inflow rises on the early limb, averaging 2,000 m³/s | 28 h | 1,800 | 181.4 | ≈154.0 m (no margin) |
| Wet antecedent conditions, inflow averaging 2,500 m³/s | 28 h | 1,300 | 131.0 | ≈153.5 m |
| Worst update timing plus inflow averaging 2,000 m³/s | 22 h | 1,800 | 142.6 | ≈153.6 m |
| Optimistic: no intervening flow during the drawdown, release 5,000 m³/s | 28 h | 3,500 | 352.8 | >155 m (not usable as a design basis) |

What the table shows:

- **Forecast timing alone uses up the margin.** With issues every 6 hours, the guaranteed detection lead is 30 h, not 36 h. Detection at 154 m needs 29.7 h, so the slack is about 0.3 h.
- **At 154 m there is no time to confirm a flood.** The last moment to commit to full release is about 30 h before arrival, so the first forecast that shows a flood must trigger an immediate man-made release of 3,800 m³/s. Every false alarm then costs up to 180 million m³ of water and an unannounced-looking rise downstream. At about 153.5 m the operator can wait for one confirming update.
- **The result is very sensitive to the inflow during the drawdown.** Every 500 m³/s of extra average inflow removes about 50 million m³ (about 0.55 m) from the ceiling over 28 h. In southern China's main flood season, consecutive rain events are common. The 1,500 m³/s "typical" inflow is a median-type value, not a design value.
- **The intervening flow is the main uncertainty on the release side.** It is ungauged, and 1,200 m³/s is an estimate. If the true value in a design storm is higher, the allowable release falls one-for-one. For example, at 1,500 m³/s intervening flow the net release drops to 2,000 m³/s and the nominal ceiling to about 154.2 m.

These conditions are not independent: the same storm raises reservoir inflow, intervening flow and the chance of forecast error together. Treating them as separate small risks would understate how often several occur at once. That is why I set the provisional limit from the combined case (about 153.5–153.6 m) rather than from the nominal case.

## 3. Safety margins that must be considered

**Time margins**
- The forecast update phase: up to 6 h is lost when a flood enters the window just after an issue.
- Forecast timing error: the flood may arrive earlier than forecast. I need the distribution of arrival-time error at 24–36 h lead, and whether the "36 h" refers to the start of the rise or to the peak.
- Latency: the 8 h is a nominal value. The plan should use a high percentile, such as night-time or a communication fault.
- Gate ramping and warning lead: the release cannot jump from 1,500 to 3,800 m³/s instantly. Each hour spent ramping costs about 4 million m³ (half of 8.28 million m³/h).

**Volume margins**
- Rising inflow during the drawdown, and higher wet-season base inflow.
- Forecast volume error: a flood larger than forecast needs the full 152 m starting point, with no shortfall.
- Error in the storage curve: sediment and the age of the survey both affect it. Because 180 million m³ over 2 m is about 90 million m³ per metre, a 5% storage error is worth about 0.1 m.
- Water-level measurement error: a few centimetres on the 154 m reading and on the 152 m target.

**Downstream margins**
- Uncertainty in the 1,200 m³/s intervening flow and in when it peaks.
- The travel time from the dam to the town. Releases made late in the drawdown are still in the channel when the intervening peak arrives.
- Whether 5,000 m³/s already includes freeboard, or whether the release target should sit below it.
- Rules on man-made rises. A 3,800 m³/s prerelease is more than double the natural flow before the flood, so it needs a public warning and must not create a peak downstream that the natural flood would not have produced.

**Facility margins**
- One gate out of service. A partial-gate capacity at 152–154 m still has to exceed 3,800 m³/s, which is probably comfortable against 7,500 m³/s but must be checked.
- Backup power and staffing for gate operation.
- The drawdown rate. 2 m in about 22 h is about 0.09 m/h, or about 2.2 m/day. This must be checked against the permitted drawdown rate for the dam's upstream slope and the reservoir banks.

**Dam-safety margin (the controlling one)**
- A forecast can miss a flood entirely, especially short, intense convective or typhoon rain. In that case the flood starts at 154 m instead of 152 m, and the extra 180 million m³ goes into surcharge storage.
- The raised level is acceptable only if routing the design flood, the check flood, and closely successive floods from 154 m (and from partly drawn-down levels such as 153 m) keeps the maximum level within the design and check flood levels. It must also keep downstream releases within the protection standard for the event class.

**Refill and benefit side**
- The extra storage is worth something only if it is kept most of the time. A false alarm costs up to 180 million m³. The plan should report how often a drawdown happens without a flood, and how much of the released water can be recovered under a low-side inflow forecast.

## 4. Verifications to carry out

1. **Forecast verification over the lead times that matter.** Use hindcasts from the same forecast system and configuration that will be used in operation. Measure:
   - the probability of detecting a flood at least 30 h and 36 h ahead;
   - the false-alarm rate;
   - the arrival-time error, and the volume and peak error at 24–36 h;
   - separate results for typhoon, frontal and convective floods.

   The question these answer is how many floods would have been detected too late to empty 154 m.
2. **Closed-loop back-testing of the proposed rule on historical floods.** Hold 154 m, update every 6 h, trigger on the actual forecast, include the 8 h delay and ramping, and draw down at no more than 3,800 m³/s minus the simulated intervening flow. Record for each event:
   - the level when the flood arrives;
   - the maximum reservoir level;
   - the peak flow at the town, including intervening flow routed with the actual travel time;
   - false-alarm drawdowns and refill.

   Compare against the current 152 m rule under identical conditions.
3. **Routing design and check floods from raised starting levels.** Start from 154 m, 153.5 m and 153 m, include back-to-back floods, use the actual discharge-capacity curve with one gate out of service, and compare against the design and check flood levels and the town's protection standard. If the rule passes this for a missed forecast at 154 m, the forecast becomes a benefit rather than a safety dependency, and 154 m becomes much easier to justify.
4. **Risk compared with the current rule.** Use Monte Carlo sampling of:
   - update phase (uniform over 0–6 h);
   - arrival-time error;
   - inflow on the rising limb;
   - intervening flow, sampled jointly with reservoir inflow rather than independently;
   - latency.

   Estimate the probability that the drawdown is incomplete when the flood arrives and the resulting probability of exceeding the dam's design level or the town's 5,000 m³/s. The usual acceptance test is that risk is no higher than under the fixed 152 m level. Then find the highest ceiling that passes; I expect it to be near 153.5 m.
5. **Can the record resolve the risk?** A flood-season forecast archive typically holds only a few dozen independent significant floods. With 30 events and no failures, the 95% upper bound on the failure rate is still about 10% (the "rule of three": 3/30). So back-testing alone cannot show that a small risk is acceptable, and design-flood routing and stress tests must carry most of the argument.
6. **Checks of the intervening catchment and the downstream channel.** Use rainfall–runoff analysis or regional methods for the intervening catchment, or install a temporary gauge. Compute the travel time from dam to town. Check whether 1,200 m³/s is a design-storm value or a typical one.
7. **Equipment and operating tests.** Gate operating times, the ramp rate actually achievable, capacity with one gate out, and the timing of the warning chain, using a drill to measure the real latency.

## 5. Information still missing before the final limit can be set

- The level–storage curve, especially between 152 m and about 155 m, and the date of its survey.
- The discharge-capacity curve by level and by gate, ramp limits, and any limit on drawdown rate.
- Forecast skill statistics: detection rate, false-alarm rate, timing and volume error by lead time. Also, whether the 36 h lead refers to the start of the rise or to the peak, and what forecast value means "no flood".
- The flood-season inflow distribution, both before the flood and on the rising limb, rather than a single typical 1,500 m³/s.
- The intervening catchment's area, its hydrology, the basis for the 1,200 m³/s figure, and when its flow peaks relative to the reservoir's flood.
- The travel time and attenuation from dam to town, and whether 5,000 m³/s includes freeboard. Also whether other protected points lie between the dam and the town.
- The design and check flood hydrographs, the design and check flood levels, the crest and freeboard, and the existing flood-regulation rule from 152 m.
- Whether the flood season is divided into stages. For example, 154 m may be justifiable early or late in the season but not in the main flood period.
- The actual distribution of time taken by decision and warning, and the procedure for man-made releases.
- Institutional constraints: who approves the dynamic range, and what acceptance criteria they require.

## 6. How the raised level would be operated, and approved

**Operating rule**
- The 152–154 m band remains flood-control storage that is borrowed for benefit. It must be returned whenever a flood is forecast.
- At each 6-hourly update, compute the time still needed to reach 152 m at the allowable net release. Compare it with the forecast arrival time minus latency.
- When the remaining slack falls below a set margin, begin prerelease without waiting.
- Below the ceiling, the operator may wait one update for confirmation.

**Abort and correction rules**
- If the flood arrives early while the drawdown is under way, switch immediately to the normal flood-regulation rule from the current level. Keep the town's flow within 5,000 m³/s unless the dam-safety rule requires otherwise.
- If inflow on the rising limb exceeds the forecast, increase the release up to the downstream limit at once.

**Reversion triggers.** Return to 152 m immediately if any of the following occurs:
- the forecast is missing or late;
- forecasts diverge from observed inflow beyond an agreed tolerance;
- a gate or power failure occurs;
- the intervening catchment is already producing high flow.

**Approval.** In China, raising the operating level above the design flood-limited level is a change to the flood-control operation.
- It has to be approved by the flood-control authority with jurisdiction over the reservoir. That is normally the flood control and drought relief headquarters or water-resources department at the relevant level, and the river basin authority where the reservoir falls under its management. The change is written into the approved flood-season operation plan.
- Until approval, the design flood-limited level of 152 m remains the legal basis.
- I recommend a trial season of shadow operation: compute the 154 m and 153.5 m decisions beside the actual 152 m operation and log them. The acceptance criteria should be agreed in advance with the dam-safety authority.
- Every man-made release needs a defined downstream warning (public, emergency services, any downstream operators).
- After each flood there should be a review of every drawdown decision.

## 7. What would confirm or change the provisional 153.5 m

**Toward 154 m or higher**
- Forecasts reliably detect floods 36 h or more ahead in all flood types.
- Rising-limb inflow stays near 1,500 m³/s during the drawdown window.
- The intervening flow is shown to peak after the drawdown is complete.
- Above all, design and check floods routed from 154 m with a missed forecast stay within the design and check levels.
- A meteorological trigger with a longer lead time, approved for starting the drawdown, would also add usable time.

**Toward 153 m or lower**
- Missed floods or arrival-time errors larger than about 3 h at 30–36 h lead.
- Common wet-season inflow above about 2,000 m³/s.
- An intervening-flow design value above 1,200 m³/s.
- A real decision and warning chain longer than 8 h.
- Limits on drawdown rate below about 2 m/day.
- Unacceptable dam-safety results when routing floods from a raised start.

**Key assumptions in the numbers above**
- The intervening flow coincides with the drawdown at 1,200 m³/s.
- No release increase is possible during the 8 h latency.
- Storage is linear at 90 million m³/m between 152 m and 154 m and extrapolated above.
- The 36 h lead refers to when the flood starts to arrive at the reservoir.
- The 5,000 m³/s limit applies at the town without further freeboard.

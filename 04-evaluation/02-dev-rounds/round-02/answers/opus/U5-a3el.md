# Recommendation: a conditional, moderated version of (a)

**Provisional choice: (a), both reservoirs act now, with the action weighted heavily toward the upper reservoir.** The upper reservoir starts a controlled prerelease now. Its job is to empty the occupied part of its flood-control zone before the flood arrives, and not to draw below the flood-limited level. The lower reservoir keeps up the drawdown it is already making and later raises its release just enough to pass the upper reservoir's extra water. City flow stays well below 6,000 m³/s throughout.

Ranking: (a) moderated > (c) > (b). Option (b) puts the effort into the reservoir that needs it least. Option (c) can still work physically, but it uses up about half of the time margin and gains nothing in return, unless the water now in the flood-control zones has a value that the question does not mention (see the conditions below).

The condition behind this choice is that the water to be released sits **inside the flood-control zones**, above the flood-limited levels. Outside a flood, the operating rule should be emptying that water anyway. If it does, the prerelease costs almost no conservation water. If the operator is instead allowed to keep this water (for example, under a dynamic flood-limited level, or because refill is scarce), the case for acting now gets weaker and (c) becomes defensible.

## The questions that decide it

1. **Is the upper reservoir's free space the weak point, and is there time to enlarge it?** Yes on both counts. The arithmetic follows.
2. **Does prereleasing raise risk at the city or at the lower dam?** Not materially. All prerelease water passes through the lower reservoir, which has a lot of free space and also controls the tributary.
3. **Is the forecast volume the flood volume, or the total inflow volume?** This is unresolved. It changes how large the flood is, but it does not change the ranking of the options. I treat the 250 (150–400) million m³ as the flood volume the system must manage. That is the conservative reading. Read as total inflow over 72 h, it averages only about 965 m³/s (579–1,543 m³/s), which is below the current inflow and would mean a minor event.

## Numbers that support the choice

Conversion used throughout: 1,000 m³/s sustained for 1 hour = 3.6 million m³.

**Current state**

| | Upper | Lower |
|---|---:|---:|
| Free flood-control storage | 0.40 × 300 = **120 million m³** | 0.85 × 500 = **425 million m³** |
| Occupied flood-control storage | 180 million m³ | 75 million m³ |
| Current net release (release − inflow) | 1,500 − 1,200 = 300 m³/s | 2,500 − (1,500 + 300) = 700 m³/s* |

*Lower inflow = upper release from 18 h earlier (assumed steady at 1,500) + tributary 300.

What the table shows:
- **The lower reservoir is already emptying its flood-control zone at current settings** and will be nearly empty in about 30 h. Its 425 million m³ of free space is more than the high end of the forecast flood volume (400). Prereleasing it further, as in (b), adds little. This is why (b) is dominated.
- **The upper reservoir is the constraint.** Even after the current slow drawdown, it would have only about 152 million m³ free, less than the central flood volume of 250 and well below the high end of 400. It is also the reservoir whose own dam safety is at stake if it fills and loses control.

**Can the upper zone be emptied in time?**
- If prerelease starts now at about 3,000 m³/s: net 1,800 m³/s = 6.5 million m³/h, so 180 million m³ takes **about 28 h**. That finishes just before the forecast onset at about 30 h, with little slack.
- If we wait 12 h, as in (c): about 13 million m³ more is released at the current rate. The remaining ~167 million m³ in 18 h needs net ≈ 2,580 m³/s, a release of **about 3,800 m³/s**.
- If we start at hour 18: net ≈ 3,900 m³/s over 12 h, a release of **about 5,100 m³/s**. That is near the most we could send toward the lower reservoir while still giving the city a margin if the tributary also rises.
- **Point of no return for fully emptying the upper zone: roughly hour 15–18.** If the flood arrives earlier than forecast, or if the outlets cannot pass full capacity at the current head, this point moves earlier. Waiting is therefore feasible only for about one forecast cycle, and it puts the operation close to its limits. Acting now spreads the same volume over 28 h at a moderate rate.

**Downstream effect of acting now**
- The upper's 3,000 m³/s reaches the lower reservoir after about 18 h. Lower inflow then becomes about 3,300 m³/s. If the lower releases about 3,500 m³/s, city flow is about 3,500 m³/s, reached around hour 28. That is 2,500 m³/s below the safe flow.
- The tributary joins **above** the lower dam, so a tributary surge goes into the lower reservoir's storage and does not reach the city directly. Even in the worst case during prerelease, lower inflow is 3,000 + 1,500 = 4,500 m³/s. The lower reservoir can pass this or hold it.
- The upper's flood releases cannot reach the lower reservoir before about hour 48 (onset at 30 h plus 18 h travel). By then the prerelease water will already have passed. The two do not stack.

**Is the system at risk under any option?** Rough stress test without prerelease (c), using the high-end volume of 400 million m³ compressed into 24 h. That gives a mean of about 4,600 m³/s and, for a triangular hydrograph, a peak of about 9,300 m³/s above the base flow. To hold the upper release to about 4,500 m³/s, the upper must store the part above 4,500. That is about ½ × 12 h × 4,800 m³/s ≈ 105 million m³, which just fits within the ~120–150 available. Whatever the upper passes on, the lower can absorb at a 3,500 m³/s excess (inflow up to 9,500 against a 6,000 release) for about 34 h before its 425 million m³ is used up.

Conclusion: **the city is probably safe under all three options for the central forecast.** The options differ in how much margin they keep for a high, peaky flood that coincides with a tributary storm, and in how close the upper dam comes to losing control. Acting now buys that margin at almost no cost. This is why the recommendation is low-regret rather than a claim that disaster depends on it.

## Key assumptions (to confirm in the first hour)

- The occupied storage in both reservoirs is above the flood-limited level. Prerelease stops at the flood-limited level, so no conservation water is lost and there is no refill risk if the low-side forecast of 150 million m³ comes true.
- No significant local inflow enters between the lower dam and the city. If some does, the city margin shrinks by that amount.
- The 8,000 m³/s release capacity is available at current heads, with all gates working. In practice capacity drops at lower heads, which is one more reason to start early.
- The 18 h and 10 h travel times stay roughly constant over this range of flows.
- Ramping limits and drawdown-rate limits (for dam slopes and river banks) allow the upper to go from 1,500 to 3,000 m³/s within a few hours. If they do not, start sooner, not later.
- The forecast onset of "about 30 hours" carries roughly ±6–12 h of timing uncertainty. This is an assumption. A 72-h volume range of 150–400 suggests large uncertainty generally.

## Actions now (within the hour)

1. **Upper reservoir:** raise the release in steps, within ramping limits, from 1,500 to about 3,000 m³/s. Target: reach the flood-limited level (300 million m³ free) by about hour 28. Do not go below the flood-limited level.
2. **Lower reservoir:** keep releasing 2,500 m³/s for now, since it is already drawing down. From about hour 16–18, when the upper's extra water starts arriving, raise the release to about 3,300–3,500 m³/s. Its storage then stays close to the flood-limited level, and city flow stays at about 3,500 m³/s. Cap city flow at about 4,500 m³/s during prerelease. That leaves a margin for routing error and for any local inflow below the dam.
3. **Information requests:** ask the forecasters for the expected inflow hydrograph shape (peak and time to peak), a quantitative rainfall forecast for the tributary, and an interim update at hour 6 if one is possible. Put the tributary gauges on hourly reporting.
4. **Equipment check:** gate availability, outlet capacity at current heads, staff and power at both dams, and level-measurement checks.
5. **Warnings:** notify the city, emergency services, downstream river users, and the operators of both dams of the man-made rise to about 3,500 m³/s at the city from about hour 28. State that the flow stays below the safe flow, and give the expected duration.
6. **Authorization and logging:** the flood-control headquarters approves the deviation from routine operation under the applicable flood-control plan or dispatch authority. Record the decision, the forecast it was based on, the triggers below, and who can override.

## What can wait for the 12-h forecast, and what would change the action

Can wait: the final release rates of both reservoirs during the flood, how storage is shared between the dams, and whether to draw the lower below its current trajectory.

| New information at the next update (or earlier from gauges) | Change |
|---|---|
| High end of the volume range ≤ ~130 million m³ and peak inflow ≤ ~4,000 m³/s (fits within the upper's free space as it already stands) | Slow the upper back toward its routine drawdown. This is effectively (c). No harm done, since the water released was flood-zone water. |
| Central volume ≥ ~350 million m³, peak inflow ≥ ~6,000 m³/s, or onset ≤ ~24 h | Raise the upper to ~4,500–5,000 m³/s. The lower releases up to ~5,000 m³/s at the city to empty its zone fully. Prepare a city warning for flows approaching the safe flow. |
| Tributary forecast > ~1,000 m³/s coinciding with the arrival of the upper's flood releases at the lower dam (hours ~48–100) | The lower becomes the binding store. Empty it fully before hour 48. The upper then holds more of the flood, within its own dam-safety limits. |
| Upper inflow starts rising above its release earlier than forecast | **Stop the prerelease.** Switch the upper to flood-control mode: limit its release so that release plus tributary stays within what the lower can pass at ≤ 6,000 m³/s, and use the upper's storage. |
| Tributary rises sharply during the prerelease | The lower holds the tributary water. City flow is not raised above the planned cap. |
| The water in the flood-control zones turns out to be conservation storage that may legitimately be kept, or refill is unlikely | Switch to (c) with the routine drawdown. The system arithmetic shows city safety is probably still met, but recheck by hour 12–15, before the point of no return. |
| Gate outage, or reduced outlet capacity at current heads | Start earlier and at a higher rate at the affected dam, or shift the role to the other dam. |

## Missing information and its effect

- **Inflow hydrograph peak and timing (most important).** Volume alone does not decide this. A flatter flood of 250 million m³ never makes the upper release above ~4,500 m³/s, whatever we do now. A sharp flood of 400 million m³ uses up all of the upper's space. This uncertainty is why acting now is worthwhile: it keeps the options open whatever the peak turns out to be.
- **Tributary forecast.** This decides whether the lower reservoir's very large margin is really available for the upper's flood, or must be kept for a simultaneous tributary storm. The worst case, 1,500 m³/s held for the whole event, would add about 311 million m³ above the current 300 m³/s over 72 h. Together with 400 from the upper, that exceeds the system's free storage. It is manageable only because the lower can release up to 6,000 m³/s.
- **Whether the forecast volume is the flood volume or the total inflow volume.** If it is total inflow, the event is small and every option is safe. The recommendation still holds, because releasing water that sits in the flood-control zone costs little.
- **Status of the water in the flood-control zones, local inflow below the lower dam, ramping limits, and outlet capacity at current heads.** Each of these can move the point of no return or the size of the city margin. None reverses the ranking unless the flood-zone water turns out to be conservation water that may be kept.

If the first-hour checks confirm that the occupied storage is flood-zone water, the information is enough to choose moderated (a). If that cannot be confirmed within the hour, start the upper prerelease anyway at a modest rate, about 2,500 m³/s. It is easy to reverse, it keeps the upper's point of no return within reach, and the decision can be revisited at the 12-h forecast.

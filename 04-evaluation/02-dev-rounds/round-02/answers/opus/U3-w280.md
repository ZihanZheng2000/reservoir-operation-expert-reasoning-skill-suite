# Review of the prerelease-rule evaluation (600 million m³ reservoir)

## Bottom line

I would not recommend adoption on the basis of this analysis. The new rule may well be better, but the evidence does not show it, for three reasons:

1. **The "95% confidence" does not measure what the owner thinks it measures.** It describes how the result varies across random draws from the firm's own error model, for one design hydrograph. It says nothing about whether that error model is right, and nothing about hydrograph shape, starting level, other flood magnitudes, or model structure. Those are the uncertainties that decide whether the 18% is real.
2. **The error model is biased in favor of the forecast-based rule.** Pooling errors across season, flow size and lead time, assuming normality, and sampling each day independently all make the synthetic forecasts look more accurate than the real ones during large floods, and especially at the longest lead time, which drives prerelease. A rule that relies on forecasts looks better under errors that are too small. Each of these simplifications moves the 18% upward and narrows its spread.
3. **The evaluation counts only the benefit side, for a single event type.** It reports a reduction in peak outflow for a 100-year flood. It does not show the maximum reservoir level, the downstream cost of the prerelease itself, what happens after false alarms (refill shortfall), or how the rule performs in smaller, larger, or back-to-back floods. A rule that cuts peak outflow by releasing earlier can raise the maximum level when the forecast is wrong, and it can create man-made flows downstream when no flood comes.

My provisional recommendation is to keep the current rule as the legal basis. Have the analysis redone with the corrections below, and if the corrected results still hold, run the new rule in shadow operation for at least one flood season before any change to the operating manual.

## The questions that decide the recommendation

- **Q1. Does the benefit survive a realistic error model for large floods at 2–3 day lead?** This is the main question. If it does not, nothing else matters.
- **Q2. Does the new rule stay within the dam-safety and downstream standards in its worst plausible cases, not just on average?** That means maximum level under design and check floods, behavior when the forecast misses, and downstream flows caused by prerelease. Safety cannot be traded against a lower average peak outflow.
- **Q3. Is an 18% reduction physically possible given the storage and release capacity that prerelease can actually free up in the lead time?** Check this by mass balance before trusting any statistics.

## 1. What the "95% confidence" claim can and cannot mean

The firm needs to state which of three things it means:

- (a) In 95% of the 5,000 runs, the new rule's peak outflow was lower than the current rule's.
- (b) A 95% interval for the mean reduction excludes zero, or is centered on 18%.
- (c) 18% is a lower bound reached in 95% of runs.

These are very different claims. Under (a), the 18% might be a mean, while 5% of runs (about 250) show the new rule doing worse. Those are exactly the runs where the forecast missed, and their size has not been reported.

Whichever it is, the stated confidence covers only Monte Carlo variation under the assumed error model. With 5,000 draws, sampling noise is negligible. For a proportion near 0.95, the standard error is √(0.95 × 0.05 / 5000) ≈ 0.003. So the "95%" is precisely estimated, but it answers a narrow question: *if real forecast errors were pooled, normal and independent, and the 100-year flood had exactly this shape and started from exactly this level, how often would the new rule win?* Uncertainty in the error model itself, which is the dominant term, is not included at all. The claim should be restated as conditional on the model, or the confidence should be recomputed with error-model uncertainty included.

The 12-year record also supports less than it appears to. The chance that a 12-year record contains even one flood of 100-year size or larger is 1 − 0.99¹² ≈ 11%. So the error statistics almost certainly contain no event of the size being evaluated. Errors at that size are extrapolated from much smaller floods. Twelve years also hold at most about 12 annual flood peaks, and probably only a handful of large, independent flood events. That is too few to estimate how forecast errors behave at the tail, whatever distribution is fitted.

## 2. Problems in the error model, with direction and rough size

| Simplification | Why it matters | Direction of effect on the claimed benefit |
|---|---|---|
| **Pooled across flow size** | Absolute errors usually grow with flow. A pooled fit is dominated by many low- and medium-flow days, so its σ is too small for flood flows. Relative errors may also differ between regimes. If σ in floods is k times the pooled σ, every scenario's error is understated by a factor k. | Overstates the benefit and understates the miss risk. |
| **Pooled across lead time** | Error grows with lead time. A single σ overstates day-1 error and understates day-3 error. Prerelease decisions depend on the day-2 and day-3 forecasts, because the reservoir has to start releasing early enough. | Understates error exactly where the rule depends on it, so it overstates the benefit. |
| **Pooled across season** | Errors in the flood season (convective or typhoon rain, wet antecedent conditions) are usually larger and more skewed than in the dry season. Dry-season days dilute σ. | Overstates the benefit. |
| **Normal, zero-mean** | Flood forecast errors are often skewed. Systematic underforecasting of large peaks and volumes, plus timing errors, are common. A symmetric normal with near-zero pooled mean hides any flood-time bias, and its thin tails underweight large misses. An additive normal can also produce negative forecast flows. | Overstates the benefit and understates tail risk. Bias toward underforecasting is particularly harmful: the rule prereleases too little and too late. |
| **Independent day-to-day sampling** | Real errors are strongly correlated across lead days within one forecast (one storm placement error affects all three days), and across successive forecast issues. Independent sampling lets errors cancel when they are summed over the 3-day window. For n = 3 days with equal σ and pairwise correlation ρ, the variance of the 3-day volume error is 3σ²(1 + 2ρ), not 3σ². With ρ = 0.7 (an assumed, plausible value for errors within the same event), the true SD is √(1 + 1.4) ≈ 1.55 times what the firm simulated. The model understates 3-day volume error by about 35%. | Strongly overstates the benefit. Prerelease is a volume decision, so 3-day volume error is the quantity that matters. |
| **Independent successive forecasts** | In real operation the rule sees a forecast issued each day, and consecutive issues are consistent with each other. If sampled independently, a rolling rule sees forecasts that jump around. Depending on the rule's logic, this can either hurt or help it in an unrealistic way. | Unknown direction. It still needs to be fixed. |

These biases add up in the same direction: the synthetic forecasts are more accurate than real flood-time forecasts. Combining just two of the effects (flood σ about 1.5 times the pooled σ, and correlation raising volume SD by about 1.5) suggests true volume error at 3-day lead could be around twice what was simulated. These are illustrative assumptions, not measured values. At that error level, the probability that the rule under-prereleases or prereleases for a flood that does not come could be several times higher than the firm's runs show. Whether the 18% shrinks a little or disappears cannot be known without redoing the analysis.

## 3. Problems in the scenario design

- **One design hydrograph.** The 100-year flood is presumably a single typical hydrograph scaled to the 100-year volume or peak. Peak timing, single versus double peaks, and volume-to-peak ratio all change how much a prerelease helps. A rule tuned to one shape can do worse with another, for example a late second peak after the reservoir has already used its storage.
- **Initial water level.** It is not stated. The benefit of prerelease depends strongly on the starting level. If both rules started at the flood-limited level, the result does not apply when the reservoir is already above it, which is when it matters most.
- **Single flood magnitude.** A 100-year result says nothing about:
  - smaller, frequent floods, where prerelease may create downstream flows the natural flood would not have produced, and may leave the reservoir unable to refill;
  - the design and check floods for dam safety (and the PMF if it applies), where the question is maximum level and spillway adequacy, not peak outflow;
  - false alarms: forecasts of a big flood that does not come.

  A design-flood evaluation is conditioned on a flood occurring, so it can never show the cost of acting on a wrong forecast.
- **Whether the forecasts represent the system that will be used.** The 12 years of forecasts need to come from the same forecast system, version and configuration that would drive the rule operationally. Archived operational forecasts and reforecasts made later with an improved model have different error properties.
- **How errors are applied.** Additive or multiplicative errors, on daily flows or on volumes, and whether the "observed" path is the design hydrograph: these choices need to be documented. A multiplicative error on a 100-year hydrograph yields very different absolute spreads than an additive pooled σ.

## 4. Physical plausibility check (mass balance)

The firm should show the volume that the 18% requires and where it comes from. The arithmetic is simple. Lowering peak outflow by ΔQ over the part of the hydrograph where outflow would otherwise exceed the new peak requires extra storage of about ΔQ × (duration of that peak segment), minus any volume already freed by prerelease.

As an illustration only (none of these flows are given), suppose the current rule's 100-year peak outflow is 4,000 m³/s. An 18% cut is 720 m³/s. If that cut has to be held over about 2 days of the peak, the extra storage needed is about 720 × 172,800 s ≈ 125 million m³. That is about 21% of the reservoir's total 600 million m³. Only the flood-control part of the 600 million m³ is relevant here, and it may be much smaller. Prerelease must create that space within the lead time. At a net extra release (release minus inflow) of 500 m³/s, it takes 125 × 10⁶ / 500 ≈ 250,000 s ≈ 2.9 days. That is essentially the entire 3-day forecast horizon, and it only works if the forecast triggers at day 3 and the downstream channel can carry the extra 500 m³/s on top of the rising flows.

The point is not these numbers but the test. The firm must report:

- the flood-control storage;
- the 100-year flood volume and peak;
- the outlet capacity at the relevant levels;
- the downstream safe discharge;
- the volume actually prereleased in the median run and in the worst runs.

It should then show that the 18% is consistent with these. If the required volume exceeds what can be released within the effective lead time at the safe downstream flow, the result is an artifact of the model or of the error model.

The comparison also needs to rule out a baseline that is artificially poor. Was the current rule simulated with the same simulator, constraints, starting level and paired scenarios, and is it encoded as actually operated? Does the current rule already use forecasts informally? If the baseline is driven by a constraint the new rule is allowed to ignore (for example, a ramping or downstream limit), the gain is not a real rule improvement.

## 5. Problems with the metric and risk scope

- **Maximum outflow is one indicator.** The new rule must be shown not to increase:
  - the maximum reservoir level and the probability of exceeding the design and check flood levels;
  - the duration of flows above the downstream safe capacity;
  - downstream flows during the prerelease phase above what the natural flood would have produced.
- **Tail, not average.** Dam safety cannot be traded off. The test must cover the worst runs (for example, the 99th percentile of maximum level, or the probability of exceeding the safety level), compared with the current rule's risk as the acceptable benchmark. A lower median peak outflow does not compensate for a higher probability of overtopping risk or emergency spillway use.
- **Costs when the flood does not come.** These include water released on false alarms, the probability of failing to refill to the normal level by the end of the flood season, and lost generation or supply. A design-flood-only study cannot see them.
- **Operational limits.** The rule must still work with one gate out of service, actual discharge capacity at the actual head, ramping limits for downstream safety and bank stability, drawdown-rate limits for the dam and reservoir slopes, and level-measurement error.

## 6. What I would require before recommending adoption

In order of priority:

1. **Rebuild the error model from the forecast–observation pairs.**
   - Stratify by lead time (1, 2, 3 days), by flow regime (flood flows above a threshold, separately) and by season (flood season only).
   - Test for bias and skew in flood-time errors. Use relative or heteroscedastic errors if absolute σ grows with flow.
   - Fit candidate distributions and keep a heavier-tailed or skewed one if normality is rejected.
   - Estimate correlation across lead times within a forecast and between successive issues, and generate errors jointly (multivariate or copula-based, or by resampling whole historical error trajectories from flood events).
   - Report the 3-day volume error SD actually simulated against the one observed in flood events.
2. **Validate the synthetic forecasts against the record before using them.** Check that observed flood-event errors fall inside the synthetic ranges at the expected rate, using coverage, rank histograms by lead time, and the largest events checked individually. If they do not, the error model is not fit for purpose.
3. **Replay the historical floods with the actual archived forecasts.** Run both rules in closed loop with the real forecasts on every significant flood of the 12 years, and compare trajectories event by event. This uses no error model at all and is the most convincing evidence available. It will be thin, but it should agree with the synthetic results.
4. **Broaden the scenario set.**
   - Several hydrograph shapes for the 100-year flood, including double peaks and late peaks.
   - A range of starting levels.
   - Smaller floods (for example 5-, 10- and 20-year), the design and check floods for dam safety, and back-to-back floods.
   - Non-flood periods with false-alarm forecasts.
5. **Bracket the result with information-condition baselines.** Run both rules with no forecast and with a perfect forecast, so it is clear how much of the 18% comes from the rule's logic and how much depends on forecast quality. Then run a stress test that inflates forecast-error σ (for example by 1.5× and 2×) and adds correlation and an underforecast bias. Report the error level at which the new rule stops beating the current one. If that break-even point lies within the plausible range of real flood-time errors, the rule is not robust.
6. **Report risk measures, not just a central estimate.**
   - Distribution of the reduction (median, 5th and 95th percentiles).
   - The share of runs where the new rule is worse, and by how much.
   - Probability of exceeding the design and check flood levels and the downstream safe discharge under each rule.
   - Downstream flows caused by prerelease.
   - Refill probability.

   Express uncertainty in the error-model parameters (for example by bootstrapping over flood events in the 12-year record) so the confidence statement includes it.
7. **Check by mass balance and constraints.** Show the volumes released, stored and refilled; that the outlet and downstream limits hold throughout; and that the result holds with one gate unavailable.
8. **Verify the simulator.** It should reproduce historical levels and releases under the current rule as actually operated, and the baseline encoding should be confirmed by the operators.
9. **Disclose the forecast system.** Confirm that the 12-year forecasts come from the same system and configuration that will be used operationally. If the forecast system changed during the period, test the two segments separately.

## 7. Adoption path if the corrected analysis supports the rule

- **Agree acceptance criteria in advance with the dam-safety authority and the body responsible for flood control.** For example: no increase in the probability of exceeding design and check flood levels; no prerelease that raises downstream flow above the natural flood or the safe discharge; refill probability not below an agreed value; a peak-outflow benefit that remains positive at the upper end of the plausible error range.
- **Approval instrument.** Adoption means revising the operating manual or the annual flood-control plan. The current rule stays the legal basis until the competent authority approves the change. The owner cannot adopt it alone. Approval timing depends on the jurisdiction, which the question does not name.
- **Shadow operation.** Run the new rule in parallel for at least one flood season. Record what it would have released and compare that with what happened, before giving it control of the gates.
- **Reversion triggers and audit.** Revert to the current rule if the forecast is missing or late, diverges from observed inflow beyond an agreed threshold, or if equipment fails. Specify who decides, and log every forecast, decision and deviation.
- **Communication and training.** Prereleases are man-made rises in flow. Downstream communities, emergency services and other operators must be warned under an agreed protocol, and operators need training and version control for the rule.
- **After-event review.** Review each significant flood under the new rule and compare it with the counterfactual.

## What would change my view

- **Toward adoption:** a stratified, correlated, validated error model that still gives a clearly positive reduction; a historical replay with archived forecasts that agrees; no increase in maximum-level or downstream exceedance risk in the tails or in false-alarm cases; and a break-even error level well above the plausible real error.
- **Against adoption:** a benefit that shrinks to near zero, or reverses, once errors are inflated to realistic flood-time levels; a higher maximum level or more prerelease-caused downstream exceedances in the worst runs; or a mass balance showing that the required volume cannot be released within the effective lead time at the safe downstream flow.

Key assumptions in this review: the flood-control storage, design peak flows, outlet capacity and downstream safe discharge are not given, so the volume figures above are illustrative. The correlation value (ρ = 0.7) and the 1.5× flood-time σ factor are assumed planning values used to show the order of magnitude. They need to be replaced with values estimated from the firm's own forecast–observation pairs.

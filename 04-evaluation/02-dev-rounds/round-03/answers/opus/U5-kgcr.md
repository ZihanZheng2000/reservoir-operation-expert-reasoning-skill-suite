# Recommendation to the flood-control headquarters

## Bottom line

**Conditional preferred alternative: (a), in a moderate, upper-led form.** The upper reservoir starts a controlled evacuation of its occupied flood-control storage now. The lower reservoir raises its release only modestly, to finish emptying its own small occupied volume and then to pass on the upper's extra outflow. City flow stays near 3,000–3,500 m³/s, well below the 6,000 m³/s safe flow. All of this is re-decided at the 12-hour forecast update.

- **(b) is dominated.** The lower reservoir is not the constraint. At its current release it will be fully empty in about 30 hours, long before the upper flood reaches it (about hour 48). (b) raises city flows for no gain and does nothing for the reservoir that is actually short of space.
- **(c) is feasible but second-best.** Waiting is not dangerous if the forecast timing holds. Its cost is that the same evacuation would later have to be done faster, with steeper ramps, closer to the flood, and with no margin if the flood arrives early.

The recommendation depends on one key assumption. The water now occupying 60% of the upper's flood-control storage is water that the operating rule requires to be evacuated (flood-zone water), not water the operators are authorized to keep for supply or power. If that assumption is wrong, the cost of releasing it changes, and (c) becomes the preferred choice for the upper (see triggers below).

## The deciding questions and the arithmetic

Three questions decide the choice:

1. **Is there a volume problem at all, or only a peak-timing problem?**
2. **Which reservoir is short of space, and does the direction of the right action depend on where the forecast lands in its 150–400 million m³ range?**
3. **What does waiting 12 hours cost? That is, what is each option's point of no return?**

Conversions used: 1,000 m³/s sustained for 1 hour = 3.6 million m³; 72 h = 259,200 s.

Both reservoirs are therefore *already* releasing more than they receive. Option (c) is not "do nothing". It continues an evacuation that is already under way.

### Is there a volume problem? (question 1)

- Flood volume forecast: 250 (150–400) million m³ over 72 h. As a mean flow, that is 965 (580–1,540) m³/s.
- **This needs immediate clarification.** If 250 million m³ were the *total* inflow, the mean (965 m³/s) would be *below* the current inflow of 1,200 m³/s, which is not a flood. I therefore read it as volume *above the current base flow*. On that reading, total upper inflow over the 72 h would be about 311 + 250 ≈ 560 million m³ (range 460–710), a mean of about 2,170 m³/s (range 1,780–2,740). Peaks would be higher: perhaps 4,000–6,000 m³/s for a broad hydrograph, more for a peaky one. The hydrograph shape is not available.
- Joint free storage at hour 30, even with no action: about 152 + 500 ≈ **650 million m³**. That exceeds the *upper bound* of the flood volume (400). The extra tributary contribution would add roughly 130 million m³ for a 24 h storm at +1,500 m³/s, or 260 for 48 h. Even then, the system can absorb the worst stacked case only if it released almost nothing, and in reality it can send up to 6,000 m³/s to the city (about 1,550 million m³ over 72 h).
- **Conclusion: by mass balance, none of the three options is required for volume.** The real threats are (i) the upper reservoir's own small margin and (ii) the coincidence of upper release, tributary peak, and city flow, which depends on hydrograph shape and tributary timing. Neither is known yet.

### Which reservoir is short? (question 2)

- The **upper** is the tight one. Its free space at arrival (≈ 152 with no action) is below the central flood volume (250) and far below the upper end (400). In *every* forecast case except the very low end (150), the upper will have to pass 100–250 million m³ into the lower *during* the flood, on top of its base flow.
- The **lower** has ≈ 500 free at arrival. That alone exceeds the central forecast volume. Its role is to be the last buffer for whatever arrives from the upper and from the tributary.
- **The direction of the upper's action does not change anywhere in the forecast range; only its size does.** That is the main reason to act on the upper now rather than wait. Moving water out of the upper *before* the flood, while the tributary is at 300 m³/s and the channel is calm, is better than moving it *during* the flood, when its arrival at the lower dam can coincide with a tributary peak of up to 1,800 m³/s.
- Upper water evacuated now moves into the lower. That is close to neutral for the system's total free storage. Its value is (a) the upper's own dam-safety margin and (b) controllability: an emptier upper can hold back its release while the tributary peaks, so the two flows do not stack at the lower dam.

### What does waiting cost? Points of no return (question 3)

- **Proposed moderate (a).** The upper ramps over about 6 h from 1,500 to about 2,500–3,000 m³/s (net 1,300–1,800 m³/s, or 4.7–6.5 million m³/h). By hour 30 its free storage reaches **≈ 250–300 million m³**, up from 152, and at 3,000 m³/s it is essentially empty after about 28–29 h. That covers the central flood volume within the upper itself.
- **Under (c), deciding at hour 12.** Upper free storage is then ≈ 133 and occupied ≈ 167, with 18 h left.
  - Reaching the same state by hour 30 requires about 167 / 18 h ≈ 9.3 million m³/h, i.e. net ≈ 2,600 m³/s. That means an upper release of **≈ 3,800–4,200 m³/s** with steeper ramps.
  - That water reaches the lower dam from about hour 30 onward. Then the tributary may be rising, and 4,200 + 1,800 = 6,000 m³/s at the lower dam is right at the city's safe flow, so the lower would have to store.
  - If the flood arrives early (say hour 24), only about 12 h remain, and the upper can reach only about 133 + 130 ≈ 260 even at 4,200 m³/s.
- **Point of no return for the upper's full evacuation.** At an aggressive 6,000 m³/s (net 4,800, or 17.3 million m³/h), emptying 180 million m³ takes about 10.4 h. With arrival at hour 30, the latest start is about hour 19–20; with arrival at hour 24, about hour 13–14, which is essentially at the next forecast. **Waiting therefore uses up most of the timing margin if the arrival forecast is early, and leaves only aggressive, disruptive releases.**
- **Lower.** It has no meaningful point of no return. It is close to empty by hour 30 under any option, and the upper flood does not reach it until about hour 48.
- **City.** Lower releases take 10 h to reach the city, and releases already made will still arrive. Under moderate (a) the city sees 3,000–3,500 m³/s, leaving a margin of about 2,500 m³/s to the safe flow. That margin is what absorbs an unexpected tributary rise while the lower cuts back.

### Cost if the flood does not come

- **Upper.** If the released water is flood-zone water, it would have had to be evacuated anyway. There is no refill loss, and the cost is only the timing. If instead it is authorized carry-over storage, releasing it is a real loss, and the release should be capped at what the low-side forecast (150 million m³) could refill.
- **Downstream.** City flow rises from 2,500 to about 3,000–3,500 m³/s, which is man-made but below the safe flow. The reach between the dams carries about 2,500–3,300 m³/s, higher than now. Both require warnings.

## Actions to take now (within the hour)

1. **Upper reservoir.** Begin a staged ramp from 1,500 toward 2,500 m³/s over about 6 h, within the permitted ramping and drawdown rates for dam slopes and banks. Hold the decision to go to 3,000 m³/s until gate availability and ramp limits are confirmed. Target: upper free storage of about 250 million m³ or more by hour 30.
2. **Lower reservoir.** Raise the release modestly, from 2,500 to about 3,000 m³/s. This finishes emptying its occupied 75 million m³ (about 17 h at net 1,200 m³/s). From about hour 18, when the upper's increase arrives, match the release to inflow so the lower stays at or near 100% free. Do not exceed about 3,500 m³/s at the city during this pre-flood phase.
3. **Information requests, urgent.**
   - Ask the forecast center to confirm whether 250 (150–400) million m³ is total inflow or volume above base flow, and what probability the range represents.
   - Request the best available upper-inflow hydrograph shape and peak, the timing uncertainty on "about 30 h", and any quantitative or qualitative tributary rainfall outlook.
   - Ask whether interim (for example 6-hourly) updates are possible.
4. **Check the system state.**
   - Gate and outlet availability at both dams, including the case of one gate out of service.
   - Discharge capacity at the actual heads.
   - Level-gauge reliability.
   - Staff and power for continuous operation.
   - Local inflow between the lower dam and the city, and the current stage at the city.
5. **Warnings.**
   - Notify downstream authorities, river users between the dams and below the lower dam, emergency services, and the city flood office about the man-made increase in releases and the expected flows.
   - Inform power or other dispatch authorities affected by the release changes.
6. **Authority and record.** Headquarters issues the instruction under the current flood-control plan. It records the basis (this analysis and its assumptions), who approved it, and the reversion triggers below. All gate changes and observed flows are logged for after-event review.
7. **Set up the event plan for the update.** Run a simple routed water-balance check of both reservoirs, the 18 h and 10 h lags, and three tributary timings:
   - tributary peak *before* the upper's flood water reaches the lower (before about hour 48);
   - *coincident* with it (about hours 48–80);
   - *after* it.

   In each case, set the upper's in-flood release cap so that upper release + tributary ≤ about 6,000 m³/s at the lower dam whenever the lower would otherwise have to store heavily, and check that the city stays ≤ 6,000 m³/s.

## What can wait for the 12-hour update

- Whether to go from 2,500 to 3,000 m³/s or more at the upper, or back off.
- The in-flood release rule: how much the upper passes versus stores, and how the 6,000 m³/s city limit is shared between upper release and tributary flow.
- Any city-level public warning beyond operational notifications.

From then on, plan over the whole event but commit only to the next 12 h, and recompute at each update (hours 12 and 24, both before arrival).

## Triggers that would change the recommendation

**Move toward (c), or slow the upper evacuation:**

- The occupied upper flood-control storage turns out to be water the operators are authorized to keep for supply or power. In that case, cap the upper release so the low-side forecast (150 million m³) can refill what is released, and lean to (c). A 12 h wait is still within the point of no return if the arrival forecast holds.
- The next forecast lowers the upper bound to about 150 million m³ or less, close to the upper's free space at arrival with current releases. Return the upper to its normal evacuation pace and the lower to about 2,500 m³/s.
- Ramp, drawdown, or bank-stability limits, or gate outages, prevent the planned ramp.
- Downstream users between the dams or below the lower dam cannot be warned in time.

**Escalate (stronger version of (a)):**

- The forecast upper bound rises above about 400, or the central value rises above about 300 million m³ (the upper's entire flood-control storage). Evacuate the upper fully, at up to about 4,000 m³/s, before arrival, keep the lower at 100% free, and prepare city warnings.
- The forecast arrival moves earlier than about 24 h, or upper inflow is observed rising before the forecast. Go to full evacuation immediately, since the point of no return is then at or before the next update.
- A tributary forecast shows a rise of more than about 1,000 m³/s coinciding with the upper flood's arrival at the lower dam (about hours 48–80). Empty the upper further, so it can hold back during the tributary peak and keep upper release + tributary ≤ about 6,000 m³/s.
- The combined flood volume (upper flood plus tributary storm) approaches the joint free storage (about 650 million m³) *after* allowing for what can be passed at 6,000 m³/s, or a peaky hydrograph implies upper inflow above its 8,000 m³/s release capacity. Treat this as an emergency branch: protect dam safety first, and give the city early evacuation warning.

**Abort and correction rules during evacuation:**

- If the flood arrives early while the upper is drawing down, the upper switches immediately to flood-control mode. Its release is capped so that, combined with the tributary, it does not overload the lower. The water already released is absorbed by the lower, which has the room.
- If a tributary storm starts while elevated upper releases are in transit, the lower stores rather than passes. Its release cuts take 10 h to reach the city, so the 2,500 m³/s margin at the city must be kept until the tributary is known.
- If a forecast is late or missing, revert to the current rule, with releases based on observed levels and inflows, and keep the city ≤ 6,000 m³/s.

## Most important missing information and its effect

| Missing item | Effect on the decision |
|---|---|
| Whether 250 million m³ is total or above base flow | Changes total upper inflow from about 250 to about 560 million m³. It does not flip (a) versus (c), but it sets how far the upper must evacuate. |
| Status of the occupied upper flood-control storage (must be evacuated, or authorized to keep) | **The only item that can flip the choice between (a) and (c).** It determines whether releasing now has any real cost. |
| Upper inflow hydrograph and peak | Determines whether the upper must release more than about 4,200 m³/s during the flood (risk of stacking with the tributary at the lower dam) and whether its 8,000 m³/s capacity is ever binding. |
| Tributary forecast and timing | Determines the in-flood release cap for the upper and how much of the lower's storage is used. Cannot be resolved now. It is the main reason to finish the upper evacuation *before* the flood window. |
| Timing uncertainty of "about 30 h" | Sets the point of no return. An earlier arrival makes waiting (c) costly. |
| Local inflow below the lower dam, ramp limits, gate status | Lowers the usable city margin and the achievable evacuation rate, and could cap the moderate (a). |

## Key assumptions

- "Flood-control storage" means the zone above the flood-limited level, so occupied water there is meant to be evacuated outside a flood.
- The upper release has been about 1,500 m³/s for the past 18 h, so the lower inflow is about 1,800 m³/s.
- Local inflow between the lower dam and the city is small, so city flow ≈ lower release lagged 10 h.
- Travel times (18 h and 10 h) are roughly constant over the flow range considered.
- No other constraints on the channel between the dams below about 6,000 m³/s.
- The 150–400 million m³ range is a plausible, not absolute, range. Volumes beyond 400 are not excluded, which is why the escalation triggers exist.

With these assumptions, the information available does support a single provisional choice: moderate, upper-led (a). The item that could overturn it is the status of the water held in the upper's flood-control storage. Headquarters should confirm that first, together with the meaning of the forecast volume.

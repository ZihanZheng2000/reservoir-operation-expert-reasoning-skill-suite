# Recommendation: two reservoirs in series, decision due within 1 hour

## 1. Bottom line (provisional, conditional)

**Preferred alternative: a moderate, staged, reversible version of (a). Both reservoirs start coordinated prerelease now. The upper reservoir is the priority, and releases are capped so the city stays well below 6,000 m³/s even if the tributary surges. Review every forecast update, and earlier if the triggers below are hit.**

- (b) gives almost no benefit over what is already happening. Every benefit it does give is included in (a).
- (c) is the fallback. It becomes preferred if the key assumption in Section 3 (A1) fails, meaning the water now held in the upper flood-control pool has real value or must not be released.

This is a conditional single choice, not a confident one. The margin between a moderate (a) and (c) is not large in volume terms. What tips the choice toward (a) is that the costs and risks are lopsided (Section 4).

## 2. Quick quantitative screening (from the numbers given only)

**Free flood-control storage now**
- Upper: 40% × 300 = **120 million m³** free. 180 million m³ is occupied.
- Lower: 85% × 500 = **425 million m³** free. 75 million m³ is occupied.
- System total: about 545 million m³ free.

**What current operations already do**
- Upper: release 1,500 against inflow 1,200 gives a net drawdown of 300 m³/s, about 1.08 million m³/h. That adds about 32 million m³ free storage over 30 h, so about 152 million m³ free when the flood arrives.
- Lower: inflow is about 1,500 (upper release, 18 h lag) + 300 (tributary) = 1,800. Release is 2,500, so the net drawdown is about 700 m³/s, about 2.5 million m³/h. The remaining 75 million m³ is cleared in about 30 h even with no action.
  - **So the lower reservoir is already prereleasing in effect. Option (b) only speeds up recovery of at most 75 million m³.**

**Upper storage compared with the flood**
- The upper reservoir's free storage (120 to 152 million m³) is at or below even the **low** end of the forecast (150 million m³). It is far below the central estimate (250) and the high end (400).
- The upper dam will therefore have to pass a large share of the flood to the lower reservoir in every scenario. The system depends on the lower reservoir's reserve, and that same reserve must also absorb the uncontrolled tributary.
- The occupied 180 million m³ in the upper pool is where the extra protection is.

**Timing windows**
- Flood reaches the upper reservoir at about t+30 h. Upper-reservoir floodwater reaches the lower dam at about t+48 h or later, and the city at about t+58 h or later.
- Water released from the upper dam in the next 12 to 30 h reaches the lower dam at t+18 to 48 h and the city at t+28 to 58 h. That is **before** the upper flood itself.
  - So upper prerelease does not overlap with the upper flood peak.
  - Its only possible overlap is with a tributary rise, and the lower reservoir's large free storage can absorb that.
- This "clear the channel ahead of the flood" window closes at about t+30 h. Waiting 12 h cuts it from 30 h to 18 h.

**City safety during prerelease (worst-case tributary)**
- Suppose the upper releases 3,000 m³/s and the tributary jumps to its 1,500 m³/s maximum. If the lower reservoir simply passed everything, the city would get about 4,500 m³/s. That is below 6,000 with about 1,500 m³/s margin.
- So a capped upper prerelease of 2,500 to 3,000 m³/s is safe for the city **even with no help from lower-reservoir storage**. In practice the lower reservoir can also hold water back.

**Volume adequacy under (a)**
- A rough example: upper release about 2,500 m³/s from now until t+30 h gives a net 1,300 m³/s, about 140 million m³ more free storage. The upper reservoir would then be about 85–87% free (about 260 million m³) at flood arrival.
- The lower reservoir would be essentially 100% free (about 500 million m³).
- Total free storage would be about 760 million m³ against an upper flood of 150–400 million m³ plus the tributary excess. A sustained +1,200 m³/s for 24–48 h is about 100–210 million m³.
- With the lower reservoir able to release up to about 6,000 m³/s minus any local inflow, this leaves a comfortable margin even at the upper bound.

**Volume adequacy under (c)**
- The system would still have about 650 million m³ free at flood arrival if current releases continue. That is probably enough for the central case.
- But the upper reservoir has only about 150 million m³ to work with. Recovering the gap after the next forecast means upper releases of about 3,100–3,300 m³/s over the last 18 h, which:
  - is later and steeper;
  - is closer in time to a possible tributary rise;
  - stays exposed if the next forecast is late or still very uncertain.

**A caution on the forecast figure**
- 250 million m³ over 72 h averages about 965 m³/s, which is *below* the current upper inflow of 1,200 m³/s.
- The 250 (150–400) is therefore almost certainly a flood volume **above base flow**, or a different accounting basis. This must be confirmed (Section 6).
- If it really were total inflow, the event would be mild and (c) would become more attractive.

## 3. Key assumptions

- **A1 (decisive):** The occupied 60% of the upper reservoir's flood-control storage is above the flood-limit level. Operating rules call for evacuating it anyway, so releasing it does not use conservation (water-supply or hydropower) storage. Its regret cost if the flood turns out small is low. **If this is false, the case for (a) weakens considerably.**
- **A2:** Prerelease is stopped at the flood-limit level. Neither reservoir is drawn into conservation storage on the basis of this uncertain forecast.
- **A3:** Local inflow between the lower dam and the city is small compared with the 6,000 m³/s safe flow, or can be added once known. City flow is roughly lower-reservoir release (10 h lag) plus local inflow.
- **A4:** The channel between the dams and below the lower dam can carry 2,500–4,500 m³/s without significant damage. There are no people, works or navigation that would be affected at these flows, or they can be warned and cleared within a few hours.
- **A5:** Gates are available, and the 8,000 m³/s capacities hold at the water levels expected. At lower heads, discharge capacity may be smaller, which argues for evacuating early rather than late.
- **A6:** There is no hard limit on how fast releases may be raised that would stop a ramp to about 2,500–3,000 m³/s within a few hours.
- **A7:** The tributary storm may arrive before, with, or after the upper flood. Its timing is treated as unknown, which is why the lower reservoir's reserve is protected first.

## 4. Why (a)-moderate over (b) and (c)

**Asymmetric regret.**
- If the flood is small (about 150) and we prereleased, the loss is small under A1. The water was flood-pool water that would have been evacuated anyway; what is lost is mainly some hydropower head and the operational effort.
- If the flood is large (about 400) or the tributary surges and we waited, two things go wrong:
  - The upper reservoir enters the flood with only about 150 million m³ free against up to 400, so it must pass high flows early.
  - That leaves the lower reservoir to absorb both the upper pass-through and the uncontrolled tributary.
- Catching up later requires steeper releases closer to the storm.

**The upper reservoir is the binding element.** The lower reservoir is already 85% free and empties itself in about 30 h at current releases. The system lacks free storage in the upper reservoir, and only (a) addresses that.

**(a) needs both reservoirs.** If only the upper reservoir prereleases, its extra water would partly fill the lower reservoir and simply move the problem downstream. The lower reservoir must raise its release when the upper reservoir's prerelease arrives (about t+18 h) so that it reaches and holds the flood-limit level.

**(b) is dominated.** It gains at most 75 million m³ that is already being recovered. It does nothing about the upper reservoir's shortfall. Every benefit of (b) is included in (a).

**(c) has real merit but costs the most useful window.** Its merit is avoiding action on a forecast that is uncertain by a factor of about 2.7. But it spends 12 of the 30 hours available for safe upper evacuation.

**(a) can be undone.** Moderate rates can be cut back at the next update if the forecast drops, so most of (c)'s option value is kept.

## 5. Immediate actions (within the hour and the next 12 h)

**1. Upper reservoir**
- Begin a ramp from 1,500 toward about **2,500 m³/s**, with an approved ceiling of about 3,000 m³/s, over a few hours, within ramp-rate rules.
- Target: about 85–90% free (roughly 255–270 million m³) by t+30 h. Never draw below the flood-limit level.

**2. Lower reservoir**
- Raise release now to about **3,000–3,500 m³/s**. That keeps city flow ≤ about 3,500 plus local inflow and clears the remaining 75 million m³ in about 12–15 h.
- From about t+18 h, release about what flows in (about 2,800 m³/s with upper at 2,500) to hold the flood-limit level.
- Plan the lower release so that city flow stays ≤ about **4,500–5,000 m³/s** (75–85% of the safe flow) during the prerelease phase. That keeps margin for local inflow, gauge error and a tributary surge.

**3. Warnings and readiness**
- Issue downstream notices for raised flows between the dams and below the lower dam.
- Confirm that gates, power supply and staff are ready at both dams for flood operations.
- Confirm the 8,000 m³/s capacities at current heads.

**4. Information requests (do not wait 12 h for these)**
- Ask the forecasters for a provisional upper inflow hydrograph or ensemble, with peak, timing and the meaning of the 250 figure.
- Ask for any rainfall forecast covering the tributary basin.
- Ask for an interim update if the event is developing fast.
- Put the tributary gauge and the rainfall gauges in its basin on high-frequency (hourly) reporting.

**5. Quick coordinated routing check**
- Before the next forecast, route the three scenarios (150 / 250 / 400 million m³) together with a tributary scenario: base 300, and a surge to 1,500 m³/s for 24–48 h at early, coincident and late timing.
- Use lags of 18 h and 10 h.
- Check that:
  - neither reservoir exceeds its flood pool;
  - the upper release never exceeds 8,000 m³/s;
  - city flow stays ≤ 6,000 m³/s;
  - the cumulative release schedule is consistent across both dams.
- Prepare the flood-phase schedule now:
  - The upper reservoir stores early and passes enough not to fill.
  - The lower reservoir limits release to 6,000 minus local inflow, and stores the upper pass-through and the tributary excess.

**6. Record the decision**
- Log the decision, assumptions A1–A7 and the triggers below, so that the next forecast update is a structured re-decision rather than a fresh debate.

## 6. What would change the recommendation (triggers and thresholds)

**Move to (c), or scale (a) back toward current releases, if:**
- A1 fails. The occupied upper flood-pool water has high supply or power value, or rules forbid evacuating it ahead of a forecast.
- A4 fails. Releases of 2,500–3,500 m³/s would cause real damage or danger between the dams or below the lower dam that cannot be cleared quickly.
- The forecast volume is confirmed to be *total* inflow, so the event is mild. Or the next update cuts the range to about ≤ 200 million m³ with the upper bound ≤ about 250. In that case current releases already give enough system storage (about 650 million m³), and upper prerelease can stop at about 60–70% free.

**Escalate (a) if:**
- The next forecast's central estimate is ≥ about 300 million m³, the upper bound is > 400, or the peak is concentrated (most of the volume within about 24 h, meaning an inflow peak of several thousand m³/s above base). Then:
  - Raise the upper release toward about 3,000–3,500 m³/s to empty the flood pool fully before arrival.
  - Hold the lower reservoir at the flood-limit level.
- The tributary forecast shows a rise to ≥ about 1,000 m³/s during the period when upper prerelease water is passing through the lower reservoir (t+18 to 48 h). Then:
  - Let the lower reservoir store rather than pass that water.
  - Hold the upper release so that upper release + tributary ≤ the lower reservoir's planned release, keeping city flow ≤ about 4,500–5,000 m³/s.
  - The upper reservoir would still prerelease, but tributary control would take priority for the lower reservoir's storage.
- The flood arrives earlier than forecast (upper inflow rising clearly above about 1,500 m³/s before about t+24 h). Then:
  - Stop increasing the upper release.
  - Switch to flood-phase operation.

**Hard limits, at any time:**
- City flow (forecast or observed) approaching about 5,000 m³/s during the prerelease phase: cut back the prerelease.
- Lower free storage projected to fall below the reserve needed for a tributary surge plus the upper pass-through, about 300–400 million m³ until the event shape is known: hold or reduce the upper release.

## 7. Most important missing information and its effect

1. **Tributary forecast (timing and magnitude).** This is the most important gap.
   - The tributary is uncontrolled and goes straight into the lower reservoir and on to the city.
   - It decides how much of the lower reservoir's free storage must be kept for it.
   - It also decides whether upper prerelease water could meet a tributary surge at the lower reservoir.
   - Without it, the recommendation caps upper prerelease conservatively (≤ about 3,000 m³/s) and keeps the lower reservoir at full free storage.
2. **Upper inflow hydrograph: peak, timing and volume basis.**
   - Volume alone does not show whether the upper reservoir must release near 8,000 m³/s, or whether the lower reservoir must hold back a sharp peak.
   - The inconsistency between the 250 figure and the current inflow must be resolved. It could flip the choice toward (c).
3. **Operating rules and the value of flood-pool water (A1, A2, A6).** These decide whether prerelease is low-regret, and so decide between (a) and (c).
4. **Downstream constraints (A3, A4):** local inflow between the lower dam and the city, and the impact of 2,500–4,500 m³/s flows. These set the allowed prerelease rates and the true margin to 6,000 m³/s.
5. **Head-dependent release capacity and gate status (A5).** These determine whether the 8,000 m³/s capacity is actually available at the water levels expected during the flood.

If items 1 and 3 cannot be confirmed within the hour, the recommendation still stands as a **cautious (a)**: moderate rates that keep city flow well below 6,000 m³/s even with a maximum tributary surge, and that can be undone at the next update. Only a clear "no" on A1 or A4 should switch it to (c).

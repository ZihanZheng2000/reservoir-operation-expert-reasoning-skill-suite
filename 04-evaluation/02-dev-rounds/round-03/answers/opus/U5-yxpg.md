# Recommendation on the three prerelease alternatives

## Bottom line

**Provisional preference: alternative (a), in a limited form.** Both reservoirs should start now, but only to empty the water already sitting in their flood-control zones, bringing each back to its flood-limited level before the flood arrives. Neither reservoir should go below the flood-limited level yet. That deeper prerelease is a separate decision and can wait for the 12-hour and 24-hour forecast updates.

The main reason is that this limited form of (a) costs almost nothing whichever way the forecast turns out. Water stored above the flood-limited level is not conservation water. Outside a flood it has to be released anyway. The upper reservoir holds most of that water (60% of its flood-control zone is occupied) and has the least space compared with the flood that is coming. Alternative (b) works on the wrong reservoir, because the lower one is nearly empty and will be completely empty in about 30 hours at its current release. Alternative (c) gives up 12 of the 30 hours of lead time and gains nothing for the no-regret part of the decision. It also pushes the upper reservoir's emptying into larger, later releases that reach the lower reservoir just as the storm is likely to raise the tributary.

The mass balance below shows something the headquarters should be told plainly: **within the forecast range, none of the three alternatives puts the city's 6,000 m³/s limit at serious risk.** This holds as long as my key assumption is true: little local inflow enters between the lower dam and the city. The choice is therefore about safety margin, the upper dam's own margin, and robustness to a flood larger or earlier than forecast. It is not a choice between a safe option and an unsafe one. That is why I prefer the action that costs nothing and leave the costly decision (going below the flood-limited level) for the next forecast.

## The deciding questions

1. **Which reservoir's storage is short compared with the flood, and can it make room in time?** The upper reservoir: 120 million m³ free against a forecast of 250 (range 150–400). The answer is yes, and comfortably, if it starts now.
2. **What actually limits flow at the city?** The lower reservoir's release, plus any local inflow below the lower dam. The tributary enters the **lower reservoir** and does not flow straight to the city. It therefore uses lower-reservoir storage and does not add directly to flow at the city.
3. **Does the action cost anything if the flood is small or does not come?** Not if it stops at the flood-limited level. It does if it goes below.

## Key arithmetic (order of magnitude)

Unit conversions: 1 m³/s for 1 hour = 3,600 m³. 1,000 m³/s for 1 hour = 3.6 million m³.

**Current state**

| | Upper | Lower |
|---|---:|---:|
| Free flood-control storage | 0.40 × 300 = **120 million m³** | 0.85 × 500 = **425 million m³** |
| Occupied flood-control storage | **180 million m³** | **75 million m³** |
| Inflow now | 1,200 m³/s | ≈ 1,500 (upper release, 18 h lag) + 300 (tributary) = **≈ 1,800 m³/s** (assumes upper release was about 1,500 over the past 18 h) |
| Net release now | 1,500 − 1,200 = 300 m³/s | 2,500 − 1,800 = 700 m³/s |
| Emptied by flood arrival (30 h) at current rates | 300 × 30 × 3,600 ≈ 32 million m³, leaving **152 free (51%)** | 700 × 30 × 3,600 ≈ 76 million m³, so **≈ fully empty (≈ 500 free)** |

Both reservoirs are already releasing more than they take in, so a recession or emptying phase is in progress. The lower reservoir is already doing what (b) proposes. The upper reservoir is emptying too slowly to finish before the flood.

**Interpreting the flood volume.** 250 million m³ over 72 h is a mean of 250×10⁶ / 259,200 s ≈ 965 m³/s. That is *below* the current inflow of 1,200 m³/s, so it cannot be the total inflow of a flood. I treat 250 (150–400) million m³ as **flood volume on top of the base flow**, which is the conservative reading. On that reading, total upper inflow over 72 h ≈ 311 (base) + 250 = 561 million m³ in the central case, and 711 million m³ in the high case. The forecasters must confirm this (see missing information).

**What (a) requires at the upper dam.** To empty 180 million m³ before the flood:
- Finishing in 30 h needs a release of 1,200 + 180×10⁶/108,000 ≈ **2,870 m³/s**.
- Finishing in 24 h, which leaves 6 h for an early arrival, needs 1,200 + 180×10⁶/86,400 ≈ **3,300 m³/s**.

Both are well below the 8,000 m³/s capacity. At the flood's start the upper reservoir would then have **≈ 300 million m³ free**, more than the central 250 and 75% of the high 400.

**What it does to the lower reservoir and the city.** The upper reservoir's extra release reaches the lower reservoir from about hour 18 to hour 44. Lower inflow then is ≈ 3,300 + 300 = 3,600 m³/s.
- Suppose the lower release is raised now to about 3,000 m³/s. It then releases (3,000 − 1,800) × 18 h × 3,600 ≈ 78 million m³ by hour 18 and has emptied its 75 million m³ by then.
- After hour 18 it can roughly match its inflow (about 3,600 m³/s), or cap its release at about 4,000 and store the rest.
- Flow at the city becomes ≈ 3,000–4,000 m³/s plus local inflow. That leaves about 2,000–3,000 m³/s of margin below 6,000. It is an increase caused by operation, but it stays far below the safe flow.

**High-flood check under (a).** The upper flood is 400 million m³ above base flow, so total upper inflow over 72 h is 711 million m³.
- If the upper reservoir releases about 2,000–2,500 m³/s on average, it releases 518–648 million m³ and must store about 60–190 million m³. Allowing for the hydrograph's shape, the peak storage needed is roughly 200–300 million m³.
- That fits within the ≈ 300 million m³ it will have free.
- The lower reservoir then receives at most ≈ 2,500 + 1,500 (tributary peak) = 4,000 m³/s. It can pass all of that and stay below 6,000 at the city while keeping ≈ 500 million m³ free in reserve.

**High-flood check under (c), or (b) with the upper unchanged.**
- The upper reservoir enters the flood with only ≈ 152 million m³ free. In the 400 case it must release an average of at least (711 − 152)×10⁶ / 259,200 ≈ 2,160 m³/s, with peaks probably 3,000–5,000 m³/s depending on the hydrograph's shape.
- The lower reservoir receives those peaks plus up to 1,500 from the tributary, so up to roughly 6,500 m³/s. It must store the part above its city-limited release.
- With ≈ 500 million m³ free it can still do so. The city stays protected, but the upper dam runs with a thin margin, and the lower reservoir's reserve for a flood beyond the forecast is used up early.

**How much the system can absorb.**
- If (a) is carried out, the system has ≈ 800 million m³ of free flood-control storage at the start of the flood. Over 72 h it can also release up to about (6,000 − local inflow) × 259,200 s ≈ 1,500 million m³ toward the city.
- Upper inflow (high case: 711 million m³) plus a saturated tributary (at most 1,500 × 259,200 ≈ 390 million m³) totals about 1,100 million m³.
- So by volume the system is not close to failing. What remains at risk is timing (an early or very peaky flood), the upper dam's own level, and local inflow below the lower dam.

## Why (a) over (b) and (c)

| | (a) Both start now (to the flood-limited level) | (b) Lower only | (c) Wait 12 h |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper free storage at flood arrival | ≈ 300 (all) | ≈ 152 | ≈ 152 if still no action; ≈ 300 only if it then releases ≈ 4,000 m³/s for 18 h |
| Lower free storage at flood arrival | ≈ 500 | ≈ 500 | ≈ 500 |
| Upper dam margin in the 400 case | Holds about 75% of the flood | Must pass about 250 million m³, with large peaks | Same as (b), or a rushed late release |
| Coincidence at the lower reservoir | Extra release spread over hours 18–44, at moderate rates | Upper flood peaks pass during the storm | Late large release lands during the likely storm window |
| Cost if the flood is small (150) | None. Only water above the flood-limited level is released | None | None |
| Downstream rise caused by operation | City ≈ 3,000–4,000 m³/s, far below 6,000 | Small | Larger and later if acted on at hour 12 |
| Dominated? | No | Yes, by (a): same lower outcome, worse upper outcome | Yes, by (a) for the no-regret part |

**Point of no return.**
- **Upper reservoir.** At full capacity (net 6,800 m³/s), emptying 180 million m³ takes ≈ 7.4 h, so in principle the decision could wait until about hour 22. In practice, ramping limits, bank-stability limits on drawdown rate, and the safe flow in the reach between the dams cap the release well below 8,000. I assume a practical maximum of about 4,000–5,000 m³/s, which moves the point of no return to **about hours 12–17**. Waiting for the hour-12 forecast therefore leaves almost no slack.
- **Lower reservoir.** No meaningful point of no return. It is nearly empty and its release can be changed within hours.
- **Water already released** keeps travelling: 18 h to the lower dam, then 10 h to the city. For this reason the lower reservoir, not the upper one, is where an early flood is absorbed.

## Immediate actions (within the hour)

1. **Upper reservoir.** Ramp the release from 1,500 to about **3,000–3,300 m³/s** over the next few hours, within the allowed ramping and drawdown rates. The target is the flood-limited level by about hour 24. **Do not go below the flood-limited level** without a further decision.
2. **Lower reservoir.** Raise the release to about **3,000 m³/s** now so the lower reservoir reaches its flood-limited level by about hour 18. From hour 18, when the upper reservoir's extra water arrives, release at about its inflow of 3,600 m³/s.
   - Set an **operating ceiling at the city of about 4,500 m³/s** (75% of the safe flow) until local and tributary forecasts are in hand.
   - Above that ceiling, the lower reservoir stores the excess instead of passing it, using its 425–500 million m³ of space.
3. **Coordination.** Put both dams under one joint schedule run by the headquarters. Each dam's release plan must be shared hourly, because the lower dam's schedule depends on the upper dam's release 18 h earlier.
4. **Warnings.** Notify the city's emergency services, the river reach between the dams, and the reach between the lower dam and the city of the planned rise caused by operation (city flow from about 2,500 to about 3,500–4,000 m³/s from roughly hour 10 onward). Give its expected duration and the fact that it is well below the safe flow.
5. **Information requests, due before the hour-12 update.**
   - Clarify how the flood volume is defined.
   - Obtain the expected peak and shape of the upper inflow hydrograph.
   - Obtain a tributary forecast with timing.
   - Estimate local inflow below the lower dam.
   - Confirm the safe flow in the reach between the dams, the ramping and drawdown-rate limits at both dams, and outlet availability (all gates in service?).
6. **Monitoring.** Watch hourly observed inflow at the upper reservoir and the tributary gauge, comparing them with the forecast to detect an early start.
7. **Log** the decision, its basis, the assumptions, and the triggers below, for audit and for review after the event.

## What can wait for the next forecasts (hour 12, hour 24)

- **Whether to go below the flood-limited level.** On current numbers this is not needed, because the system has a large margin. Reconsider it only under the triggers below.
- **How the lower reservoir shares storage during the flood.** That is, whether it passes the upper reservoir's flood-period releases or holds them. This depends on the tributary forecast.
- **The upper reservoir's release during the flood**, which depends on the shape of the hydrograph.

## Triggers that would change the recommendation

**Toward more aggressive action (going below the flood-limited level, mainly at the upper dam):**
- The forecast's upper bound rises to roughly **≥ 500–600 million m³**. Upper space plus a moderate release would then no longer hold the flood.
- The forecast peak upper inflow is high enough that the upper reservoir, starting at its flood-limited level, would need releases above the safe flow in the reach between the dams.
- The tributary forecast shows a long storm near 1,500 m³/s (more than about 150–200 million m³ above base) that coincides with the upper flood.
- Significant forecast local inflow below the lower dam, which reduces the usable 6,000 at the city.

**Toward slowing down or holding:**
- The next forecast lowers the flood to **≤ 150 million m³** or delays it. Then finish only the emptying already under way, at a gentler rate, and do not prerelease further.
- The flood-control zones turn out to be authorized for temporary conservation use (a dynamic flood-limited level). Then cap the upper reservoir's emptying at what the low-side forecast can refill, about **150 million m³**, instead of all 180.

**Abort and switch to measured-flow operation:**
- Observed upper inflow rises clearly above forecast before hour 24 (for example, a sustained rise past about 2,000 m³/s). Then cut the upper release back toward inflow so the remaining space holds the flood. Water already released is absorbed by the lower reservoir.
- Observed city flow approaches **5,000 m³/s**. Then cut the lower release immediately (10 h travel time) and store in the lower reservoir.
- The forecast update is missing or late, or diverges from observations. Then fall back to rules based on measured conditions for both dams.
- A gate or outlet failure at either dam. Then recompute the schedule with the reduced capacity.

## Key assumptions

- 250 (150–400) million m³ is flood volume above the base flow, not total inflow.
- Local inflow between the lower dam and the city is small compared with the 6,000 m³/s limit.
- The reach between the dams can safely carry about 3,300–4,000 m³/s.
- The occupied flood-control storage at both dams is water above the flood-limited level that the rules require to be emptied. It is not authorized conservation storage.
- The upper reservoir's release over the past 18 h was about 1,500 m³/s, which sets the lower reservoir's inflow now.
- Ramping and drawdown-rate limits allow the stated increases within a few hours.
- The tributary storm and the upper flood are driven by the same weather system and may peak together. I have treated them as coinciding, which is conservative.

## Most important missing information and its effect

1. **Definition of the flood volume and the shape of the upper inflow hydrograph.** If 250 million m³ is total inflow, the flood is minor and (c) is almost as good as (a). If the flood is short and peaky, the upper reservoir needs its full space and (a) becomes more important.
2. **Local inflow below the lower dam.** This is the only flow the reservoirs cannot control before it reaches the city. If it is large, the city ceiling must be lowered and the lower reservoir must store more. That makes (a)'s early emptying even more valuable, because it frees space without adding release during the storm.
3. **Tributary forecast (timing and volume).** This sets how much lower-reservoir space must be kept for it while the upper reservoir's releases are passing through.
4. **Safe flow between the dams and ramping limits.** If these are lower than assumed, the upper reservoir's point of no return moves earlier, which strengthens the case against waiting.

None of these gaps reverses the choice of limited (a). That option does not depend on the flood forecast, because it only releases water that must be released anyway. The gaps decide whether anything beyond it is needed.

## Approval and accountability

The flood-control headquarters authorizes the joint schedule and any departure from the dams' operating rules. Emptying to the flood-limited level is normally within existing rules. Going below it would normally require explicit authorization under the operating plan in force, so it should be prepared now as a contingency and decided at the hour-12 or hour-24 update. Each dam operator remains responsible for its own dam's safety limits. Downstream emergency services and the communities along both reaches must be warned before releases rise, and a review after the event should compare the forecast, the actions taken, and the outcome.

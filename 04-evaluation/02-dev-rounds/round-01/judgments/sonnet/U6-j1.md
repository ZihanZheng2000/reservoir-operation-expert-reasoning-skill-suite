# Judgment U6-j1

## What an excellent answer must contain (judge's own outline)

- Paired differences against the current rule. A: +0.3 bn kWh (+3%), flood equal, irrigation -2 pts, navigation +2 d. B: +0.5 (+5%), flood +0.3 pp (+30% relative), irrigation +3, navigation -3 d. C: +0.2, flood -0.1 pp, irrigation -7, navigation +6 d. No scheme dominates the current rule. C is nearly dominated by A, being better only on the flood indicator, where the gain is negligible.
- A statistical-resolution check. At p = 1% the expected number of exceedances in 40 years is 0.4, against 0.52 at 1.3%. The binomial SE of an empirical estimate is sqrt(0.01*0.99/40), about 1.6 pp, which is several times the 0.3 pp difference. The estimates therefore come from the model tail and the synthetic scenarios, not from the record. Comparisons should use common random numbers and paired intervals.
- Risk translated into terms the commission can use. A 1.0% annual risk is a 100-yr return period, and 1.3% is about 77 yr. Over a 50-yr horizon the chance of at least one exceedance is about 39% under the current rule and about 48% under B. If the city's protection standard is 1-in-100, B falls below it as tabled.
- Flood safety and dam safety treated as constraints, not traded objectives. The approving authority for the flood-limit level must be named. Severity, duration and tail metrics are needed, plus design- and check-flood freeboard.
- Feasibility of a higher flood-limit level: pre-release and drawdown time against forecast lead time, release capacity under the downstream safe flow, refill risk, and a forecast-failure fallback. Variants to test include dynamic or staged flood-limit levels.
- A recommendation: keep the current rule for now. Take forward conditional variants (A as flood-neutral but with water-supply losses, B only if redesigned), with intervals, compensation, a pilot and reversion triggers.
- Stakeholder roles split into decider, safety veto or approval holders, affected parties and advisers. Fix the criteria before the results are seen, manage the proposer's conflict of interest, and use independent review.

## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1
- **KQ:** Finds the deciding questions. It treats flood risk as a constraint, flags that the 40-year record cannot resolve the differences, asks for paired intervals and dam-safety margins, and spots the puzzling A/C mechanisms. It also says who sets the tolerance. The safety-standard implication of 1.3% (about a 77-yr return period) is not settled. The forecast and drawdown feasibility question is raised only as "robustness to forecasts", without lead time or release capacity.
- **TC:** The expected exceedance counts (0.36/0.40/0.52) and all paired differences are correct. One sloppy sentence says every alternative "worsens or leaves unchanged the flood indicator", which contradicts C's 0.9%, although the dominance section has it right. There is no binomial SE and no return-period or horizon-risk calculation. These slips do not change the conclusion.
- **ME:** The four steps are ordered: hard-constraint gate, dominance check, layered MCDA with weight sensitivity, and design search. The uncertainty sources are enumerated well (block bootstrap, tail parameters, structure, non-stationarity) and model validation against historical floods is included. The feasibility verification steps for drawdown are less concrete.
- **PR:** Good. It covers regulator approval of the flood-limit change, the city's override authority in operations, automatic reversion triggers, a phased trial, compensation or benefit-sharing, independent review and a public communication plan. It does not mention the operating-manual revision, release capacity, or a measurement-based fallback rule.
- **CA:** Well scoped. It refuses to estimate revenue without prices, labels assumptions, and notes when it is unsure that stakeholders exist. Its choice of B as "the one real candidate" while B is the only scheme that raises flood risk is argued, but it leans slightly toward optimism about mitigation.

### Answer 2
- **KQ:** Finds the same deciding questions. It also makes feasibility explicit: drawdown against forecast lead time, release capacity, downstream safe release, travel time, refill, and forecast-failure behaviour. It applies the safety-first rule literally (B fails as tabled unless the risk is shown to be noise or the benchmark owner relaxes it) and mentions the city's protection standard as a possibly stricter benchmark. Like Answer 1, it does not compute the return-period or horizon risk.
- **TC:** The differences and percentages (+3%, +5%, +30% relative) are correct, as are the 0.4/0.5 expected events. "C almost dominated by A" is correct. The common-random-numbers point is sound. There is no binomial SE. There are no errors that change the conclusion.
- **ME:** Ordered and executable. The evidence fixes are paired runs on common sequences, leave-one-year-out and scaled floods, and mechanism checks against the releases. Then come the safety gate, the feasibility gate, a non-inferior set, and a transparent ranking with weight-stability and rank-acceptability tests. The no-forecast versus perfect-forecast bracket is a good verification device.
- **PR:** Specific. It proposes a temporary modification of the operating manual for one or two flood seasons, predefined reversion triggers and post-flood reviews, and a fallback to a measurement-based rule when forecasts fail. It gives the reservoir operator a real-time feasibility role, names a safety sign-off gate before any pilot, and notes cascade operators. Compensation is concrete: a dedicated irrigation storage target and a minimum navigation-flow commitment.
- **CA:** Well scoped. It lists what is not given and not assumed, states its assumptions explicitly, and labels the recommendation provisional. Calling A "the most plausible adoptable option" is suitably hedged on intervals and on compensation.

## 2. Notes for revision

### Answer 1
- **Done well:**
  - Treats flood risk as a constraint whose tolerance is set by those who bear it, before the ranking.
  - Gives a thorough taxonomy of uncertainty sources.
  - Proposes severity, CVaR and dam-safety metrics.
  - Provides a strong stakeholder table with veto-like standing and conflict-of-interest management.
  - Sets out reversion triggers and override authority, and refuses to monetise without prices.
- **Missing or wrong:**
  - The summary sentence on flood effects misstates C, which improves flood risk.
  - The physical feasibility of raising the flood-limit level is not worked through: pre-release time against forecast lead time, outlet and spillway capacity under the downstream safe flow, and refill risk.
  - There is no measurement-based fallback when forecasts fail.
  - It does not explain the likely mechanism for B's irrigation and navigation gains (more water carried through the season) or how to check it against the release series.
  - The preference for B over A is not tested against a flood-neutral benchmark.

### Answer 2
- **Done well:**
  - Applies the safety benchmark explicitly and literally.
  - Uses a common-random-numbers paired design and reports the probability that each scheme beats the current rule.
  - Proposes leave-one-year-out and scaled-flood stress tests.
  - Treats drawdown and refill feasibility in detail and brackets forecast value.
  - Plans a pilot through a temporary manual modification, with a measurement-based fallback.
  - Gives concrete compensation instruments and staged screening with clear decide/veto/advise roles.
- **Missing or wrong:**
  - Less depth on parameter and tail-shape uncertainty in the flood-frequency fit, and on non-market and equity valuation.
  - Public participation is thin ("informed and given a route to comment").
  - It does not name regulatory approval of the flood-limit level as a formal legal step, beyond the safety gate.
  - It does not convert risk into return periods or horizon probabilities.

### Points an expert answer must contain that neither had
- The binomial SE of a 40-year empirical estimate at p = 1% is about 1.6 pp, which shows the 0.3 pp and 0.1 pp differences are far below empirical resolution. Required sample sizes, or synthetic-series lengths, should be given for a stated confidence.
- The return-period translation (100 yr to about 77 yr for B) and the horizon risk (about 39% against about 48% chance of at least one exceedance in 50 years). If the city's protection standard is 1-in-100, B as tabled falls below the standard. This is a deciding regulatory fact, not just a statistical one.
- The flood-limit (flood-season control) water level is normally a formally approved parameter of the reservoir's flood-control operation plan. The approving flood-control headquarters or water authority, and the procedure for revising it, should be named explicitly.
- Standard engineering routes to a higher level without added risk: staged flood-season limits (pre-, main and post-flood season) and dynamic control of the flood-limit level within a forecast-based band, with mandatory pre-release to the base level on a forecast trigger.
- Expected annual damage under each scheme, set against the revenue gain, gives the commission at least an order-of-magnitude comparison once prices and damage curves are supplied. Flood safety still remains a constraint, and this comparison does not replace it.

# Judgment U4-j2

## What an excellent answer must contain (judge's own outline)

- Verdict: do not replace the approved rule next season. One retrospective event, one metric and one comparator do not support operational adoption. The model can at most run as a shadow or advisory tool, with the current rule kept as the governing rule.
- What the evidence supports: a single out-of-time holdout event, in the authors' simulator. That is feasibility only. Leakage still has to be ruled out (2023 data used in tuning, post-event corrected inputs, information not available at decision time).
- The comparator problem:
  - Actual operator decisions are not the current rule.
  - The counterfactual needs an executable version of the rule, run on the same inflow, initial level and information.
  - It also needs no-forecast and perfect-forecast brackets.
  - The simulator itself must be validated: feeding it the actual releases should reproduce the observed levels.
- Mass-balance check (the key calculation). A 30% peak cut has to be paid for in storage. The extra stored volume is about ∫ max(0, Q_out,actual(t) − 0.7·Q_out,peak) dt, adjusted for any extra pre-release. That volume must fit within the flood-control storage that was actually left (pool level vs flood-limit level and dam-safety level). If it does not fit, the 30% result is physically infeasible or relied on encroachment. If it does fit only through earlier pre-release, the forecast lead time and how reliable the pre-release decision was become the deciding question.
- Metrics and trade-offs:
  - Maximum pool level and the margin to the design and check flood levels.
  - Storage left for a second peak.
  - Downstream stage at control points.
  - Ramp rates and the end-of-event level.
- Generalization:
  - Multi-event leave-one-event-out testing.
  - Events larger than anything in 2010–2022, scaled up to design-flood magnitudes.
  - Multi-peak floods.
  - Forecast error and data dropout, including the error level at which the model becomes worse than the rule.
- Safety architecture: a hard-constraint layer, automatic fallback to the rule, human override and pre-specified stop criteria.
- Institutional and regulatory steps:
  - The approved scheduling rule and the flood-control command chain.
  - Dam-safety authority approval.
  - A formal revision of the operating manual.
  - Accountability.
  - Downstream and cascade coordination.
- Timeline realism: the next flood season leaves too little time for both a full evaluation and approval, so shadow mode is the realistic maximum for that season.

## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1 (U4-ltgn)

- **KQ:** Identifies the deciding questions:
  - n = 1.
  - The wrong baseline: operators' actions rather than the rule.
  - Hindsight and information leakage.
  - Peak outflow as a single metric with trade-offs (encroachment, a double peak, the end level).
  - Physical consistency of the simulation.
  - Distribution shift.
  - What the model was trained to imitate.
  - Legal authority.

  These are settled well. It does not explicitly tie the 30% to a storage-volume budget. It also does not ask the paper to validate its simulator against observed levels driven by actual releases; it asks only whether simulated levels follow from model releases.
- **TC:** No technical errors. It correctly says actual decisions differ from the rule, and its list of mechanisms by which a peak can be lowered (earlier fill, encroachment, shifting the burden) is sound. It does no quantitative mass-balance reasoning. The MPC baseline suggestion is appropriate.
- **ME:** The A–E ordering is clear: offline replication, then baselines, multi-event, stress, metrics and a constraint audit, then the safety architecture in parallel, then shadow, then bounded use. It includes "what would change my advice." Gaps:
  - No no-forecast vs perfect-forecast bracket to isolate the model's contribution.
  - No explicit simulator validation step.
  - No forecast-error threshold analysis (it does test forecast error).
- **PR:** Specific provisions:
  - Guardrail layer, bounded deviation from the rule and automatic reversion triggers.
  - Exclusion conditions (return-period thresholds, gate failure).
  - No silent retraining during the season.
  - Headquarters approval, legal review, an audit trail and cascade stakeholders.
  - Pre-specified shadow success and stop criteria.

  This is strong.
- **CA:** Assumptions are explicit and conclusions are tightly scoped ("promising hypothesis from a single simulated event"). The suggestion of selection bias (picking an event that works) is framed as a possibility. It invents nothing.

### Answer 2 (U4-76hg)

- **KQ:** Covers all of Answer 1's deciding questions and adds:
  - An explicit executable-rule comparator under identical initial state.
  - Validation of the simulator against observations driven by actual releases.
  - No-forecast and perfect-forecast brackets.
  - Whether the 30% is a mean or the best case.
  - The mechanism behind the reduction (earlier pre-release vs a higher pool).
  - Whether pre-release can finish within lead time and whether storage can absorb the design flood.
  - Successive floods.
  - Over-caution in small floods.

  It gets closer to the mass-balance question but still gives no volume calculation.
- **TC:** No errors. It correctly notes that holding water raises the pool and reduces capacity for a second wave, and that forecast accuracy is not operational value. One weak point: "papers in this field show benefits..." is a vague appeal to literature, not a technical error.
- **ME:** A nine-step ordered plan: baseline and validated simulator, then verification of the claim, then out-of-sample and stress tests, then the safety envelope, then explainability, then shadow, then approval, then pilot, then governance. It includes trajectory diagnosis, the forecast-error break-even point and a fixed metric set before runs. Slightly more complete and better verified than Answer 1.
- **PR:** Specific provisions:
  - A safety layer that clips recommendations, automatic fallback and escalation triggers.
  - Dam-safety authority and cross-agency review.
  - Formal revision of the operating manual through staged assessment.
  - Operator review of historical events.
  - An accountability chain, drift monitoring and a post-event reanalysis.

  Comparable to Answer 1 and slightly broader on institutions. Answer 1 is slightly more concrete on exclusion conditions and the in-season change freeze.
- **CA:** Assumptions are explicit and the conclusions are scoped. Its framing of the burden of proof and the asymmetry of risk is good. The literature reference is unsupported and generic, which is a small deduction. It also notes the conditional possibility of an earlier pilot, with appropriate caveats.

## 2. Notes for revision

**Answer 1, done well:**
- A clear verdict.
- A strong list of trade-off mechanisms behind a lower peak.
- A hard-constraint audit.
- A guardrail and exclusion-condition design.
- An in-season freeze on retraining.
- "What would change my advice."

**Answer 1, missing:**
- An executable-rule comparator run under the same initial level, and simulator validation against observed levels.
- No-forecast and perfect-forecast brackets.
- A forecast-error break-even analysis.
- A check of the reduction mechanism along the 2023 trajectory.
- Successive-flood and small-flood over-caution tests.
- The formal operating-manual revision process.

**Answer 2, done well:**
- A precise comparator design.
- Simulator validation.
- Information brackets.
- Diagnosis of the 2023 trajectory (what pool level was held, the mechanism).
- Pre-release feasibility within the lead time.
- A forecast-error threshold.
- Staged institutional revision and operator review.

**Answer 2, missing or weak:**
- An unsupported generic appeal to "papers in this field."
- Less concrete in-season provisions than Answer 1 (exclusion thresholds, no silent retraining).
- No quantitative storage check.

**Points neither answer had:**
- **An explicit mass-balance feasibility check of the 30% claim.** Compute the extra stored volume ΔV ≈ ∫ max(0, Q_actual − 0.7·Q_peak) dt over the 2023 hydrograph and compare it with the flood-control storage actually remaining above the 2023 operated pool. Then translate it into the implied rise in maximum pool level through the storage–elevation curve, and compare that with the design and check flood levels. This immediately shows whether the claim is physically possible, or possible only through encroachment or forecast-based pre-release.
- **Whether operators' 2023 peak was itself forced** by the rule, by downstream conditions or by an emergency, which changes what "30% lower" means.
- **The practical time budget.** A full multi-event, stress and approval cycle is unlikely to fit before the next flood season, so shadow mode is the realistic ceiling for that season.
- **A quantitative acceptance criterion tied to the design standard,** for example no increase in the probability of exceeding the flood-limit or design level, or no increase in the downstream safe-discharge exceedance, compared with the rule.

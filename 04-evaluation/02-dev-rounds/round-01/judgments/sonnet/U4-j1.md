# Judgment U4-j1

## What an excellent answer must contain (judge's reference)

- Clear verdict: do not replace the approved rule next season. Keep the rule in force, run the model in shadow or advisory mode, and allow staged, bounded use only after it passes pre-agreed gates.
- Scope of the evidence: one retrospective event (N=1) in the authors' simulator. The comparator is what operators actually did, not the current rule applied to the same inflow. The 30% is a simulated upper-bound-type figure, not an operational benefit.
- Mass-balance check (the key calculation): with the same inflow volume, a lower peak outflow must be paid for in storage. The extra volume stored is ΔV ≈ ∫ max(0, Q_out,actual(t) − 0.7·Q_out,peak) dt over the peak period. Convert ΔV to an extra pool rise through the stage–storage curve. Compare that with the flood-control storage, the flood limit level, the design and check flood levels, and the room left for a second peak. Also check whether the model's lower peak simply came from early pre-release, which needs forecast lead time and release capacity. Then ask whether the 30% reduction matters at all: if the actual 2023 peak release was already below the downstream safe discharge, the gain has little flood-control value. Peak outflow also does not map linearly to stage at the downstream control point, because local inflow adds to it.
- Information leakage and hindsight: perfect inflow knowledge, data revised after the event, and 2023 used in model selection or tuning.
- Training-label paradox: a model trained on 2010–2022 operator decisions that claims to beat those operators by 30% needs an explanation. Is it imitation learning, an optimization or RL target, or forecast inputs?
- Extrapolation: whether the record has enough large floods, how the model behaves at design-flood magnitude, and non-stationarity.
- Evaluation plan: replicate the result, encode the rule as a baseline, add simple or MPC baselines plus no-forecast and perfect-forecast brackets, test across many events with leave-one-event-out, stress-test with scaled and design floods and forecast error, use a multi-metric set with safety treated as a constraint, and run a hard-constraint audit.
- Practicality: a safety layer outside the model, automatic fallback to the rule, human override, a chain of command and approval of any change to the water-control manual or scheduling rule, shadow-season criteria, change control, and accountability.

## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1
- **KQ (7):** Finds almost all the deciding questions: N=1, the wrong comparator (operators versus the rule), leakage and real-time inputs, peak outflow as a proxy, the storage and second-wave trade-off, the extreme-flood training gap, failure modes and institutional authority. It states that holding more water raises the pool, but never turns this into a storage-volume check or asks whether the 2023 actual peak was already within safe channel capacity. It hints at the training-label issue ("operator decisions ... conflated with optimal labels") but does not settle it.
- **TC (7):** No conceptual errors. The no-forecast and perfect-forecast bracket, validating the simulator by driving it with actual releases, and "safety as a constraint, not a trade-off" are all correct. No calculation is attempted, not even a qualitative mass-balance statement in volume terms.
- **ME (8):** A well-ordered nine-step program: baseline, verification of the 30%, out-of-sample testing (LOYO/LOEO with retraining, seeds), stress tests, finding the forecast-error break-even point, a safety envelope, shadow operation, approval, pilot and governance. Metrics are fixed before running. It is executable, but the pass/fail thresholds are generic.
- **PR (7):** Covers a safety layer that clips outputs, automatic fallback, escalation triggers, shadow mode, stakeholder and dam-safety authority review, revision of the operating manual, accountability and drift monitoring. These are reasonably specific, but less concrete than Answer 2 on exclusion conditions, deviation bands and change control during the season.
- **CA (8):** Assumptions are listed explicitly, and the verdict is scoped well (there is even a "what would change this" list). One mild unsupported general claim: "Papers in this field show benefits can be ... negative".

### Answer 2
- **KQ (8):** Covers the same set and adds two points. First, the simple-baseline test: if a heuristic or MPC also gets about 30%, the ML contribution is small. Second, it explicitly asks whether the model imitates operators or is optimized in a simulator, and why that changes validation. It also asks whether the simulated levels follow from the model's releases (water-balance closure) and whether 2023 lies inside the training range. Like Answer 1, it never quantifies the storage cost of the 30% or asks whether the actual peak was already below safe discharge.
- **TC (6):** Mostly correct, but one mechanism is internally confused: "releasing earlier and filling flood storage sooner". Early release empties storage. The real risk is either holding water, which fills storage, or pre-releasing on a forecast that turns out wrong. It is a minor slip that does not change the conclusion. Its insistence on water-balance closure and on physically feasible gate and rating-curve releases is technically strong. No calculation is attempted.
- **ME (8):** Ordered from cheap offline work to operations-facing steps. It covers replication and leakage, the baseline set (rule, rule with forecasts, MPC, observed as reference only), multi-event rolling or LOEO, distribution and regret percentiles, synthetic scaling (1.2–2x), design floods, forecast-error and data-dropout tests, a hard-constraint audit in which one violation is a blocker, sensitivity to sedimentation and rating changes, and OOD detection. Shadow criteria are pre-specified, though thresholds are still placeholders ("X%").
- **PR (8):** More operational detail: a guardrail with bounded deviation from the rule, a display showing both the rule's and the model's release to the duty officer, fallback drills, conditions under which the model is excluded (return-period threshold, gate failure, missing forecasts), no silent retraining during the season, approval from the flood-control headquarters and legal review of the scheduling rule, and cascade stakeholders.
- **CA (8):** Assumptions are flagged and conclusions scoped, with explicit more-favorable and less-favorable outcomes. Its remarks about selection bias and light review of a conference paper are framed as possibilities, not as facts.

## 2. Notes for revision

**Answer 1**
- Done well: the clearest framing of operators versus rule versus the no-forecast and perfect-forecast bracket; validating the simulator against observed levels when driven by actual releases; finding the forecast-error break-even point; testing the opposite failure (over-caution and lost refill in small floods); the asymmetric-risk argument for putting the burden of proof on the model; a staged institutional route.
- Missing or weak: no volume or mass-balance check of the 30% (the extra storage needed and the resulting pool rise against the flood limit and design levels). It does not ask whether the actual 2023 peak was already below the downstream safe discharge. There is no simple-heuristic or MPC baseline to isolate the ML contribution. The training-label paradox is only touched on. The pilot and fallback provisions lack concrete exclusion thresholds and in-season change control.

**Answer 2**
- Done well: the simple and MPC baseline test; the distinction between imitation and simulator-trained models; the water-balance closure check and the requirement that levels follow from releases; the hard-constraint audit as a blocker; regret percentiles; tests for sedimentation and rating-curve change; concrete exclusion conditions, bounded deviation bands and no silent retraining.
- Missing or wrong: the "releasing earlier and filling flood storage sooner" mechanism is confused. There is no quantitative storage-cost estimate for a 30% peak cut. It does not ask whether the actual peak exceeded safe channel capacity, which decides whether the reduction had any value. It lacks the no-forecast lower bracket and an explicit forecast-error break-even analysis.

**Points an expert answer must contain that neither answer had**
- A mass-balance calculation of what a 30% peak reduction costs: ΔV equals the volume of the actual outflow hydrograph above 0.7·Q_peak. Convert it to a pool rise through the stage–storage curve and compare it with the flood-control storage, the flood limit level and the design and check flood levels. Also check whether any pre-release was feasible within outlet capacity and forecast lead time.
- Whether the 2023 actual maximum outflow exceeded downstream safe discharge or caused damage. If it did not, a 30% lower peak has little flood-control value. If it did, translate the reduction into stage and inundation at the control points, accounting for local inflow and routing.
- Whether operators deviated from the rule in 2023 because of information or constraints the simulation omits (dam-safety instructions, downstream emergencies). This would make the operator baseline both stronger and weaker than the rule.
- A plain statement that one season is too short to collect enough flood events to validate the model in shadow mode. Advance therefore depends mostly on offline, multi-event and synthetic evidence. Shadow mode mainly tests the pipeline and the operators' interaction with the model.

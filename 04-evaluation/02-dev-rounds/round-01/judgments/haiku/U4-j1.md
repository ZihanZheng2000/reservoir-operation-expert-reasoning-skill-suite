# Judgment U4-j1

## What an excellent answer must contain (judge's reference)

1. **Information leakage / hindsight**: the retrospective simulation almost certainly fed the model observed 2023 inflows (or hindcasts better than what operators had). Operators decided under real-time uncertainty. The 30% gap partly measures the value of perfect information, not the model. The fair test is model vs. operators (and vs. the rule) using only the forecasts that existed at each decision time.
2. **Wrong baseline**: "actual operator decisions" is not "the current operating rule". Operators may have deviated from the rule. The replacement decision needs ML vs. the rule, both simulated under the same information.
3. **Mass-balance / storage trade-off (the key calculation)**: dV/dt = I − O. A lower peak outflow means more water stored, so the peak reservoir level goes up. Example: if the original peak release is Qp and the new cap is 0.7·Qp, the extra storage is about the integral of (O_actual − 0.7·Qp) over the period when the actual release was above 0.7·Qp. For a peak of 10,000 m3/s held about 30% higher for about 2 days, this is on the order of 0.5 × 3,000 m3/s × 172,800 s ≈ 2.6×10^8 m3. That volume has to be checked against the flood-control storage left between the starting level and the flood-control/design-flood level, and against the routing of a second flood peak. A 30% peak cut is only "better" if no level limit, dam-safety freeboard or post-flood drawdown limit was breached. Downstream flow at the control points (with tributary timing) is the real objective, not dam outflow alone.
4. **Training-range extrapolation and sample size**: 13 years (2010–2022) hold only a few large floods. Was 2023 larger than anything in training? Was 2023 used in model selection or tuning? If the model imitates past operations, why would it beat them?
5. **Robustness evaluation**: many historical events held out, design floods (e.g., 100/1000-yr, check/PMF for dam safety), different flood shapes and initial levels, and degraded forecasts. Report the worst case, not only the mean.
6. **Hard constraints**: level, release, ramping and downstream-capacity limits enforced by a rule-based safety layer outside the ML model. Also physically feasible gate operations.
7. **Regulation and governance**: the dispatch rule and flood-control plan are approved by the competent authority, so a change needs formal review and approval. Liability and accountability. The ML model can at most be decision support next season.
8. **Deployment path**: shadow (parallel) run next season, then advisory mode with operator authority, with explicit triggers to fall back to the current rule (data outage, out-of-range inputs, constraint violation). Post-season review against pre-set acceptance criteria.

## 2. Notes for revision

### Answer 1
- **Done well**: a clear "do not adopt directly" verdict. It proposes perfect-forecast and no-forecast bracketing to isolate forecast value, and it separates "actual decisions" from "current rule" as the baseline. It covers synthetic floods beyond the training range, an explicit risk standard set before testing, flood-stage (pre-release, rising limb, peak, recession) logic, revert triggers, and post-event reanalysis.
- **Missing or wrong**:
  - It treats real-time forecast availability as an assumption instead of asking whether the 2023 simulation used observed future inflows. That hindsight question is the deciding one.
  - It does not say that a 30% lower peak release means more water stored and a higher peak pool level. No mass-balance check.
  - It does not ask whether 2023 exceeded the 2010–2022 range or was used in tuning.
  - The pilot alters real operations for a season instead of starting with a shadow run.
  - It cites "standard practice in the literature" without basis.

### Answer 2
- **Done well**: it explicitly flags perfect foresight in the retrospective run and asks whether 2023 was tuned on. It asks whether 2023 is an outlier relative to the training distribution. It proposes a proper shadow-run, then advisory, then constrained-automation sequence with operator authority. It covers fallback testing before the season, licence and regulator review, liability, and downstream impacts on top of dam outflow.
- **Missing or wrong**:
  - The spillway and catastrophic-risk sentence garbles the mechanism. It should say that reduced release raises the pool and erodes flood-control storage and freeboard.
  - No mass-balance estimate of the extra storage.
  - No design-flood or extreme-flood (dam-safety) tests.
  - No pre-set acceptance criteria.
  - It does not say that amending the approved operating rule needs a formal approval process.

### Points an expert answer must contain that neither answer had
- A **mass-balance quantification**. The extra storage needed is about the integral of (O_actual − 0.7·Qp) dt over the peak period. It should be converted to a pool-level rise using the stage–storage curve and compared with the flood-control and design-flood levels and the starting level. The 30% claim is meaningless until the peak water level, residual capacity for a second flood, and post-flood drawdown are reported.
- **Downstream control-point flows with tributary timing.** Peak dam outflow is a proxy. Delaying releases can make them coincide with tributary peaks.
- **Pre-registered acceptance criteria** before evaluation. Examples: no exceedance of the flood-control level in any test event or design flood; downstream safe-flow compliance; worst-case performance no worse than the current rule.
- A **rule-based hard-constraint safety layer** wrapped around the ML output, plus out-of-distribution input detection as an automatic trigger to revert to the current rule.
- A **formal rule-amendment pathway**: the current dispatch rule stays the legal operating basis next season. The ML model may only run as shadow or advisory until the competent authority approves a revised rule.

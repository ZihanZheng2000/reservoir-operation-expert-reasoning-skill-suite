# Judgment U6-j2

## What an excellent answer must contain (own working)

- **Changes against the current rule.**
  - A: +0.3 billion kWh (+3%), flood risk unchanged, irrigation -2 pp, navigation +2 days.
  - B: +0.5 billion kWh (+5%), flood risk +0.3 pp (30% relative, return period about 100 yr to about 77 yr), irrigation +3 pp, navigation -3 days.
  - C: +0.2 billion kWh (+2%), flood risk -0.1 pp, irrigation -7 pp, navigation +6 days.
- **Dominance check.** No alternative dominates the current rule, and no alternative dominates another. A vs C: A is better on generation, irrigation and navigation but worse on flood risk. All four schemes are non-dominated, so the choice cannot be settled by dominance.
- **Flood safety is a constraint, not a weight.** The downstream flood-protection standard, the approved flood-limit water level and dam safety (design and check floods) are statutory constraints set by the flood-control authority. Flood risk must not be traded against kWh in a weighted score. Under the current standard, B is infeasible unless the competent authority formally revises the standard.
- **Evidence limits.** A 1% annual probability estimated from 40 years means about 0.4 expected exceedances in the record. The 0.9 / 1.0 / 1.3% figures therefore come from model extrapolation and cannot be told apart statistically without uncertainty intervals. The 40-year record also does not contain design-level floods, so "A has unchanged flood risk" despite a higher level must be checked with design-flood routing and forecast-error analysis. Some results look odd and need a consistency check: B has a higher level yet better navigation and irrigation; C has lower flood risk yet higher generation.
- **Life-cycle risk.** Over 50 years, the chance of at least one exceedance is about 39% at 1.0% and about 48% at 1.3%. Expected flood damage should be set against the value of the extra generation.
- **Recommendation.** Keep the current rule now. Take A forward as the only candidate that does not worsen flood risk, conditional on:
  - uncertainty intervals for all indicators;
  - design-flood and dam-safety routing;
  - forecast-based pre-release capability and a rule for when the raised level must be drawn down;
  - irrigation-district and navigation acceptance of the losses;
  - a trial period with reversion triggers;
  - approval by the flood-control authority.
- **Who takes part.** The flood-control authority approves the flood-limit level and can veto. Also: the dam safety regulator, the water resources administration (water allocation), the navigation authority, the grid dispatcher, the hydrological forecast service, an independent technical reviewer, the environmental agency, and the public.

  None of these changes the conclusion.

## 2. Notes for revision

**Answer 1, done well:**
- Treats flood risk as a floor and excludes B under the 1.0% limit.
- Gives correct relative deltas.
- Makes the irrigation district's acceptance of 90% an explicit condition.
- Requires uncertainty intervals.
- Proposes a compensation and benefit-sharing mechanism.
- Includes a stakeholder table with veto rights.

**Answer 1, missing or wrong:**
- The relative flood-risk increase is 30%, not 50%. The return-period sentence is garbled.
- The trigger "1.2% over a 5-year window" cannot be measured. Triggers should instead be operational: observed exceedances, forecast errors, and drawdown achieved before flood peaks.
- No check that A's higher level leaves flood risk unchanged under design floods and dam-safety check floods, which the 40-year record does not contain.
- No forecast-based pre-release or drawdown rules.
- No statutory flood-limit level approval.
- No expected-damage versus revenue comparison.
- No grid dispatcher, hydrological forecast service or independent reviewer.

**Answer 2, done well:**
- States that flood risk is non-compensable and a binding constraint.
- Warns against early weighted aggregation.
- Tests sensitivity to the weights.
- Cross-checks the flood tail with an independent method.
- Requires explicit, documented risk acceptance linked to levee standards.
- Includes a forecast service and an independent review panel.
- Proposes a pilot with reversion.
- Specifies an operating manual amendment with pre-release thresholds and emergency overrides.

**Answer 2, missing or wrong:**
- C's flood-risk change is reversed: 0.9% is better, not worse. C is wrongly declared dominated, and A is mis-described.
- The kWh to GWh units are wrong by a factor of 1000.
- It recommends B, which goes against its own safety-first step. A 30% relative increase in exceedance probability is material.
- It treats possible model uncertainty as a reason to accept the increase rather than to be cautious.
- It cites reference papers that do not exist.
- No dam safety review and no legal approval of the flood-limit level.

**Points an expert answer needs that neither answer had:**
1. A correct dominance check: all four schemes are mutually non-dominated, so the decision rests on the flood constraint and on stakeholder trade-offs.
2. A 40-year record gives about 0.4 expected exceedances at 1%. The 0.9 / 1.0 / 1.3% differences come from extrapolation and need bootstrap or Bayesian intervals before any ranking.
3. Life-cycle risk: about 39% vs 48% chance of at least one exceedance in 50 years. Annualised expected flood damage should be compared with the monetised generation gain.
4. Verification of A and B against design-flood and check-flood routing and dam-safety freeboard, not only against the historical record.
5. A consistency check on the table, where results look counterintuitive: B is higher yet better for irrigation and navigation; C has lower flood risk yet more generation.
6. Operational conditions for any raised level:
   - forecast-based dynamic flood-limit level, with the lead time and forecast skill it requires;
   - mandatory pre-release and drawdown when a large flood is forecast;
   - a staged flood season;
   - coordination with the grid dispatcher for spill and pre-release.
7. Statutory approval of the flood-season limit level by the flood-control authority, plus a dam safety regulator review, before any trial.
8. "Keep the current rule until the conditions are met" as the default fallback.

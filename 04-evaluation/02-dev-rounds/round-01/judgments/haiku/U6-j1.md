# Judgment U6-j1

## Reference: what an excellent answer must contain

- Changes relative to current rule: A = +0.3 bn kWh (+3%), flood risk unchanged, irrigation -2 pp, navigation +2 days. B = +0.5 bn kWh (+5%), flood risk 1.0% to 1.3% (+0.3 pp, +30% relative; return period about 100 yr to about 77 yr), irrigation +3 pp, navigation -3 days. C = +0.2 bn kWh, flood risk 0.9% (better; return period about 111 yr), irrigation -7 pp, navigation +6 days.
- Dominance check: none of the four schemes is Pareto-dominated. B is worse only on flood risk and better on everything else. C is better on flood and generation and worse on irrigation and navigation. So the decision turns on whether flood safety is a hard constraint.
- Flood safety is a non-compensable constraint tied to the city's legal or design protection standard (a 1.0% exceedance is about a 100-year standard). B breaches that standard unless the competent authority formally revises it, and generation revenue cannot buy that change.
- Statistical resolution: 40 years of record gives about 0.4 expected exceedances at 1%. So 0.9%, 1.0% and 1.3% come almost entirely from the model and probably cannot be told apart without uncertainty intervals. Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis (record length, forecast error, non-stationarity) must come before any adoption.
- Sensible recommendation: keep the current rule as the operative default. Take A forward for more study, since it is the only scheme with no added flood risk, but its gains are small and its irrigation and navigation losses need the affected parties' consent. Treat B only as a candidate for a conditional or forecast-based flood-limit-level variant that keeps the 1.0% standard. Drop C or hold it back.
- Practical provisions: formal approval of the flood-limit water level by the flood-control authority, dam-safety check, pre-flood drawdown and forecast-based pre-release rules, refill timing, a staged trial with reversion triggers, and compensation or benefit-sharing.
- Stakeholders: the basin commission, the flood-control authority holding veto or approval power over flood risk, the hydropower company, the irrigation district, the navigation authority, dam-safety and forecast services, environmental agencies, the public, and independent technical review.

## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1 (recommends Scheme B, conditional on flood-risk acceptance)
- **KQ (5):** It frames flood safety as a binding constraint and spots the missing uncertainty intervals, both of which decide the answer. It then sets its own constraint aside and recommends B. It never settles the real question, which is that B breaches the existing flood standard. It also does not see that 40 years of data cannot resolve differences at the 1% level.
- **TC (3):** It misreads Scheme C as "raises flood risk" and "worsens flood control (0.9% vs 1.0%)", and so calls C "dominated". That is wrong: C lowers flood risk, and the error drives the elimination of C. It says "none is dominated" and then calls C dominated, which contradicts itself. It gives the units as GWh instead of billion kWh, which is off by a factor of 1000. It says A is "a small gain everywhere except irrigation", but navigation is also worse under A. It calls +0.3 pp a "small increment" and never states the 30% relative increase or the shift from a 100-year to a 77-year return period.
- **ME (5):** The steps are executable in order: constraint screen, criteria matrix, Pareto set, sensitivity. But the Pareto step is done wrong, the ±10% perturbation proposal is arbitrary, and the method is not applied consistently to reach the recommendation.
- **PR (7):** Good specifics: formal documented acceptance of flood risk, an operating-manual amendment with pre-release and emergency-override thresholds, a 1–2 year pilot with pre-agreed reversion criteria, a post-event reanalysis protocol, and involvement of the forecast service and an independent review panel.
- **CA (4):** It cites "the papers in the reference" and "the reference material", which do not exist in the question. It suggests the 0.3 pp increase is "often within uncertainty" and uses that to soften the flood penalty for its preferred scheme, which is asymmetric reasoning. Its recommendation of B is stronger than the evidence supports.

### Answer 2 (recommends Scheme A, conditional on uncertainty analysis and irrigation consent)
- **KQ (6):** It sets floors for every objective first and states that B drops out if 1.0% is a ceiling. It identifies the missing uncertainty intervals, the irrigation district's consent, and distributional fairness. It does not settle whether the point differences can be resolved statistically, and it does not ask whether the current rule should simply be kept while the uncertainty is resolved.
- **TC (5):** It reads all scheme deltas correctly and recognises that C improves flood risk. Its errors: "a 1-in-77-year event becomes 1-in-77" is garbled (it should be 100 yr to about 77 yr), "~50% increase" should be 30%, and "flood risk and generation cannot be improved simultaneously in A, B, C" is false because C improves both. None of these slips changes its conclusion.
- **ME (6):** The order is executable: floors, then uncertainty and robustness, then weighted scoring and negotiation, then an adaptive agreement, then approval. It proposes worst-case and range comparison. Gaps: the example weights are arbitrary, and the trigger "flood probability exceeds 1.2% in a 5-year rolling window" cannot be detected statistically for a 1% event, so its verification design is flawed.
- **PR (7):** A clear authority table with veto roles for the flood-control and irrigation agencies, revenue-sharing or compensation funded by the hydropower gain, monitoring and re-evaluation cycles, and public documentation. It lacks operating detail such as the flood-limit level, pre-release rules and refill timing, and it does not mention a dam-safety check or a formal regulatory approval route for the operating level.
- **CA (5):** Its conclusions are mostly made conditional. But "navigation is typically the least binding constraint", "85% is too low for irrigation planning" and "likely ≥88%" floors are unsupported assertions. "Likely implementation" of A overstates what point estimates without intervals can support.

## 2. Notes for revision

**Answer 1, done well:** It puts safety first as a non-compensable screen. It flags the missing confidence intervals and asks for an independent model cross-check. Its implementation design is concrete: pilot, reversion clause, operating-manual amendment, post-event reanalysis. Its stakeholder list includes the forecast service and independent reviewers.
**Answer 1, missing or wrong:** It misreads C's flood risk and builds a wrong dominance claim on it. Its units are wrong (GWh). It contradicts its own binding constraint by recommending the one scheme that breaches the current 1% standard. It does not quantify the change as +30% relative or as a return period falling from 100 to 77 years. It cites reference material that does not exist. It does not note that B's irrigation and navigation gains come from holding more storage, which is the same thing that erodes the flood buffer.

**Answer 2, done well:** Its deltas are correct and its screening logic is consistent. It has explicit veto roles and a decision-authority table. It proposes compensation and benefit-sharing from the hydropower gain, attends to distributional fairness, and explicitly seeks the irrigation district's consent to 90%.
**Answer 2, missing or wrong:** Its return-period and relative-increase arithmetic is garbled. It claims no scheme improves both flood risk and generation, but C does. Its monitoring trigger cannot be detected statistically for a 1% event. It makes unsupported generalisations about navigation and irrigation thresholds. It gives no operating-level specifics (flood-limit level, pre-flood drawdown, forecast-based pre-release, refill timing) and no dam-safety or regulatory approval step.

**Points neither answer had:**
- With 40 years of record, about 0.4 exceedances would be expected at 1%. The flood-risk estimates therefore rest almost entirely on the model, and the 0.9/1.0/1.3% differences may not be distinguishable. Any revert trigger must be based on model re-analysis or on precursor indicators such as encroachment into the flood pool, not on counting observed exceedances.
- Keeping the current rule should be the explicit default until uncertainty analysis shows that the advantage of the chosen alternative is robust. The default should not hinge only on how much risk the city will tolerate.
- Flood-control capacity and the flood-limit water level fall under statutory approval (flood-control authority and dam-safety regulator). Changing the protection standard is a separate legal decision, not a trade-off weight.
- Variants worth designing: a forecast-informed or staged flood-limit level, such as raising the level only late in the flood season or only under a low-inflow forecast with guaranteed pre-release capacity. Such a variant could capture some of B's gains while keeping 1.0%.
- Consequence-based flood risk (expected damage, exposure, levee condition), not just exceedance probability. Climate non-stationarity in the 40-year record.
- Whether a mean navigation-deficit-days indicator and an end-of-season storage reliability indicator hide severity or the timing of shortfalls in the worst years.

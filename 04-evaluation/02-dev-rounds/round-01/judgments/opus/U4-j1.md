# Judgment U4-j1

## What an excellent answer must contain (judge's own reference)

- Clear verdict: no direct replacement next season; shadow or advisory use at most. The current approved rule stays the legal basis for operation.
- What the evidence supports: one event, retrospective, one metric (peak outflow), compared against actual operator decisions. That shows feasibility and is a hypothesis, nothing more.
- Deciding questions:
  1. Information leakage or perfect foresight: observed versus as-issued forecasts.
  2. Wrong comparator: actual operations versus the current rule simulated on identical inputs and initial level.
  3. Closed-loop, mass-balanced simulation, with the simulator validated by replaying the recorded releases.
  4. Where the water went: peak-outflow reduction is bought with storage. That means higher maximum level, less freeboard, encroachment toward the design and check flood levels, and end-of-event storage left for a following flood.
  5. Downstream control-point effects: timing against tributary peaks and duration of high flows.
  6. Constraints: outlet capacity at each level, ramp rates, regulatory minimum and maximum releases.
  7. Generalization: one sample; 2010–2022 is 13 seasons with only a few large floods; extrapolation beyond the training range.
  8. Mechanism: an imitation model beating its teacher by 30% needs explaining.
  9. Robustness to data and forecast failure.
- Calculation (order of magnitude): the extra detention needed for a 30% peak cut is roughly the area between the actual and simulated outflow hydrographs. As a triangle approximation, ΔV ≈ 0.5 × 0.3·Q_peak × (duration of the clipped period). Compare this with the remaining flood-control storage and the surcharge up to the design and check levels. The calculation shows whether the 30% is physically plausible and how much safety margin it consumes. Also: 13 years of training data contain few independent large events, so statistical support is thin.
- Method:
  - independent reproduction;
  - executable current rule as the comparator, plus a perfect-foresight upper bound;
  - hindcast forecasts;
  - leave-one-event-out validation;
  - stress tests (scaled floods, design and check floods, back-to-back events);
  - full metric set (dam safety, downstream, constraints, post-event state, other uses);
  - acceptance criteria set before testing;
  - distribution and worst-case reporting.
- Practicality:
  - shadow season with logging;
  - human-in-the-loop operation;
  - hard constraints;
  - fallback and reversion triggers;
  - data and IT readiness;
  - regulatory approval of any change to the operating manual;
  - accountability;
  - stakeholder and emergency action plan updates;
  - staged pilot, then formal rule revision;
  - an after-action review of 2023 that is independent of the model.

### Points an expert answer must contain that neither had

- **Storage plausibility check.** Estimate the extra detention volume implied by a 30% peak-outflow cut: ΔV ≈ the integral of (Q_actual − Q_model) dt, or roughly 0.5 × 0.3·Q_peak × the clipped duration. Convert ΔV to a level rise through the level–storage curve. Compare it with the flood-control storage left at the 2023 peak and the surcharge up to the design and check levels. This quickly shows whether the 30% was physically available or came from encroaching on the safety margin.
- **Check the operators' actual peak outflow against the inflow peak.** If the actual operation already attenuated the inflow, a further 30% cut is a strong claim. If the outflow roughly equalled the inflow because the flood pool was full, the model's gain must come from earlier pre-release, which depends on forecast skill.
- **Statistical framing.** 13 seasons give only a handful of independent large events, so any skill estimate has wide uncertainty. The evaluation should report uncertainty, for example bootstrap or ensemble spread across events, not point gains.
- **Regulatory specifics.** A flood-season operating rule (flood-limited water level, staged release schedule) is normally approved by a higher authority and coordinated with the downstream flood-control command. Any model-driven change to pre-release or to the flood-limited level needs that approval before the season, which is a timing constraint on the next flood season itself.

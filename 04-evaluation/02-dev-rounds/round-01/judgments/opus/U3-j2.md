# Judgment U3-j2

## What an excellent answer must contain (judge's reference)

- Pooled normal error model: ignores heteroscedasticity (error grows with flow; 100-year flood lies beyond the 12-year calibration range), lead-time growth (day-3 error drives prerelease volume), seasonality, bias/skew/heavy tails (systematic peak underforecast), and forecast-system nonstationarity; fitted-parameter uncertainty from a short, autocorrelated record.
- Independent daily sampling: understates 3-day volume error. For three equicorrelated errors, Var(sum) = 3σ²(1+2ρ), so relative to independence the variance factor is (1+2ρ): about 3x at ρ→1, i.e. the volume-error SD is understated by up to √3 ≈ 1.7. It also removes persistent under/overforecast runs, issue-to-issue consistency and peak-timing errors. Net bias is most likely optimistic for the forecast-based rule.
- "95% confidence": with 5,000 runs the Monte Carlo SE is tiny. The confidence is conditional on the assumed error model, the single hydrograph, the initial level and the simulator. It does not cover epistemic uncertainty. The answer must ask whether the claim is about the sign of the effect or its size, and whether "18%" is a mean, a median or a percentile.
- Scenario coverage: multiple hydrograph shapes and durations, multi-peak and successive floods, a full range of return periods (frequent floods up to the dam-safety design flood or PMF), realistic initial storage, and design-flood quantile uncertainty.
- Metrics: maximum reservoir level and freeboard; downstream flow and stage including local inflow; flows caused by the prerelease itself versus channel capacity; false alarms and refill; water supply and hydropower; ramping limits.
- Comparison: paired runs with common random numbers; probability that the new rule is worse and its tail losses; faithful encoding of the current rule; no in-sample tuning; no-forecast and perfect-forecast bounds.
- Feasibility with numbers: on a 600 Mm³ reservoir, a prerelease of Q m³/s held for T days removes Q·T·86,400 m³. For example, 1,000 m³/s for 3 days is about 259 Mm³, roughly 43% of total storage. That volume has to be checked against outlet capacity at low head, the safe downstream discharge and the usable lead time (less than 3 days once issue time, decision lag and travel time are deducted). The answer should also check whether the 18% peak reduction is consistent with the flood-control volume that can actually be created.
- Required evidence: a hindcast on the real 12-year archive, a rebuilt error model with validation, stress and break-even tests, an acceptance criterion agreed beforehand with the dam-safety regulator, and an independent review.
- Adoption: shadow/parallel trial, caps on prerelease volume and rate, a fallback to the current rule when the forecast fails, an abort/refill protocol, updated operating manual and emergency action plan, regulator approval, downstream warning procedures and liability.

The conclusion is right, but the written factor "(1 + 2·2ρ/3 + …)" is garbled and non-standard; for equicorrelated errors the factor is (1+2ρ). It does not separate errors that are additive in flow from errors scaled to flow. It does no storage-scale arithmetic for 600 Mm³. Otherwise the concepts are sound. Score: 7.
- **ME:** Twenty ordered, executable requirements: an error-model rebuild with validation, block resampling or an AR model, ensembles, a hindcast, broader scenarios, a break-even analysis, model verification and split-sample testing. ## 2. Notes for revision

### Answer 1
- **Done well:** Thorough error-model critique, including dependence across successive issuances and timing/shape errors. It is the only answer to quantify the √3 understatement of volume SD. It asks clearly about sign versus size of the effect, separates the four uncertainty sources, and recommends block resampling and ensembles. It includes a break-even analysis and a staged adoption pathway with caps, abort/refill and operator override.
- **Missing or wrong:**
  - The variance factor expression is garbled; it should be (1+2ρ) for equicorrelated errors.
  - There is no check that the prerelease volume can physically be released within the usable lead time, given outlet capacity at low head and the safe downstream discharge.
  - There are no no-forecast or perfect-forecast reference runs, and no check of the daily versus sub-daily time step.
  - There is no acceptance criterion agreed with the regulator before results are seen.
  - There is no shadow/parallel operation phase and no explicit rule for falling back when the forecast fails.

### Answer 2
- **Done well:** Effective lead time and physical feasibility of the drawdown; the perfect-forecast bound used as a diagnostic for over-optimistic errors; the time-step concern; bootstrapping of parameter uncertainty; stress tests with inflation factors and a break-even point; a pre-agreed acceptance criterion with the dam-safety authority; a fallback rule; liability; a parallel trial run; and revert triggers.
- **Missing or wrong:**
  - It never quantifies the variance understatement caused by independent sampling (factor 1+2ρ, up to about 3x in variance).
  - It does not use ensembles, consider climate-adjusted floods, or mention updating the emergency action plan.
  - It does no arithmetic on prerelease volume against the 600 Mm³ storage.

### Points an expert answer must contain that neither answer had
- Storage-scale arithmetic. Prerelease volume is Q·T·86,400 m³; for example, 1,000 m³/s for 3 days is about 259 Mm³, roughly 43% of the 600 Mm³ total. Compare this with the flood-control pool and the safe channel capacity, and check whether an 18% peak reduction is consistent with the volume that can actually be evacuated in the usable lead time.
- A rough estimate showing that Monte Carlo sampling error is negligible at n = 5,000. The SE of a proportion near 0.95 is about 0.003, so the "95%" carries essentially no information about real-world uncertainty.
- An explicit treatment of the asymmetric consequences: an overforecast causes lost storage and refill failure, while an underforecast causes a higher maximum level and emergency spillway operation. Both should be weighted in expected annual damage and dam-safety risk.
- Coordination with flood-control or water-allocation authorities and with downstream regulation of release rates, as a precondition for approval.

# Judgment U6-j2

## Reference: what an excellent answer must contain

- Differences from the current rule. A: +0.3 bn kWh (+3%), flood risk unchanged, -2 pp irrigation, +2 navigation days. B: +0.5 (+5%), +0.3 pp flood risk (30% relative increase, return period 100 to about 77 yr), +3 pp irrigation, -3 days. C: +0.2, -0.1 pp, -7 pp, +6 days. No scheme dominates all the others. B is the only one that raises flood risk, and the only one that improves every other objective.
- Evidence quality. A 1% event over 40 years gives about 0.4 expected exceedances, so the absolute probability comes from the fitted model. The decision should rest on paired differences computed on common scenarios, with intervals. A reliability figure from a 40-year record moves in 2.5 pp steps (one year in 40), so the 92% vs 90% gap is less than one failure year if it was computed on the historical years.
- Flood safety should be a constraint (no credible increase, with a tolerance set in advance by the flood authority), not a weight. Add severity and tail indicators. Add reservoir-side and dam-safety indicators: design and check flood levels, surcharge, and back-to-back floods.
- Check the mechanism and consistency. A "raised level" that lowers irrigation storage (A) needs explaining. So does B improving refill.
- Floors for irrigation and navigation. Show trade-offs openly within the admissible set. Use stakeholder weights with a sensitivity test. Consider benefit-sharing.
- A scheme that can be designed rather than chosen: staged or variable flood limits, or forecast-based drawdown with a feasibility check and a fallback.
- Recommendation: keep the current rule for now; take A forward with conditions; redesign B; drop C. Add a staged trial with triggers for reverting.
- Governance: the flood authority holds a veto; the dam-safety regulator and the body that approves the rule must sign off; the forecasting agency, the grid and an independent review panel take part; the public is consulted.
- Useful calculations: horizon risk 1-(1-p)^n. For 50 years this gives 39.5% at 1.0%, about 52% at 1.3% and 36.4% at 0.9%. Also a break-even check of B's revenue against the expected change in damage (0.003 x damage per event).

### Points neither answer had
- **Sample granularity:** over a 40-year historical record, one failure year equals 2.5 pp of reliability. A's -2 pp is therefore less than one year, and B's +3 pp is about one year. The irrigation differences may not be resolvable without the synthetic ensemble and intervals.
- **Break-even check on B:** monetise +0.5 bn kWh per year at the tariff and compare it with 0.003 x the expected damage per exceedance event. This shows the damage per event that would make B's trade "worth it", and why expected-value reasoning still should not override the safety standard.
- **Detection power:** at about 1% per year, a monitored trial of a few flood seasons cannot confirm or refute a change in flood risk. Triggers for reverting during a trial must rest on process indicators (levels reached, drawdown compliance, forecast performance), not on observed exceedance counts.
- **Head effect:** distinguish the generation gain that comes from higher head from the gain that comes from extra turbined volume, since the two have opposite implications for downstream users.
- **Sediment:** check whether a higher flood-season level affects sediment flushing and the long-term loss of flood-control storage.

# Judgment U1-j2

## What an excellent answer must contain (judge's reference)

- A research question framed as "staged FLWL vs current fixed FLWL, safety as a hard constraint, benefits second", with hypotheses that can be falsified.
- **Deciding questions:** (1) Is there real, stable flood seasonality in peaks and in the multi-day volumes that govern flood storage? (2) Does the fixed FLWL actually bind, meaning the reservoir fails to refill or spills because of it? If it rarely binds, the gain is small. (3) What is the downstream safe discharge at the town, including intervening-catchment inflow and dam-to-town travel time? (4) What is the official design basis behind the current FLWL (design flood, PMF/SPF, MWL, freeboard)? (5) Can the extra storage be drawn down safely at each transition to a lower stage?
- **Risk combination with arithmetic.** With k independent stages, P_annual = 1 − Π(1 − p_i). Three stages each at 1% give 1 − 0.99³ ≈ 2.97%, about three times the annual standard. Holding 1% overall needs p_i ≈ 1 − 0.99^(1/3) ≈ 0.335% per stage, roughly a 1-in-300-year stage event. Each stage has only 45 samples, so 1-in-100 to 1-in-300 quantiles are heavy extrapolations with wide confidence intervals.
- **Drawdown/pre-release feasibility arithmetic.** ΔV from ΔH via the level–storage curve, and t = ΔV / (Q_release,max − Q_in), where Q_release,max ≤ Q_safe − Q_intervening. Compare t with the reliable forecast lead time minus decision and gate delays. Example: 50 Mm³ at a net 500 m³/s takes about 28 h.
- **Travel time.** 40 km at 1–3 m/s is about 4–11 h, which is below the daily time step. This argues for sub-daily routing.
- Method steps: encode the current rule, then baseline simulation, seasonal division, seasonal design floods and hydrographs, routing to find the maximum stage FLWL, long-term historical and stochastic simulation, forecast error analysis kept as a separate layer, and robustness/stress tests.
- **Indian regulatory context.** The Dam Safety Act 2021 (NDSA/SDSO), CWC guidance on reservoir rule curves and operation manuals, BIS design-flood criteria, Emergency Action Plan updates, and approval by the dam owner, the irrigation and power departments, and the district administration for the town. Also a monitored trial and a fallback rule.
- Graded claim levels tied to which data are actually obtained.

### Points an expert answer must contain that neither had

- **Numeric risk allocation.** 1 − 0.99³ ≈ 2.97% combined if each of three stages uses 1%. About 0.335% per stage (≈1-in-300) is needed to hold 1% annually. With 45 samples per stage, these quantiles are far outside the record.
- **Drawdown-time calculation.** ΔV from ΔH via the level–storage curve, and t = ΔV / (Q_safe − Q_intervening − Q_in), compared against the forecast lead time that actually has skill. A worked example would show whether the late-stage rise is operationally reversible.
- **Travel time estimate.** 40 km at roughly 1–3 m/s is 4–11 h. A daily step cannot resolve the timing of release plus intervening-flow superposition at the town.
- **Indian regulatory route.** Dam Safety Act 2021 with NDSA/SDSO review, CWC guidelines for rule curves and O&M manuals, BIS design-flood criteria (IS 11223), revision of the Emergency Action Plan and downstream warning protocol, and district administration involvement for the town.
- **Rule-curve context.** Many Indian reservoirs already run on monthly or dekad rule curves. The study should state how a staged FLWL maps onto, or differs from, the existing rule-curve structure.
- **East-coast or post-monsoon cyclone risk.** It should be tested explicitly as a late-stage scenario using storm-type separation, because it directly threatens the late-stage rise, which is where the main benefit is.

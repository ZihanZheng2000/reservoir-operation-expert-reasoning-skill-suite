# Judgment U3-j1

## What an excellent answer must contain (judge's own outline)

- Pooling flaw: errors are heteroscedastic (grow with flow), lead-time dependent (day 3 >> day 1), seasonal, biased (peaks under-forecast on the rising limb), skewed or heavy-tailed. A pooled normal fit is dominated by ordinary days and understates error at 100-year flows. The 12-year record holds few or no near-100-year events, so applying it to that flood is extrapolation. The forecast system has likely changed over the 12 years.
- Independence flaw, with a calculation: for 3 daily errors with equal sigma and pairwise correlation rho, Var(3-day volume error) = 3σ²(1 + 2ρ). Independent sampling gives 3σ², so at rho near 1 the true variance is up to 3x larger (SD √3x larger, 3σ against √3σ). With lead-time growth in sigma the gap is larger still. Persistence across successive issues and peak-timing errors are also missing. The net bias is optimistic for a forecast-based rule.
- "95% confidence": with 5,000 runs the Monte Carlo standard error is tiny (for a proportion near 0.95, SE ≈ √(0.95·0.05/5000) ≈ 0.003). So the 95% figure reflects sampling noise inside one assumed model, not epistemic uncertainty. Ask what 95% refers to: a CI on the mean, the share of runs won, or the sign versus the size of the effect.
- Scenario scope: one hydrograph shape at one return period, with an unknown initial level. Frequent floods (false alarms, refill loss, prerelease-induced downstream flow) and above-design floods (maximum level, freeboard) also need testing, as do successive floods and design-flood uncertainty.
- Metrics: maximum reservoir level and freeboard, downstream control-point flows and stages, prerelease rate against safe channel capacity and ramping limits, refill and supply losses, and tail or worst-case comparisons on paired runs.
- Model credibility: a validated simulator, a faithful baseline rule, out-of-sample tuning, and no-forecast and perfect-forecast bounds.
- Feasibility: prerelease volume against outlet capacity and effective lead time. As a scale check against the 600 million m³ total storage: drawing down V m³ in t days needs a release of V/(t·86400) m³/s above inflow. Prerelease should be capped at a volume that can be refilled under a low-side forecast.
- Evidence and approval: a real-forecast hindcast, stress and break-even tests, acceptance criteria agreed beforehand with the dam-safety authority, a shadow trial, a fallback to the current rule when the forecast fails, operating-manual and emergency-action-plan changes, and independent review.

## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1
- **KQ:** Finds nearly every deciding question: pooling by lead time, flow and season; bias and tails; inter-day and inter-issue dependence; timing error; 12-year extrapolation; parameter uncertainty; the meaning of 95%; single shape and single return period; maximum level; downstream flows; false alarms and refill; baseline fidelity; feasibility in effective lead time. It does not quantify the independence effect.
- **TC:** No technical errors. Its claims are correct: independent errors cancel over 3 days, MC significance is trivial at n=5000, and lowering peak outflow by storing more can raise the maximum level. It gives no arithmetic, so nothing can be checked, but nothing is wrong either.
- **ME:** Gives a well-ordered, executable program: rebuild the error model (stratify, copula or forecast-evolution model, fidelity checks, block bootstrap for parameters), then hindcast with holdout, then scaled historical floods, stress tests with a break-even error level, finer time step, paired reporting, and no-forecast and perfect-forecast bounds. Verification steps are explicit.
- **PR:** Strong and specific. Acceptance criteria are agreed in advance with the dam-safety authority. It specifies fallback triggers (forecast missing, late or diverging from observed inflow), re-deciding on each forecast issue, release liability, downstream warning, a parallel shadow trial, a temporary documented manual change with revert triggers, and independent review.
- **CA:** Very well scoped. It states that the direction of bias is "likely optimistic" but uncertain, notes that old errors could also be too pessimistic, and lists its assumptions explicitly ("I have not seen the firm's report").

### Answer 2
- **KQ:** Similar coverage, plus probability weighting and expected annual damage, design-flood estimation uncertainty, the definition of "100-year" by peak or by volume, and missed events. It lacks perfect-forecast and no-forecast bounds, the effective lead-time feasibility check and the time-step issue.
- **TC:** It attempts the key calculation, but the formula is wrong. The factor is given as "(1 + 2·2ρ/3 + …)", which equals 2.33 at ρ=1, not the 3 it then states. The correct factor is (1 + 2ρ). The sentence "A volume-error standard deviation that should be about √3 times larger looks much smaller" is muddled. The conclusion (variance about 3x, SD √3x at full correlation) is right, so this is a slip that does not change the conclusion. Everything else is correct, including the near-zero MC sampling uncertainty.
- **ME:** Executable and well ordered: a heteroscedastic, transformed-space model; AR or multivariate dependence or block resampling of trajectories; timing errors; ensembles; validation of error statistics; hindcast; broadened scenarios; break-even analysis; split-sample tuning; and an uncertainty decomposition into (a) to (d). It lacks benefit bounds and an explicit feasibility check.
- **PR:** Good. It covers dam-safety regulation, downstream release limits, water rights, warning coordination, staged adoption with ensemble-probability triggers, caps on prerelease volume and rate, an abort or refill protocol, operator override, EAP and manual updates, and re-evaluation. It is slightly less concrete on fallback triggers and on pre-agreed acceptance criteria.
- **CA:** Good, with assumptions listed. It is slightly more assertive ("almost certainly overstated", "possibly by a large factor", "My expectation is that a corrected analysis would show a smaller... benefit"), though these are framed as expectations.

## 2. Notes for revision

**Answer 1, done well:** a clear decision-oriented bottom line, an explicit paired-run and tail analysis, no-forecast and perfect-forecast bounds as a diagnostic ("near perfect-forecast result means errors too optimistic"), effective lead time of 1.5–2 days, daily time-step smoothing of the peak, acceptance criteria fixed beforehand with the dam-safety authority, and a shadow trial with revert triggers.
**Answer 1, missing:** any quantification of how independent sampling shrinks the 3-day volume error (3σ²(1+2ρ) against 3σ²). It also omits a numeric MC standard error, expected annual damage and probability weighting across return periods, uncertainty in the 100-year estimate itself, and ensemble forecasts as a direct alternative.

**Answer 2, done well:** the attempt to quantify the variance inflation, the decomposition of uncertainty into MC, error model, design flood and model components, expected-damage weighting, the peak versus volume definition of the 100-year event, missed events, ensemble triggers and an abort protocol.
**Answer 2, wrong or missing:** the variance-factor formula is wrong (it should be 1+2ρ, giving 3x at ρ=1, not 1+4ρ/3) and the SD sentence is garbled. It also lacks perfect-forecast and no-forecast bounds, a physical feasibility check of the drawdown within the usable lead time, a time-step check, and explicit fallback triggers and pre-agreed acceptance criteria.

**Points neither answer had:**
- A numeric MC standard error (≈0.003 on a proportion near 0.95 at n=5000), showing the 95% figure is pure sampling precision.
- A scale check of prerelease against the 600 million m³ storage: the required drawdown rate V/(t·86400) against outlet and safe channel capacity, and what fraction of the 100-year flood volume the prerelease represents.
- A requirement that prerelease volume be bounded by what can be refilled under a low-quantile forecast (the risk-of-not-refilling constraint), linked to the flood-season limiting water level in the regulation.
- Verifying the error model with probabilistic forecast metrics (reliability and PIT diagrams, CRPS) conditioned on high flows, and modelling the conditional distribution of observed given forecast rather than additive error alone.

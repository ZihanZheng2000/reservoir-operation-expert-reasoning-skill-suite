# Advice to the Basin Commission: Flood-Season Operating Level Proposal

## Summary

- **Keep the current rule in force for now.** The study does not yet show that any alternative is both safe and clearly better. The estimates have no uncertainty intervals, and the 1% flood-risk numbers rest on a 40-year record. That record would contain on average 0.4 events of the size that matters.
- **Do not adopt Scheme B as proposed.** It is the best scheme on generation, irrigation and navigation, but it raises the annual probability that city flow exceeds the safe threshold from 1.0% to 1.3%. That is a 30% relative increase, and the return period falls from about 100 to about 77 years. Downstream flood safety should be treated as a constraint that revenue cannot buy down. Since B looks so good on the other three objectives, though, the commission should ask for a **redesigned B**: one that keeps its refill benefit while bringing flood risk back to no more than the current rule.
- **Take Scheme A forward as the lead candidate, with conditions.** It is the only alternative that matches the current flood risk on the point estimates while adding generation (+0.3 billion kWh, +3%). Its costs to irrigation (92% to 90%) and navigation (12 to 14 days) are modest but real. Moving it forward depends on the conditions in Part 2.
- **Drop Scheme C** unless further analysis changes the picture. Its flood-risk gain (1.0% to 0.9%) is small and probably within estimation error. Its cost to irrigation (92% to 85%) and navigation (12 to 18 days) is large.

The sections below explain how to compare the schemes, the basis and conditions for this provisional advice, and who should take part in the decision.

## 1. How the schemes should be compared and how trade-offs should be handled

### 1.1 Understand what each scheme actually does before comparing numbers

The table gives outcomes but not mechanisms. Before any choice, the commission needs a short description of each scheme: what level is held, during which part of the flood season, and under which conditions it is drawn down or raised. It also needs to know whether the scheme uses forecasts, and if so which forecasts, at what lead time, and what happens when they are wrong. Some of the numbers only make sense once the mechanism is known:

- **B is better on generation, irrigation and navigation at once.** That fits a scheme that holds more water late in the flood season, so the reservoir refills more reliably and carries more water into the dry season. The cost is less empty flood space when a large flood arrives. If that is the mechanism, the flood-risk increase sits in large and late floods, which the 40-year record barely samples.
- **A raises generation but lowers irrigation storage and adds navigation-shortfall days.** If A simply raised the flood-season level, refill should improve, not get worse. So A probably turbines more water during or after the flood season and ends the season lower. The commission should confirm this, because it means A's extra revenue comes partly out of water that irrigation and navigation would otherwise use.
- **C lowers flood risk but sharply cuts irrigation and navigation.** That fits a scheme that keeps more flood space or draws down earlier, and so refills less. It is close to the opposite of B.

If the stated mechanisms do not explain the numbers, that alone is a reason to check the simulation before comparing schemes.

### 1.2 Make sure the comparison is fair and credible

1. **Same conditions for every scheme.** All four schemes, including the current rule, must be run in the same simulation model, on the same inflow series and synthetic scenarios, with the same initial levels, discharge capacities and downstream thresholds. The current rule must be the encoded rule as actually operated, checked against historical levels and releases. Otherwise the differences may come from modelling choices rather than from the rules.
2. **Check the simulator against history.** Run the current rule over the 40 historical years and compare simulated levels, releases and city flows with what was observed, especially in the largest floods. If the model cannot reproduce past behaviour, none of the estimates in the table can be relied on.
3. **Uncertainty intervals, especially on the differences.** The commission should require:
   - confidence intervals for each indicator, from bootstrap or resampling of years and from the parameter uncertainty of the probabilistic flood model;
   - above all, **paired differences** against the current rule (for example B minus current flood risk), computed on the same scenarios. A paired difference can be estimated far more precisely than each absolute value, and it is the quantity the decision actually depends on.

   With 40 years, an absolute 1% annual exceedance probability is mostly a product of the fitted flood model. The gaps between 0.9%, 1.0% and 1.3% may or may not be distinguishable. That must be shown, not assumed.
4. **Sensitivity to the flood model.** Repeat the risk estimate under other flood distributions, other hydrograph shapes, and seasonal timing of floods. Include stochastic or scaled floods beyond the historical range. If the climate or catchment is changing, also test a non-stationary flood regime. The ranking of schemes on flood risk should hold across these choices.
5. **Forecast dependence.** If any scheme counts on forecasts to draw down before a flood, its risk estimate depends on forecast quality at the lead time it needs. That requires:
   - realistic forecast errors, not perfect foresight;
   - a check that the drawdown can physically be completed within that lead time, given outlet capacity and the safe downstream flow;
   - a fallback rule for when the forecast misses.
6. **Holdout and event tests.** Show performance on individual large historical floods and on dry sequences, not only the averages. Where the scheme or the risk model was tuned, check results on years left out of the tuning. A scheme that looks good on average but fails badly in the flood of record, or in a multi-year drought, should be flagged.

### 1.3 Widen the indicators where one number is not enough

- **City flood risk.** One exceedance probability does not show how far above the threshold flow goes, for how long, or what damage results. Add:
  - the distribution of peak flow and duration above the threshold;
  - expected flood damage and a tail measure (for example expected damage in the worst 1% of years), if damage curves exist;
  - risk over a planning horizon. Over 50 years, the chance of at least one exceedance is about 39% at 1.0%, about 48% at 1.3%, and about 36% at 0.9%.
- **Dam safety.** The table has no reservoir-side indicator. Holding a higher level in the flood season directly cuts the margin against design and check floods. The probability that the maximum reservoir level exceeds the design and check flood levels must be reported for every scheme, using design floods, not only the historical record.
- **Irrigation.** End-of-season storage reliability should be backed by the size of shortfalls and how often deficits come in consecutive years, since that is what hurts farmers. It should also be compared with the irrigation district's guaranteed or design reliability.
- **Navigation.** Mean days per year hides bad years. Report the distribution and the worst year, compared with any guaranteed navigation flow.
- **Hydropower.** Mean annual generation should be backed by firm (dry-season) output, spill, and revenue, since the timing of generation affects value. The 0.3 to 0.5 billion kWh gains should be converted into revenue at the applicable tariff, so they can be weighed against costs borne by others.

### 1.4 How to handle the trade-offs

Use a structured, staged approach rather than a single weighted score:

1. **Flood safety first, as a constraint.**
   - Set the acceptable-risk benchmark before results are compared. The natural benchmark is the larger safety margin of two: the city's legal or design flood protection standard, and the risk under the current rule.
   - Treat dam-side risk the same way, against the design and check flood standards.
   - A scheme that raises city or dam flood risk above the benchmark is **not admissible**, however large its other benefits, unless the responsible flood authority explicitly and formally accepts a new risk level. That is a policy decision outside the commission's trade-off analysis.
   - Because of estimation uncertainty, apply the constraint as "no credible increase": the upper bound of the paired risk difference should be within a small tolerance the flood authority agrees in advance.
2. **Minimum service levels for irrigation and navigation as floors.** The irrigation district and navigation authority should state their guaranteed or minimum acceptable levels, for example the design irrigation reliability and the maximum shortfall days. A scheme that pushes either below its floor should be excluded, or adopted only with an agreed remedy.
3. **Show the trade-offs openly within the admissible set.** Among schemes that pass steps 1 and 2, do not collapse the objectives into one score too early. They are not in the same units, and their weights belong to the stakeholders. Present a table of differences from the current rule, with intervals, and note where one scheme is at least as good on every indicator as another.
4. **If a ranking aid is needed**, a multi-criteria method (for example weighted closeness to an ideal point) can be used, but:
   - the weights should come from the stakeholders, not from the analysts;
   - the ranking should be tested against other weights, other methods, and other flood scenarios;
   - a ranking that flips under reasonable changes should not be treated as a result.
5. **Use benefit sharing where the losses can be compensated.** Some objectives are commensurable (hydropower revenue against irrigation and navigation losses). For these, the commission can ask whether part of the generation revenue could fund compensation or mitigation, such as guaranteed dry-season releases, a drought reserve, or navigation dredging. Flood safety is not one of these.
6. **Ask whether a better scheme can be designed, not only chosen.** The four schemes are a few points from a larger design space. The pattern in the table (B good on three objectives and bad on one) suggests a scheme between A, B and the current rule could do better. Examples:
   - raise the level only in the later part of the flood season, when large floods are less likely;
   - raise it only under forecast-based conditions, with a firm drawdown trigger;
   - split the flood season into stages with different limits.

   Searching this space with flood risk held at or below the current level is more useful than forcing a choice among four fixed options.

## 2. Recommendation and its basis

### 2.1 Provisional recommendation

| Scheme | Disposition | Main reason |
|---|---|---|
| Current rule | **Keep in force** until an alternative passes the conditions below | It is the safety benchmark. No alternative has yet been shown, with uncertainty, to be safe and better. |
| A | **Take forward as lead candidate for detailed assessment and a possible staged trial** | Same flood risk on point estimates, +3% generation. Costs to irrigation and navigation are modest and possibly within error, but must be confirmed and addressed. |
| B | **Do not adopt as proposed; ask for a redesign** | Flood risk +0.3 points (+30% relative) fails the safety-first screen. Its strength on three objectives points to a refill gain worth keeping if the flood risk can be removed. |
| C | **Do not take forward**, unless the flood authority gives high value to a 0.1-point risk reduction and it proves statistically real | Large losses to irrigation (-7 points) and navigation (+6 days) for a flood gain that is probably within estimation error. |

### 2.2 Basis

- **Safety-first screening.** On the point estimates only A and C keep flood risk at or below the current rule. B does not, and so is excluded unless redesigned or explicitly re-authorized by the flood authority.
- **Between A and C,** A loses much less for irrigation and navigation and gains more generation. C's only advantage is a 0.1-point lower flood risk, which is likely not distinguishable from the current rule's 1.0% given a 40-year basis.
- **A against the current rule** is a genuine trade-off, not a free gain. The proponent benefits (+0.3 billion kWh) while irrigation (-2 points) and navigation (+2 days) bear costs. That is acceptable only if those costs stay within the stakeholders' floors and are addressed. So A goes forward with conditions; it is not adopted outright.
- **B deserves a second look rather than outright rejection.** It improves irrigation and navigation as well as generation, which none of the other schemes does. If the flood-risk increase can be removed with forecast-conditioned drawdown or seasonal staging, a modified B could be better than A for every stakeholder except possibly the flood authority. It must then be shown not to worsen flood risk.

### 2.3 Key assumptions

- The city's flood protection standard is no weaker than the current 1.0% (about a 100-year level). If the standard is weaker, for example 1-in-50, the safety screen changes. Even then, the flood authority should decide whether to give up margin, not the trade-off analysis.
- The four schemes were simulated under identical conditions with a model checked against history.
- Dam-side safety does not worsen under A. This has not been reported.
- The irrigation and navigation figures for A are above the stakeholders' guaranteed levels. This has not been reported.

### 2.4 Conditions before A (or a redesigned B) could be adopted

1. **Flood safety shown with uncertainty.** The paired difference in city exceedance probability against the current rule must have an upper bound within a tolerance the flood authority sets in advance. The same must be shown for dam-side exceedance of design and check flood levels, under design floods and floods beyond the historical record, not only the 40 years.
2. **Robustness.** The result must hold across alternative flood-frequency models, hydrograph shapes, flood timing within the season and, if relevant, non-stationary scenarios. It must hold on the largest historical floods, not only on average.
3. **Feasibility.** The scheme must respect outlet capacity, rates of release change and the safe downstream flow. If it relies on forecast-based drawdown, the drawdown must be achievable within the reliable forecast lead time, with a defined fallback to the current rule when forecasts fail.
4. **Service floors and remedy.** Irrigation reliability and navigation shortfall days must stay within the floors those agencies set. Any remaining loss must be covered by an agreed remedy, such as dry-season release commitments, a drought trigger that restores irrigation priority, or revenue-funded compensation.
5. **Operating protocol.**
   - Clear triggers for when the raised level must be abandoned and the reservoir drawn down.
   - The flood-control authority keeps command during floods.
   - Defined emergency branches.
6. **Staged adoption.**
   - A time-limited, monitored trial or a temporary amendment to the operating rule, rather than a permanent change.
   - Agreed performance indicators and a review after each flood season and each major event.
   - An automatic return to the current rule if any safety trigger is crossed.

### 2.5 What would confirm or change this advice

- **Confirm A:** paired flood-risk differences for A centred near zero with tight intervals, no dam-side risk increase, irrigation and navigation still above their floors, and a mechanism that explains the numbers.
- **Move B up:** a redesigned B, or proof that B's 1.3% is not credibly above 1.0% (unlikely, but possible if intervals are wide). The flood authority could also formally accept a higher risk level with mitigation, such as stronger levees or warning systems.
- **Move C up:** proof that its flood-risk reduction is real and the flood authority values it, together with an irrigation and navigation remedy.
- **Favour keeping the current rule outright:**
  - A's gains are not distinguishable from zero once intervals are added;
  - A's flood or dam risk turns out higher in floods beyond the record;
  - irrigation or navigation for A falls below a guaranteed level;
  - the simulation fails its check against history.

## 3. Who should take part and how

The decision affects legal safety duties, commercial interests and water rights. It should be run as a structured multi-agency process with defined roles, not as a negotiation between the proponent and those it affects.

### 3.1 Participants and roles

| Participant | Role in the decision |
|---|---|
| **Basin commission** | Convener and decision-maker. Chairs a steering group, approves the workplan and decision criteria, makes the final determination, and owns the review cycle. |
| **City flood-control department** and higher-level flood-control or emergency authorities | Set the acceptable flood-risk benchmark and the tolerance for the paired difference. Hold an effective veto on the safety constraint. Keep operational command during floods. Approve drawdown triggers and emergency branches. |
| **Dam owner or operator and dam safety regulator** | Confirm reservoir-side safety against design and check floods, physical limits (outlet capacity, rates of release change, structural limits), and operability of the proposed rules. |
| **Hydropower company (proponent)** | Supplies the model, data, assumptions and scheme definitions in full, so others can reproduce the results. Carries the burden of showing safety and robustness. Takes part in benefit-sharing discussions. |
| **Irrigation district and farmers' representatives** | State the guaranteed and minimum acceptable reliability and the deficit tolerance. Review the irrigation results, including worst years. Negotiate remedies. |
| **Navigation authority and shipping operators** | State the guaranteed navigation flows and acceptable shortfall. Review results and remedies. |
| **Grid operator or power dispatch** | Confirm how changed generation fits system needs and how flood-time generation schedules would be coordinated. |
| **Hydrological and meteorological forecasting agency** | Needed if any scheme relies on forecasts. Verifies forecast skill at the lead times the scheme needs and runs the forecasts in operation. |
| **Water-resources or rule-approving ministry or regulator** | Formally approves amendments to the operating rule. Makes sure water rights and legal standards are respected. |
| **Environmental agency** (if environmental flows apply) | Checks that minimum environmental flows are met. |
| **Independent technical review panel** | Reviews the model, the risk estimates and the uncertainty analysis. Neither the proponent nor the affected parties should be the only ones checking the numbers. |
| **Downstream communities, local governments and public** | Informed and consulted, especially on any change to flood risk, through public notice and comment. |

### 3.2 How they should take part

1. **Agree the rules before seeing the final numbers.** In a first stage, the steering group agrees:
   - the indicators and their definitions;
   - the flood-safety benchmark and tolerance (set by the flood authority);
   - the irrigation and navigation floors (set by those agencies);
   - the uncertainty and robustness tests required;
   - how remaining trade-offs will be decided.

   This prevents the criteria from being chosen to fit a preferred result.
2. **Use a shared, transparent analysis.** All parties have access to the same model, data and scheme definitions. The independent panel reviews them. The affected agencies may run their own checks.
3. **Screen in stages.**
   - Stage one is a coarse screen against the safety constraint and service floors, which excludes or sends back schemes such as B and C.
   - Stage two is a detailed assessment of the remaining candidates, including any redesigned B.
   - Stage three is consultation on trade-offs and remedies.

   Rigour and engagement increase at each stage.
4. **Consult in rounds on the trade-offs.**
   - Stakeholders state their preferences or weights explicitly.
   - The commission shows how the ranking changes under different weights.
   - Disagreements and how they were resolved are recorded.
5. **Make a formal disposition.** At the end the commission records one of four outcomes:
   - keep the current rule;
   - defer pending more analysis or better forecasts;
   - approve a time-limited trial with conditions;
   - amend the operating rule.

   It also records the conditions, the remedy agreements and who is responsible for each.
6. **Run a trial and review.** If a trial is approved:
   - the commission monitors agreed indicators;
   - all agencies review after each flood season and each major event;
   - triggers for automatic return to the current rule are defined in advance;
   - the rule is revised before any permanent adoption.

   During floods, a standing coordination protocol, led by the flood-control authority with the operator, grid, navigation and irrigation agencies, governs real-time decisions.

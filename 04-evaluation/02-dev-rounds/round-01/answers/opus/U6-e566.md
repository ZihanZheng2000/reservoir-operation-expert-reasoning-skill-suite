# Advice to the Basin Commission: Flood-Season Operating Level Proposal

## 0. Summary

- **None of the four options is better than the others on every objective.** Each new scheme gains on some objectives and loses on others, so the choice depends on values and rules, not on one "best" number.
- **Flood safety for the city should be treated as a constraint, not as something traded for revenue.** The constraint is the existing protection level (about 1% annual exceedance under the current rule, plus how severe exceedances are). The other objectives can then be traded off openly among the schemes that meet it.
- **Provisional recommendation: keep the current rule in force for now. Take Scheme A forward to a second-stage assessment, with conditions.** Do not adopt B as proposed. Do not take C forward. Also ask for a modified version of B ("B′") that tries to keep B's irrigation and navigation gains without raising flood risk.
- **The table alone cannot support adopting any scheme.** It has no uncertainty intervals. The differences in flood probability (0.9%, 1.0%, 1.3%) are small compared with how uncertain a 1%-per-year estimate from 40 years of record is likely to be.

Assumptions I am making, since the question does not say:
- (a) The "safe threshold" is the city's flood-protection design discharge. The ~1% level matches the city's current protection standard (about a 1-in-100-year event).
- (b) All four schemes were simulated with the same model, the same historical and synthetic inflows, and the same demand assumptions.
- (c) The schemes differ in more than one parameter, for example flood-limit level, timing of refill, and release rules. That is why A and B can differ in direction on irrigation.
- (d) No legal irrigation-reliability or navigation standard was given. I treat the current values as the reference and say where a contract or legal minimum would change the advice.

## 1. How to compare the schemes and handle the trade-offs

### 1.1 First, set out the trade-off structure honestly

Changes relative to the current rule:

| Scheme | Generation | City flood exceedance | Irrigation reliability | Navigation shortfall days |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | +0.3 bn kWh (+3%) | 0 (same point estimate) | −2 pp | +2 days (worse) |
| B | +0.5 bn kWh (+5%) | +0.3 pp (1.0→1.3%, i.e. ~30% higher relative risk; return period ~100→~77 yr) | +3 pp (better) | −3 days (better) |
| C | +0.2 bn kWh (+2%) | −0.1 pp (slightly better) | −7 pp | +6 days (worse) |

What this shows:
- **No option dominates another.** Each scheme beats the current rule on some objectives and loses on others. So the decision cannot be made "technically" by Pareto screening alone. It needs explicit value judgments and rules.
- **B is the only scheme that raises flood risk, and the only one that improves every other objective.** That is exactly why the decision is contested. B is attractive to three of the four interests and worse for the one that matters most for life safety.
- **A buys generation mainly at the expense of irrigation and navigation.** Flood risk is unchanged on the point estimate.
- **C makes a small flood gain, possibly not real, at a large cost to irrigation and navigation.** The generation gain is the smallest of the three.

### 1.2 Step 1: Check the evidence before comparing anything

Before any trade-off discussion, the commission should require the following.

1. **Uncertainty intervals for every indicator.** With 40 years of record, an annual exceedance probability near 1% corresponds to about 0.4 expected exceedances in the record. The estimate therefore rests almost entirely on the fitted probability model and the synthetic scenarios. The absolute 1% value is likely to have a wide interval.
   - Ask for intervals from the sampling of the inflow series, from uncertainty in the distribution parameters, and from the choice of distribution.
2. **Paired differences, not just separate estimates.** If all schemes are run on the same synthetic inflow ensemble (common random numbers), the *difference* between scheme X and the current rule can be estimated much more precisely than either absolute value. The key quantity is the difference for each scheme with its interval.
   - Example: "B − current = +0.3 pp, 90% interval [x, y]". The question is whether that interval excludes zero and whether its upper end is acceptable.
3. **Flood-severity indicators, not just frequency.** A probability of exceeding the threshold says nothing about *how far* or *how long* the threshold is exceeded. Request:
   - peak flow at the city for the 1%, 0.5% and 0.2% events
   - how much the flow exceeds the threshold, and for how long
   - expected annual damage, if a damage curve exists
   - the highest reservoir level reached and how often surcharge storage or the emergency spillway is used
   - performance in back-to-back floods (a second flood arriving before the reservoir is drawn down)
   - performance against the dam-safety design flood

   Raising the flood-season level reduces flood-control storage. Its main risk is in large or long floods, which the 1% frequency figure may understate.
4. **Indicators that describe the bad years.** Reliability (92%, 90%…) and mean days are averages. Also request:
   - **Irrigation:** how large the shortfall is in failure years (vulnerability), how many failures come in a row (resilience), and the worst single year.
   - **Navigation:** when shortfall days occur in the season, how many come in a row, and the distribution across years. The mean may be driven by a few drought years.
   - **Hydropower:** revenue, not just kWh. Include timing and peak value, firm or guaranteed output, and variability between years. Also check the effect on output in the dry season.
5. **Consistency and plausibility checks on the model.** For example: why does A, which presumably also raises the level, *lower* irrigation reliability while B raises it? The direction of each change should be explained by the actual rule parameters. Check also:
   - whether the 40-year record is representative (trends, land-use change, climate projections, sediment loss of storage, changes in downstream channel capacity)
   - whether the simulation assumes perfect operation or perfect forecasts
6. **Independent technical review** of the model and results, especially if the study was funded or done by the proponent.

### 1.3 Step 2: Separate constraints from objectives that can be traded

- **City flood safety is a constraint, not a weight.** The city's protection standard, and any dam-safety requirement, should be set as a limit that every scheme must meet:
  - the point estimate is no worse than the current 1% level, and
  - the upper uncertainty bound on the paired difference stays within an agreed tolerance, and
  - severity indicators are not worse.

  Reasons for not trading it:
  - Flood losses are catastrophic, irreversible and spread unevenly; generation revenue is the opposite.
  - The legal and political accountability sits with the flood authority, not with the beneficiary of the revenue.
  - An expected-value trade (extra MWh against expected damage) ignores risk aversion toward low-probability, high-consequence events.

  If the commission ever wants to *relax* the city's standard, that is a separate policy decision. It needs its own legal basis and the consent of the flood authority and the city. It should not happen as a side effect of an operating-rule change.
- **Other minimum service levels.** If irrigation supply contracts or water-rights rules set a minimum reliability, or navigation has a statutory minimum depth or flow guarantee, those are also constraints. For example, if the irrigation contract requires ≥90% reliability, A is at the edge and C fails.
- **Among the schemes that pass, trade the remaining objectives openly.** Do not hide the trade inside one weighted score.
  - Show the trade-off rates directly. For example, "A gives +0.3 bn kWh/yr in exchange for −2 pp irrigation reliability and +2 navigation-shortfall days per year."
  - Turn into money only what can be valued credibly: energy revenue, crop losses in irrigation-failure years, and navigation delay or light-loading costs. Keep the rest in physical units.
  - Use multi-criteria analysis only as a way to let stakeholders state and compare their weights. Check how sensitive the ranking is to those weights.
- **Robustness.** Evaluate each scheme across several hydrological scenarios: historical, wetter, drier, more intense floods, and forecast failure. Prefer schemes that perform acceptably across all of them (low regret) over schemes that are best only under the central estimate.
- **Distribution and compensation.** Where a scheme moves benefits from one group to another (generation gains, irrigation and navigation losses), say who gains and who loses. Consider benefit-sharing: part of the extra revenue funds compensation or adaptation for the parties who lose.
- **Reversibility.** Prefer changes that can be introduced in stages, monitored and reversed. A seasonal operating rule is reversible in principle, but a single bad flood season is not.

## 2. Recommendation

### 2.1 Provisional judgment for each scheme

**Scheme B: do not adopt as proposed.**
- It raises the city's annual exceedance probability from 1.0% to 1.3%. That is roughly a 30% relative increase, and the return period of a flood above the threshold falls from about 100 to about 77 years.
- On the point estimate it does not meet the existing protection level. Under the constraint principle above, its gains on the other objectives cannot be traded against that.
- The burden of proof is on the party proposing the safety reduction. Without uncertainty intervals and severity indicators, that burden has not been met.
- B is still informative. It suggests that a higher flood-season level helps refill, which benefits irrigation and navigation. The commission should therefore ask for a **variant B′** that tries to keep those gains while bringing flood risk back to ≤1.0%, for example:
  - forecast-based pre-release, or a variable flood-limit level that drops when a large flood is forecast
  - the higher level applied only in the late flood season, when large floods are less likely
  - firm drawdown rules triggered by forecasts

  B′ could turn out to be the best scheme overall, but only once it has been modelled and shown to meet the flood constraint.

**Scheme C: do not take forward.**
- Its only advantage over the current rule on safety is −0.1 pp, which is very likely within the uncertainty.
- It costs 7 pp of irrigation reliability (92%→85%) and adds 6 navigation-shortfall days (+50%), for the smallest generation gain.
- It is the least attractive overall, unless the uncertainty analysis shows its flood reduction is real and meaningful and the city puts a high value on that.

**Scheme A: take forward to a second-stage assessment, with conditions. It is the leading candidate.**
- On the point estimates it keeps city flood risk unchanged.
- It gives +3% generation, which is the clearest benefit.
- Its costs fall on irrigation (−2 pp) and navigation (+2 days). Both are modest and can be compensated, provided they stay above any contractual or legal minimums.
- It is the only new scheme that passes the flood constraint on the point estimate and has moderate trade-offs.

**Current rule: keep it in force until A (or B′) has met the conditions below.** Adopting a change now, with no uncertainty information, would be premature.

### 2.2 Conditions before A (or B′) could be adopted

1. **Flood safety confirmed under uncertainty.**
   - The paired difference "A − current" in annual exceedance probability has an upper bound within an agreed tolerance. That tolerance is set *in advance* by the flood authority.
   - Severity indicators are no worse: peak flows at rarer return periods, how far and how long the threshold is exceeded, back-to-back floods, highest reservoir level, and spillway use.
   - Dam-safety requirements for the design flood are still met.
2. **Stress tests pass:**
   - floods larger than any in the 40-year record
   - forecast errors or missed forecasts
   - floods arriving early or late in the flood season
   - an inflow climate shifted toward more intense floods
3. **Irrigation and navigation stay at or above their minimum standards**, including at the lower uncertainty bound. Shortfalls in failure years and runs of consecutive shortfall days are not materially worse. If 90% is the contract minimum, A has no margin, and that needs explicit agreement or mitigation.
4. **Clear operating authority.**
   - The flood-control authority keeps the power to order drawdown when a flood is forecast or under defined trigger conditions, regardless of generation interests.
   - Trigger levels, how fast the reservoir must be drawn down, and who communicates what are written into the operating rules.
   - Forecast and monitoring capacity is shown to be good enough to support those triggers.
5. **Benefit-sharing and compensation.** A defined share of the extra generation revenue goes to:
   - compensating the irrigation district for added shortfalls, or providing drought reserves
   - navigation mitigation, such as dredging or scheduling support
   - possibly improving flood preparedness in the city
6. **Phased trial with monitoring and a clause for review or reversal.**
   - Run a trial for a fixed number of flood seasons, with indicators reported each year.
   - Pre-agreed triggers revert the rule. Examples: a reservoir level or downstream flow above defined limits during an event, or irrigation or navigation shortfalls outside the predicted range.
   - Hold a formal review at the end of the trial.
7. **Independent review** of the final analysis, and publication of results to stakeholders.

### 2.3 What would confirm or change this recommendation

- **It would move toward B (or B′):**
  - if B's flood-risk increase proves statistically indistinguishable from zero *and* its severity indicators are no worse, and the flood authority formally accepts it; or
  - if B′ achieves ≤1.0% with no worse severity while keeping most of B's gains. B′ would then probably be preferred to A, since it improves irrigation and navigation as well.
- **It would move to keeping the current rule:**
  - if A's flood-risk difference has an upper bound above the agreed tolerance, or its severity indicators are worse; or
  - if A's irrigation reliability falls below a contract minimum when uncertainty is included; or
  - if the economic value of +0.3 bn kWh/yr does not cover the cost of the irrigation and navigation losses plus the cost of conditions 4–6.
- **It would favour C** only if the flood authority shows that the city's current protection level is inadequate (for example, growing exposure or a non-stationary flood climate), C's flood reduction proves real, *and* the irrigation and navigation losses are acceptable or compensated. Even then, a better-designed flood-improving variant would probably beat C.
- **Updated hydrology** could shift all the results, so the conclusion should be re-tested under climate projections. Examples: evidence of rising flood magnitudes, or storage lost to sediment.

## 3. Who should take part, and how

Participation should be tiered: by legal authority, by who bears the risk, and by who has the information.

| Party | Role | How they take part |
|---|---|---|
| **Basin commission** | Decision-maker and convener | Sets the decision process and criteria in advance; makes the final decision; owns monitoring and review. |
| **City flood-control department** (and the city government / emergency management) | Holds the safety standard; must agree on flood matters | Sets the flood-safety constraint and tolerance *before* results are compared; reviews the flood analysis; must formally agree to any change affecting flood risk; keeps the power to order drawdown in an emergency; plans emergency response. |
| **Dam-safety regulator** (if separate) | Must approve on structural and dam-safety matters | Confirms that the dam-safety design flood and surcharge rules are still met at a higher operating level. |
| **Hydropower company** (proponent) | Proponent and information provider; bears the burden of proof | Provides the model, data and revenue analysis openly; funds or co-funds the additional analysis and independent review; takes on operating obligations, benefit-sharing and the conditions for reverting the rule. Its analysis should not be the only evidence the decision rests on. |
| **Irrigation district and farmer representatives** | Affected party | Help define the minimum irrigation-reliability and shortfall indicators; review results; negotiate compensation and drought reserves; take part in monitoring. |
| **Navigation authority and shipping operators** | Affected party / regulator of navigation | Define the minimum navigable flow or depth and how timing matters; review results; agree on mitigation. |
| **Hydrological and meteorological forecasting agency** | Technical partner | Provides forecast skill information that the forecast-based triggers depend on; takes part in designing B′ and the drawdown triggers; supplies operational forecasts during the trial. |
| **Water resources / energy ministries; power grid dispatcher** | Policy and system context | Confirm water-allocation rights and priorities; value the generation (peak value, firm output, grid needs); check consistency with national or regional rules. |
| **Environmental agency** (if it has a role in the basin) | Regulator of ecological flows | Confirms that ecological flow requirements are still met; the table does not report this, so it should be checked. |
| **Downstream communities and the public in the flood zone; upstream residents near the reservoir** | Bear the residual risk | Are informed openly and consulted, for example through public notice, hearings and comment periods. Upstream residents matter if a higher level affects the area flooded around the reservoir or land use there. |
| **Independent technical review panel** | Quality assurance | Reviews the models, uncertainty analysis, stress tests and the consistency of results; reports to the commission, not to any single party. |

How the process should run:

1. **Agree the rules before seeing results.** Before the comparison is reopened, the commission and parties agree on:
   - the indicators, including uncertainty and severity indicators
   - the constraints: the flood standard and tolerance, and the irrigation and navigation minimums
   - how the remaining trade-offs will be presented

   This reduces the temptation to pick criteria that favour a preferred scheme.
2. **Joint fact-finding.** Use one shared model and dataset, with access for all technical representatives. Additional runs (uncertainty, stress tests, B′) are defined together. Results go to all parties at the same time.
3. **Structured deliberation.** Hold facilitated workshops where the trade-off rates and the distribution of gains and losses are presented in physical and monetary terms. Stakeholders state their acceptable limits and weights, and the sensitivity of the ranking to those weights is shown.
4. **Formal consent where required.** The flood authority and the dam-safety regulator must formally approve on safety matters. Affected users have a formal role in the compensation and benefit-sharing agreement.
5. **Public transparency.** Publish the analysis, the decision and the reasons for it, the operating rules, and the trial-monitoring results.
6. **Monitoring and adaptive governance.** Run a joint monitoring committee during the trial, with:
   - annual reports
   - pre-agreed triggers for reverting the rule
   - a way for affected parties to raise complaints
   - a scheduled re-evaluation, especially as more hydrological data and climate information accumulate

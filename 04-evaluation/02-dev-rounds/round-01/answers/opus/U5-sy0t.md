# Recommendation to flood-control headquarters

## Provisional recommendation (conditional)

**Adopt a modified version of (a), led by the upper reservoir.** Start a moderate, reversible prerelease at the **upper** reservoir now. Use the **lower** reservoir as the buffer: keep its release near the current level instead of drawing it down hard. Re-decide magnitudes when the 12-hour forecast update arrives.

- Upper reservoir: ramp the release from 1,500 to about 2,500 m³/s over the next few hours, provided the channel between the dams can carry it. Be ready to go to 3,000–4,000 m³/s if the next forecast moves toward the upper end.
- Lower reservoir: no extra prerelease beyond what it is already doing. Hold the release at about 2,500 m³/s for now, and raise it only to pass the upper's prerelease water or a rising tributary. Keep the city flow well under 6,000 m³/s.

Ranking of the three proposals as they stand: **upper-led (a) > (c) > (b).** Option (b) draws down the reservoir that already has plenty of room and leaves the tight reservoir tight. Option (c) can be recovered from, but it gives up 12 of the 30 hours of lead time at the reservoir that needs them.

The information supports this direction. It does **not** support final release magnitudes. Those depend mainly on the shape of the upper inflow hydrograph and on the timing of the tributary flow (see the last section).

## The numbers behind it

These are quick volume checks from the given data. Hours are counted from now.

| | Upper | Lower |
|---|---:|---:|
| Free flood-control storage now | 0.40 × 300 = **120 million m³** | 0.85 × 500 = **425 million m³** |
| Occupied flood-control storage | 180 million m³ | 75 million m³ |
| Current net drawdown | 1,500 − 1,200 = 300 m³/s (≈1.1 million m³/h) | 2,500 − (≈1,500 + 300) ≈ 700 m³/s (≈2.5 million m³/h)* |
| Free storage vs forecast volume (150 / 250 / 400 million m³) | Below even the low end | Above even the high end |

*This assumes the upper has released about 1,500 m³/s for the past 18 hours. Both reservoirs are therefore already releasing more than they take in. The real question is whether, and where, to increase that drawdown.

- **Upper prerelease at 2,500 m³/s:** net drawdown about 1,300 m³/s, or about 4.7 million m³/h. Over 30 hours that frees about 140 million m³, so roughly **260 million m³ would be free when the flood arrives**, close to the central volume of 250. At 3,000 m³/s the upper's whole flood-control pool is empty in about 28 hours.
- **Upper prerelease if you wait 12 hours (option c):** about 18 hours remain. To reach the same free space the upper would need roughly 3,500–4,000 m³/s. Its 8,000 m³/s capacity makes that possible, so (c) can be recovered from. But it doubles the ramp rate, and the water arrives at the lower dam between about hour 30 and hour 48, which is when a tributary storm is more likely.
- **Where the prereleased water goes:** water released from the upper now reaches the lower dam at about hour 18 and the city at about hour 28. The flood itself cannot reach the city through the upper before about hour 58 (30 + 18 + 10). With the lower holding about 2,500 m³/s, its inflow from hour 18 is about 2,500 + 300 = 2,800 m³/s. It stores only about 300 m³/s, so it would still have **about 430–470 million m³ free** at hour 48. City flow stays about 2,500 m³/s, under half the safe flow.
- **High-end check (400 million m³) under the recommended action:** the upper stores about 260 and passes about 140 million m³ over 72 hours. That is an average of about 540 m³/s above base flow, so if the upper stores the peak, its releases can stay around 2,500–4,000 m³/s. Add a full tributary storm of 1,500 m³/s and the lower receives roughly 5,500 m³/s or less. The lower can pass that, or store the excess in its 400+ million m³. On volume, the city looks protected under all three options. **Peak, timing, and the upper dam's own safety bind, not total volume.**

## Main reasons

1. **The upper reservoir is the constraint.** Its 120 million m³ of free space is below even the low-end forecast of 150. The lower's 425 million m³ is above the high end. Prerelease effort should go where the space is short.
2. **Upper prerelease is low-regret in a cascade.** The released water is not lost to the system. It moves into the lower reservoir's spare space and can be held there if the flood turns out small. Even the low-end flood (150) would roughly refill what the upper releases (about 140 at 2,500 m³/s). The regret of acting is small; the regret of waiting is lost lead time.
3. **Lower prerelease has little value now and some cost.** The lower is already drawing down at about 700 m³/s and has ample space. More drawdown there releases water out of the system for good. It also uses city channel margin that may later be needed to pass the tributary and the upper's releases.
4. **Acting now keeps later options open.** The upper's capacity (8,000 m³/s) is far above the planned releases. That leaves room to speed up, slow down, or reverse after the update, and it protects against the flood arriving earlier than 30 hours or bringing more than 400 million m³.
5. **The main unmanaged risk is to the upper dam, not the city.** If the flood peak is sharp (for example, much of the volume in about 24 hours), the upper's peak inflow could approach or exceed 8,000 m³/s. With little free space, it would then surcharge above its flood pool. Making space at the upper is the only way to cover that case.

## Key assumptions (to be confirmed)

- **The forecast volume is flood volume on top of the current base inflow of about 1,200 m³/s.** If 250 million m³ is the *total* inflow over 72 hours, that averages only about 965 m³/s, which is below current inflow, and the event is mild. The recommendation still holds as a low-regret step but should then be scaled down.
- The free storage percentages refer to the flood-control pools only. Releases up to about 3,000–4,000 m³/s plus tributary flow are within the safe capacity of the channel between the dams, and within ramp-rate and regulatory limits.
- Local inflow between the lower dam and the city is small. It is not given, and it directly reduces the lower's allowable release below 6,000 m³/s.
- Travel times (18 hours and 10 hours) are roughly constant over this range of flows. Channel attenuation is ignored, which is conservative for peaks.
- The upper's inflow stays near 1,200 m³/s until the flood arrives.
- The forecast's 150–400 range reflects its real error at a 30–100 hour lead. Its skill at this lead time has not been verified here.
- Headquarters has the authority to order prerelease on a forecast basis.

## Actions to take immediately (within the hour)

1. **Order the upper to ramp to about 2,500 m³/s** in steps over 3–6 hours, within ramp-rate and channel limits between the dams. Notify the lower reservoir and downstream users.
2. **Instruct the lower to hold about 2,500 m³/s** and absorb the upper's extra water as it arrives (from about hour 18). Its release rule: increase only to pass tributary rises or to keep its free space from dropping below a set reserve. Provisionally, keep at least about 350–400 million m³ free until the tributary forecast is known. Hold city flow to about 5,000 m³/s or less until local inflow below the lower dam is known.
3. **Request from forecasters before the next update:** the upper inflow hydrograph (peak value, time to peak, single or double peak), ideally as an ensemble or quantiles; a tributary forecast with timing; local inflow below the lower dam; and the definition of the 250 million m³ figure.
4. **Run a quick scenario screen** with a water-balance and routing model of both reservoirs. Use the travel times above, tributary inflow at the lower dam, and local inflow to the city. Cover three volumes (150, 250, 400 million m³), at least three hydrograph shapes (early sharp peak, late peak, double peak), and tributary flows of 300 or 1,500 m³/s timed to coincide with the upper's releases arriving at the lower. Compare all three proposals plus the recommended variant. For each, check the upper's highest level against its flood pool and dam-safety levels, the release against 8,000 m³/s, the lower's highest storage, the city peak against 6,000 m³/s with margin, and the water left in storage at the end. For the upper dam, judge on the high-end scenario; for the water cost, judge on the central and low scenarios.
5. **Step up monitoring:** observed upper inflow (a rise before hour 30 means the timing forecast is wrong), tributary gauges and rainfall, and city-section flow.
6. **Pre-agree the switching thresholds below** so the 12-hour update leads to an immediate order rather than a new debate.

## What would change the recommendation

**Move to full (a), with stronger upper prerelease plus lower prerelease, if:**
- the next forecast's central volume is at or above about 350 million m³, the upper bound rises above 400, or arrival moves earlier than about 24 hours. Then the upper goes to 3,000–4,000 m³/s to empty its flood-control pool before arrival;
- the forecast upper peak inflow approaches about 6,000–8,000 m³/s, which is an upper dam-safety concern even with the upper empty;
- a tributary storm is forecast, or tributary flow rises above about 800–1,000 m³/s, and is timed to meet the upper's flood releases. Then the lower raises its release in advance, keeping the city below about 5,000–5,500 m³/s. The upper may need to slow its prerelease so the two flows do not stack at the lower;
- the scenario screen shows the lower's free space falling below what is needed to keep the city at or under 6,000 m³/s in the coinciding high-end case.

**Scale back toward (c) or the current releases if:**
- the next forecast drops to about 150 million m³ or below, or the storm track moves away from the upper basin. Then cut the upper back toward its inflow. The lower holds the water already released, so little is lost;
- channel capacity between the dams, ramp limits, or authority rules do not allow about 2,500 m³/s at the upper. Then prerelease at the largest allowed rate and accept more reliance on the 12-hour decision;
- the water has a high conservation value and refill is doubtful, for example if the 250 figure turns out to be total inflow rather than flood volume.

**Revise the lower's role if:**
- local inflow between the lower dam and the city is substantial, which cuts the lower's allowable release. The lower then needs more storage, and prerelease at the lower (option b's element) becomes worthwhile while the city still has margin.

## Is the information enough for a single choice?

It is enough to choose the **direction**: prerelease now at the upper, with the lower as the buffer. That holds because the choice is low-regret across the whole forecast range. It is **not** enough to fix how large the prerelease should be or the final split between the reservoirs. The most important missing information, in order:

1. **Upper inflow hydrograph shape and peak.** This decides whether the upper's 8,000 m³/s capacity plus its free space can handle the high-end flood without surcharge. It sets how far the upper must be drawn down. It is the largest effect.
2. **Tributary forecast and timing** relative to the arrival of the upper's releases at the lower dam. This decides the lower's peak load and whether the lower must make room in advance, and it governs the city peak.
3. **Local inflow between the lower dam and the city.** This sets the real limit on the lower's release below 6,000 m³/s.
4. **What the 250 (150–400) million m³ represents, and the forecast's verified skill at this lead time.** This affects the size of the event and how much to trust the range. Use the upper quantile for the upper dam's safety.
5. **Operating limits:** channel capacity between the dams, ramp-rate limits, and authority to prerelease on a forecast. These can cap the action regardless of what the analysis prefers.

None of these, except binding operating limits or a confirmed small event, is likely to reverse the decision to start the upper's prerelease now. They mainly set its rate and set when the lower moves from holding water to releasing it ahead of the flood.

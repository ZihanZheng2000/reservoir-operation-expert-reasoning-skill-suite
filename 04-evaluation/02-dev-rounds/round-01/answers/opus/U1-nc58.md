# Study design: staged (seasonal) flood-limited water levels for a multipurpose monsoon reservoir

## 1. Bottom line

**My provisional view.** A staged flood-limited level is worth studying here. The most likely gain is a higher allowed level in the parts of the monsoon when large floods are less frequent, especially the late monsoon. A higher late-season level helps the reservoir refill and carry water into the dry season for irrigation and hydropower. The main-season level will probably stay close to the current fixed level. This is a hypothesis, not a finding. It holds only if three things are true:

- the 45-year record shows clear seasonality in flood magnitude (peaks and multi-day volumes);
- under the current rule, the fixed level actually limits storage in those periods; if the reservoir seldom reaches the fixed level during the monsoon, raising it gains little;
- water stored above a lower next-stage level can be released safely and in time at each stage change.

**What the study must show.** The study should be built so that flood safety is a constraint that benefits cannot buy back. A staged rule becomes a candidate only if neither reservoir-side nor downstream (town) flood risk is higher than under the current rule. Water-use benefits are counted only after that.

**What the current data can and cannot support.** The data in hand can show whether a staged rule is promising and what it might gain. They cannot support recommending an actual rule change. At minimum, the study also needs:

- the town's safe (non-damaging) flow, or equivalently the safe dam release;
- the dam's design-flood standard and characteristic levels;
- the irrigation and hydropower demand and plant data;
- some sub-daily flood data.

## 2. Research question and hypotheses

**Main question.** Can the single fixed flood-limited level be replaced by a schedule of stage-specific levels so that, compared with the current rule under the same inflows:

- (a) reservoir-side flood risk does not increase, measured as the chance of exceeding the design maximum level and other safety levels;
- (b) flood risk to the town does not increase, measured as the frequency, size and duration of flows above the safe flow; and
- (c) water-use performance improves by a meaningful and robust amount: end-of-monsoon storage, refill probability, irrigation reliability (especially in dry years), hydropower energy and spill?

**Sub-questions:**

1. **Seasonality.** Does the 45-year record show statistically and physically meaningful flood seasonality? Where should the stage boundaries go, and how many stages can operators actually run?
2. **Binding.** Under the current rule, how often does the fixed level actually limit storage, in which periods, and in which kinds of years? This screens for potential benefit.
3. **Stage levels.** What is the highest safe level in each stage, given stage-specific design floods, outlet capacity, the safe downstream release and the time needed to draw down at stage changes?
4. **Forecasts (secondary, kept separate).** Do the 1–5-day forecasts add value on top of the staged levels, for example a bounded rise above the stage level when no flood is forecast, or earlier prerelease? Is that added value robust to forecast error?

**Hypotheses:**

- **H1.** Stage levels in the lower-flood periods can be raised above the current level without increasing risk.
- **H2.** Most of the benefit comes from higher late-monsoon storage and better refill, not from the early monsoon. At onset the reservoir is usually low anyway.
- **H3.** Using forecasts adds a further but smaller gain. That gain depends on skill at the lead time actually needed for drawdown.

**Why the forecast question is separate.** A staged rule that depends only on the calendar has to stand on its own, without forecasts. It is simpler to approve and does not fail when a forecast misses. Forecast-informed adjustment should be tested as an optional second layer, compared against the staged rule alone.

**First action:** confirm the critical items with the dam owner and operator. The study should be written so that a usable but limited result is still possible if they are not obtained (see Section 9).

## 4. Methods, in order

### 4.1 Baseline and diagnosis

- **Encode the current rule.** Write the current operating rule as an executable daily simulator: water balance, the release rule for irrigation and hydropower below the flood-limited level, flood-operation releases above it, and outlet capacity from the level–discharge relation.
- **Check the simulator.** If historical levels and releases are available, compare simulated with observed levels, releases and spills.
- **Simulate the 45 years under the current rule.** Record spill, floodwater use, end-of-monsoon storage and refill frequency, irrigation deficits, energy, maximum levels, and releases above the safe release.
- **Diagnose where the fixed level binds.** For each 10-day period of the monsoon, find how often the level is held down to the fixed level and how much water is spilled or released because of it. This shows where a staged level could help. If it binds mainly in September–October, gains will come from the late stage. If it hardly binds, the study should say so early and the gains will be small.
- **Route the design flood(s) from the current fixed level.** This gives the current maximum level and peak release, which become the safety benchmark.

### 4.2 Flood seasonality and stage division

- **Describe seasonality from the 45-yr record.** Look at the timing of annual maxima, and at peaks and 1-, 3-, 5- and 7-day maximum volumes by 10-day period. Use the volume window that matters for this reservoir's flood storage. If the reservoir can smooth out short peaks, multi-day volume matters more than the daily peak.
- **Divide the season into stages** with an objective statistical method, such as change-point or clustering of flood statistics by period. Then apply operating limits:
  - few stages (usually 2–3, e.g., early, main and late monsoon);
  - each stage long enough to operate;
  - boundaries that match known monsoon behaviour.
- **Check stationarity.** Test for trends or shifts in magnitude and timing, for example comparing the last 20 years with the earlier record. If timing has shifted, base the stage boundaries on the recent period and test the result against the full record.

### 4.3 Stage-specific design floods

- **Fit frequency distributions to stage maxima.** Use peak and governing-volume maxima within each stage, choosing the distribution by goodness-of-fit and keeping candidates if they fit equally well.
- **Quantify sampling uncertainty.** Use bootstrap confidence intervals: 45 values per stage give wide upper tails.
- **Keep the annual standard consistent.** If every stage used the full annual return period on its own maximum, the combined annual chance of exceedance would be larger than the design standard. So either:
  - assign stage exceedance probabilities whose combination equals the annual standard, or, preferably,
  - check the annual risk directly, by continuous simulation over the whole year (Section 5).
- **Build stage design hydrographs** by scaling observed typical floods of each stage to the stage quantiles. Match both peak and volume, and use several shapes, including multi-peak and back-to-back events. Correct daily peaks to instantaneous peaks.

### 4.4 Candidate stage levels and feasibility

- **Find the highest safe level in each stage.** Starting from each candidate level on a grid (e.g., 0.1–0.25 m steps), route the stage design floods using the same flood-operation rule as the current rule. Keep the highest level for which:
  - the maximum level stays at or below the design maximum (and other safety levels) for the dam standard;
  - the release stays at or below the safe release for the town's protection standard;
  - the result is no worse than routing from the current fixed level.
- **Check the stage changes.** Where the next stage has a lower level, the extra storage must be released before the boundary date at or below the safe downstream release, allowing for inflow and normal operating releases. This decides how early drawdown must begin, and how much of the gain is lost by releasing that water.
- **Consider the late-stage refill path.** The late-stage higher level should allow controlled filling toward FRL. Check that a late flood can still be absorbed from the level reached at any time in that stage.
- **Apply hard limits:** outlet capacity by level, release ramping (if known), minimum flows, and a gate-outage case.

### 4.5 Continuous simulation of candidate schemes

- **Run every candidate scheme and the baseline through the same 45-yr daily record**, with the same initial state, demands and conservation rule.
- **Add a synthetic long series**, e.g., thousands of years from a stochastic model that preserves seasonality, persistence and the dependence between flood peak and volume. Its purpose is to estimate risks rarer than the record can show and to include unobserved combinations, such as an early large flood arriving when the level is high.
- **Check that the synthetic series is faithful:** seasonal statistics, distribution of annual and stage maxima, multi-day volumes and the largest historical events.
- **Use sub-daily routing for flood events.** Take the reservoir state from the daily run at flood onset and route the event at an hourly or 3-hourly step, so peak levels and releases are not smoothed out.

## 5. How flood safety will be assessed

**Unsafe outcomes:**

- **Dam side:** maximum level exceeding the design maximum level (MWL) or other safety levels (e.g., FRL during flood operation).
- **Town side:** flow at the town above the safe flow. If local inflow data are missing, use dam release above the safe release instead.
- **Duration and volume** of exceedance at both points.

**Measures:**

- Chance of exceedance per stage and per year. Annual risk is computed from continuous simulation, not by adding stage design probabilities.
- A tail measure, such as the expected size of exceedance given that it occurs (e.g., CVaR of excess level or excess release volume). This makes sure risk is not pushed into rarer but worse events.
- Count and duration of days with release above the safe release.
- Results from routing the design floods: maximum level and peak release per stage, compared with the current fixed level.

**Acceptable-risk benchmark:**

- Primary: the current rule's risk under the same inflows and conditions, for both the dam and the town, with no increase allowed.
- Secondary: the official design standard and the town's protection standard, both of which must still be met.

**Uncertainties to include:**

| Uncertainty | How it is handled |
|---|---|
| Stage design flood quantiles | Report results at the central estimate and at the upper confidence bound |
| Hydrograph shape | Several typical shapes, including multi-peak and back-to-back monsoon events |
| Level at flood onset | Taken from the simulated distribution, not fixed at the stage level |
| Daily-to-peak conversion | Sensitivity cases |
| Discharge capacity | Gate-outage case |
| Storage curve | Sedimentation case, if the survey is old |
| Local inflow downstream | Assumed ratios, if not measured |

**Stage-boundary risk.** Test explicitly what happens when a flood arrives during the drawdown period before a lower stage begins, and when the stage boundaries are shifted by about ±10 days.

## 6. How the other objectives will be assessed

**Indicators,** from the 45-yr run and the synthetic run:

- **Storage and refill:** end-of-monsoon storage; chance of reaching FRL or a target level by the end of the monsoon.
- **Irrigation:** reliability, deficit volume and worst-year deficit, reported separately for dry years and by season (kharif/rabi).
- **Hydropower:** annual and firm energy, including the effect of the higher head.
- **Spill and floodwater use:** spill volume; share of inflow used.
- **Release changes needed at stage transitions,** a cost to operators.

**Trade-offs and selection:**

- Safety is applied first as a filter. Only schemes that pass all safety tests are compared on benefits.
- Among the safe schemes, keep the non-dominated set across irrigation, hydropower and refill rather than collapsing them early into one weighted score. Irrigation and hydropower may compete over release timing.
- If a single scheme must be chosen, use a transparent ranking method with weights from the operator and irrigation department. Show how stable the choice is under different weights and across wet, normal and dry years.
- Explain where the gains come from, e.g., "higher September level → earlier refill → lower rabi deficit". Do not report aggregate numbers alone.

## 7. Using the 8 years of forecasts

**Check forecast quality first:**

- Measure skill and error by lead time (1–5 d): bias, error spread and error distribution.
- Break this down by flow level and by stage. High-flow skill is what matters, and 8 years will contain few large floods.
- Check whether errors are correlated across lead times, whether error grows with flow, and whether a normal error model is defensible. If it is not, fit a better model.
- Use the error model to generate synthetic forecasts for the 45-yr and synthetic records. Check that these reproduce the observed skill, especially for the largest events in the 8 years.

**Possible forecast layer (optional, tested separately):**

- Within a stage, the level may rise above the stage level only by the volume that can be released within the effective lead time at or below the safe downstream release, after subtracting forecast inflow. Effective lead time is the forecast lead time minus decision and gate-operation delay and minus travel time to the town.
- If a large inflow is forecast, start prerelease early. Use a conservative (upper) quantile of forecast inflow, not the central forecast.
- Fallback: if a flood exceeds the forecast, return to the staged rule's drawdown.

**Compare three information conditions** under identical inflows:

- staged rule with no forecast;
- staged rule plus actual or synthetic forecasts;
- staged rule plus perfect forecasts, as an upper bound only.

This separates the value of the staged levels from the value of the forecasts.

**Stress test:** a missed forecast immediately followed by a large flood, with the level at its raised maximum.

**Limit of the claim:** with only 8 years, any forecast-layer conclusion is provisional and should be presented as an option for later assessment, not part of the main recommendation.

## 8. Testing whether the result is credible and robust

- **Same-condition comparison:** every scheme against the current rule with identical inflows, initial states, demands and constraints.
- **Historical events:** replay the 5–10 largest floods, and multi-flood monsoons, through both rules. Show full trajectories (level, release, town flow), not only summary statistics.
- **Split-sample test:** derive stage boundaries and levels from one part of the record (e.g., 30 years) and evaluate safety and benefits on the rest. Also use leave-one-year-out for benefits. This tests whether the result depends on a few years.
- **Sensitivity and stress:**
  - stage boundaries;
  - number of stages;
  - design-quantile upper bounds;
  - scaled floods (e.g., 1.2× and 1.5× the largest observed floods);
  - successive floods;
  - gate outage;
  - reduced storage from sedimentation;
  - recent-period hydrology only;
  - assumed local inflow ratios.
- **Method checks:** compare risk estimates from the historical record, the synthetic series and design-flood routing. Check that risk estimates are stable as the number of synthetic years grows.
- **Applicability:** state that the conclusions apply to this reservoir, its flood regime, its outlet capacity and its downstream conditions only.

## 9. What results would justify recommending a rule change

**Required, with no trade-off against benefits:**

1. **Dam safety.** In every stage, and for the year as a whole, the chance of exceeding the design maximum level and other safety levels is no higher than under the current rule. Stage design floods meet the official design standard. Both hold at the upper confidence bound of the design flood estimates and with sub-daily peaks.
2. **Town safety.** Frequency, peak size and duration of flows above the safe flow at the town do not increase. This must be checked with the real safe-flow value and with local inflow included or bounded. The tail measure also does not increase.
3. **Stage changes and fallback.** Drawdown at every stage change is feasible within safe release limits. The stress cases (flood during drawdown, successive floods, gate outage, scaled floods) show no new failure that the current rule avoids. If there is such a failure, there is a defined mitigation, such as an earlier drawdown date.
4. **Historical replay.** No historical flood produces a worse maximum level or town flow under the staged rule.

**Required for the benefit claim:**

5. **The benefit is meaningful.** It is large enough, in refill probability, dry-year irrigation deficit and energy, to justify the added operating complexity. The threshold should be agreed with the operator and irrigation authority beforehand; I would not set it alone.
6. **The benefit is robust.** It holds in the split-sample and leave-one-out tests, in the recent-period run and across reasonable stage boundaries. It does not depend on one or two wet or dry years.
7. **The benefit is explained** by an identified mechanism, such as late-season refill.

**Required for credibility and implementability:**

8. **The simulator is credible.** It reproduces historical operation where records exist. The data limits (daily inflows, the age of the storage curve) are resolved or bounded.
9. **The rule can be operated.** It is a simple schedule of stage dates, stage levels and transition drawdown rules that operators can follow. It has an approval route through the dam owner, the dam-safety review and the irrigation/power stakeholders. After approval, a monitored trial over one or more monsoons with review after each flood season is advisable before the operating manual is permanently changed.

**What claim each evidence level supports:**

| Evidence obtained | Supported claim |
|---|---|
| Only current data; downstream safety and design standard by proxy | "Staged levels appear promising, with estimated gains of X. Safety is shown only against proxies." Not a recommendation to change the rule |
| Plus safe town flow, design standard and sub-daily floods; all safety criteria met | Recommend the staged schedule for formal safety review and a trial |
| Plus all robustness tests passed and stakeholders agree on benefits | Recommend replacing the fixed level, subject to approval and monitored trial operation |
| Forecast layer | Only an option for later assessment, given 8 years of forecasts, unless longer hindcasts become available |

## 10. Key assumptions and what would change the conclusion

**Assumptions:**

- The current fixed level was set from an annual design flood. The flood season is mainly the southwest monsoon, with some seasonal structure in flood size.
- Flood operation above the level follows the current rule's release logic.
- Travel time to the town is within about a day.
- Demands can be taken from the current rule if no better data exist.

**What would weaken or reverse the case for staged levels:**

- Flood seasonality is weak, or the largest floods can occur late in the season, for example from late-monsoon depressions or cyclones.
- The fixed level rarely limits storage under the current rule.
- The drawdown needed at stage changes uses up most of the gain, or exceeds the safe release.
- Stage-wise design-quantile uncertainty is so wide that the upper bound removes the headroom.
- Recent years show a shift in flood timing.

**What would strengthen it:**

- A clear late-monsoon drop in multi-day flood volumes.
- Frequent late-season spill or failure to refill under the current rule.
- Large outlet capacity relative to late-stage floods.
- A generous safe flow at the town.

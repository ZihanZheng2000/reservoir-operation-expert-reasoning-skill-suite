# Advice to the head of the agency: should the ML release model replace the current rule next flood season?

## Recommendation (provisional)

**Do not adopt the model directly as a replacement for the current operating rule next season.** The evidence supports "this model is worth a serious, staged evaluation." It does not support "this model is safe and better than our rule." My provisional route is:

1. Keep the current rule as the governing rule next season.
2. Over the coming months, run the evaluations below (offline, then shadow/parallel operation).
3. If next season allows, use the model only as an advisory or shadow comparator, with the operators' existing rule and authority unchanged.
4. Consider a controlled, bounded pilot (for example limited to small and medium floods, or as a recommendation checked against hard safety envelopes) only after the gates below are passed and the agency's approval bodies agree.

What would change this: a convincing multi-event out-of-sample test against the *actual current rule* (not only against operators' historical decisions), a worst-case/failure-mode analysis that shows bounded downside, and confirmation that the model runs on real-time inputs with a proven fallback. If all of those are in hand before the season starts, a limited pilot could be justified earlier. If any is missing, shadow mode only.

Key assumptions I am making (the question does not state them): the "30% lower maximum outflow" is a simulated number from one retrospective event; the "actual operator decisions" are the comparator; the paper does not report results for other events, other baselines, stress cases, or real-time deployment; I do not know which inputs the model uses (observed only, or forecasts) or whether those inputs were available in real time.

## 1. What the current evidence can support

**Supported (modestly):**
- On the 2023 event, in the authors' simulator, the model's trajectory would have produced a peak release about 30% lower than the one operators actually produced. That is a proof of concept that the approach can find lower-peak operations in at least one case.
- The idea is plausible: data-driven policies can exploit patterns, such as earlier or smoother pre-release, that fixed rules miss. This justifies further study.

**Not supported:**
- *That the model would perform better in the next flood.* This is one event. A single flood is not a distribution. Peak reduction depends on the event's size, shape, timing, antecedent storage and forecast quality. Papers in this field show benefits can be positive in some events and negative in others (small floods can give no benefit or even negative added storage), and results can depend on one flood, one parameter set, or one record.
- *That it beats the current rule.* The comparator was what operators actually did in 2023, which is not the same as the current operating rule. Operators faced real-time uncertainty, incomplete information, institutional and downstream constraints, and possibly deviated from the rule. Historical operation is context, not a controlled counterfactual, because starting levels and decisions differ. The right comparator is the current rule encoded in executable form and run under identical conditions (same inflows, same initial level, same constraints), plus a no-forecast and perfect-forecast bracket to isolate the model's contribution.
- *That the 30% was achieved with information operators would have had.* A retrospective simulation can leak information. Questions: did the model use the observed inflow of the event, a hindcast, or forecasts that were really available in real time? Did it see data (for example rainfall observed after the decision time) that was not available at decision time? Perfect-information results are upper bounds and are not deployable.
- *That a lower maximum outflow means a safer outcome.* Peak outflow is a proxy. Safety also depends on the maximum reservoir level and the margin to dam-safety thresholds, the duration and timing of high releases, downstream stage at control points, the rate of release change, and whether storage was used up before the peak. Lowering the peak release by holding more water means a higher pool and less remaining capacity if a second wave arrives. A model that cuts peak outflow by 30% may have done so by trading dam safety or later-flood risk, which a single metric hides.
- *That the training/test arrangement is clean.* The model was trained on 2010–2022 and tested on 2023. That is a temporal holdout, which is better than random splitting, but it is still one test event. Check that 2023 was not used in hyperparameter selection, model selection or threshold tuning, and that the training years' decisions (which are operator decisions) are not simply being imitated or conflated with "optimal" labels. Also, 2010–2022 contains few extreme floods; the model may have almost no experience of events near or above the design flood, where the consequences are largest.
- *That it is robust to failure.* A black-box ML model has no inherent guarantee of respecting release limits, ramping limits, or level limits, and may behave unpredictably out of distribution, on corrupted or missing inputs, or with sensor outages.
- *That it is transferable or institutionally implementable.* There is no evidence about operating authority, regulations, operator acceptance or approval.

## 2. Unresolved questions

Grouped by what they threaten.

**A. Baseline and comparison validity**
1. Is the current operating rule implemented as an executable comparator, and how does the model compare against it on the same 2023 inflow and initial state? What were the operators' actual deviations from the rule and why?
2. Was the 30% measured against actual releases, the rule's simulated releases, or both? Is the retrospective simulator validated (does it reproduce the observed 2023 levels and outflows when fed the actual releases)?
3. Does the comparison use identical conditions: same initial level, same forecast information, same constraints?
4. Is the information condition fair: what did the model see, at what lead time, and was that available in real time?

**B. Robustness and generality**
5. How does it perform across other historical events, including those in the training period under leave-one-year-out or leave-one-event-out validation, small, medium and large floods, multi-peak floods and successive floods?
6. How does it behave for floods larger than anything in 2010–2022 (design floods, scaled floods)? Which events lie inside the training distribution?
7. How sensitive is the outcome to the model's randomness (seeds), hyperparameters, and training window?
8. Is the 30% a mean effect or the best case? What is the distribution of the benefit, including worst-case events?

**C. Safety and risk**
9. What happens to maximum pool level, margin to safety levels, remaining flood storage, and downstream stages at control points (not only peak outflow)? Does it ever exceed hard limits, such as maximum level, discharge capacity, ramping limits, downstream channel safe flow?
10. What is the risk benchmark? The model should be no riskier than the current rule on the agreed risk measures (exceedance probability of critical levels, downstream threshold exceedance, tail losses), with safety treated as a constraint and not a trade-off.
11. What does the model do when its inputs are wrong (forecast error, missing sensors, delayed data)? Where is the point beyond which forecast error makes it worse than the rule?
12. How are cascade and downstream interactions handled (if the reservoir sits in a system with other reservoirs, travel times, uncontrolled local inflows)?

**D. Forecast and input dependence**
13. Does the model depend on forecasts? If so, what is the forecast skill by lead time, and is the model's value coming from forecast quality or from the policy? Forecast accuracy is not operational value; operational value needs to be shown against no-forecast and perfect-forecast references.
14. Are the same inputs and lead times available operationally, with the same timeliness and quality as in the retrospective data (including bias correction and quality control that were done after the fact)?

**E. Implementation and institutions**
15. Can the model's recommendation be explained to and challenged by operators? Is there an interpretable rationale for release decisions, and what are the override rules?
16. Is replacement of an approved operating rule permitted under existing regulations, water-control manual and upstream/downstream stakeholders' agreements? Who has the authority to approve?
17. Computing, data pipeline, latency, cybersecurity, and monitoring requirements; who maintains and retrains the model?

## 3. Evaluations and preparations before any adoption

**Step 1. Establish the baseline properly (offline).**
- Encode the current operating rule (including level bands, release caps, ramping, downstream control-point limits) as an executable simulator. Validate the simulator against observed 2023 and other historical events: simulated vs observed levels and outflows when driven by actual decisions.
- Define the comparators: the current rule, historical operator decisions (as context), no-forecast operation, perfect-forecast operation (upper bound only), and, if forecasts are used, deterministic and ensemble forecast conditions.
- Fix the metric set before running: peak outflow, maximum level, margin to safety thresholds, time above downstream safe flow, release ramps, downstream stage at control points, remaining flood storage, refill performance, spill, benefit measures (generation, water supply) as secondary.

**Step 2. Verify the retrospective result itself.**
- Reproduce the 30% result independently. Check for information leakage (inputs available only after the decision time, post-event corrections, model selection on 2023).
- Check that the hindcast inputs are the ones that would really have been available in real time, with realistic data latency and missing data.
- Examine the 2023 trajectory, not just the maximum: when did the model release, what pool level did it hold, how much remaining storage did it leave, and what mechanism explains the reduction (earlier pre-release, flatter releases, higher pool)? A reduction that rests on an unexplained or unsafe mechanism is not credible.

**Step 3. Test out of sample, across events and conditions.**
- Leave-one-year-out or leave-one-event-out testing over 2010–2023 (retrain each time without the test event); expanding-window temporal validation; several random seeds.
- Test across event types (small, medium, large; single and multi-peak; successive floods; different seasons/antecedent levels) and report the full distribution of benefits and harms, including the worst events.
- Stress tests: scaled and synthetic floods beyond the historical record (for example up to design-flood and check-flood magnitudes), events with different hydrograph shapes, and a second flood arriving immediately after the first.
- Sensitivity to forecast error and input degradation: vary error level, bias, correlation and lead time; find the error level beyond which the model is worse than the rule. Test missing or delayed sensor data.
- Test the other direction as well: whether the model is overly cautious or wasteful in small floods (lost refill, lost generation).

**Step 4. Safety envelope and risk assessment.**
- Treat safety as non-negotiable: define the acceptable-risk benchmark (the current rule's risk or a design standard) and require the model to be no worse on those measures across the test set, with the tail (worst cases) examined and not only averages.
- Impose hard constraints outside the model (release capacity, ramping, level limits, downstream safe flow) in a safety layer that overrides or clips any model recommendation; test that the layer works. Check feasibility: can the model's pre-release finish within the forecast lead time, is storage enough to absorb the design flood, and can released water be refilled?
- Define fallback: if inputs are missing, forecast is outside its reliable range, or the model output fails a sanity check, revert automatically to the current rule or a measurement-based rule.
- Define stop/escalation triggers (for example levels or forecast thresholds) that return control to the operators.

**Step 5. Explainability and operator interface.**
- Provide a physically interpretable explanation of each recommendation (for example forecast volume, available storage, risk margin) so operators can check it against their own judgment.
- Review the model's decisions with experienced operators on historical events and ask where they would disagree and why; capture local knowledge not in the data.

**Step 6. Shadow operation (next flood season, advisory only).**
- Run the model in real time in parallel with the current rule, with no operational authority. Log inputs, recommendations, the actual decisions, and outcomes; compare with the rule's recommendations on the same real-time information. This tests data pipelines, latency, robustness and model-vs-rule agreement under real conditions without risking safety.
- Pre-agree the criteria that must be met to move on (number and type of events, no safety-metric violations, performance relative to the rule).

**Step 7. Institutional review and approval.**
- Cross-agency and stakeholder review (dam safety authority, downstream authorities, power and water-supply users). Confirm what the legal and regulatory framework permits regarding replacing or supplementing the operating rule, and who approves.
- Plan a staged route: preliminary assessment, in-depth assessment, then formal revision of the operating manual, with decisions at each stage (defer, research further, implement). Resource a post-event review process.

**Step 8. Controlled pilot, only if the above gates are met.**
- A bounded pilot: for example limited flood magnitudes, limited deviation from the current rule, with the safety layer and manual override, and a defined end and review. Treat a single pilot season as demonstration evidence, not proof of universal safety, and not a replacement for the stress and out-of-sample tests.
- After the event, do a reanalysis: model vs rule vs actual outcome, what the model did, failures and near-misses, and update the model and procedures accordingly.

**Step 9. Governance for long-term operation.**
- Monitoring for data drift and non-stationarity (climate, land use, reservoir sedimentation, changed downstream conditions), retraining and revalidation rules, version control, and an audit trail of decisions.
- Operator training and a clear accountability chain: who is responsible when the model's recommendation is followed or overridden.

## 4. How I would tell the head of the agency, in short

- The paper shows one promising simulated case. It is a reason to investigate, not a reason to replace the rule.
- The 30% figure is not directly comparable to a decision the agency would take, because it compares against historical operator actions on a single event, in retrospect, and measures only peak outflow.
- Replacing a flood-control rule is an asymmetric-risk decision: an unrecognized failure in a rare large flood costs far more than a season of delay costs. The burden of proof should sit with the model.
- For the next season: keep the current rule, run the model in shadow mode, and in the meantime complete the baseline, multi-event, stress, and safety-envelope tests. Decide on a limited pilot based on pre-agreed criteria, with fallbacks and approval from the relevant authorities.
- If the head needs one sentence to carry: "Not as a direct replacement this season; yes to testing it against our actual rule and running it in parallel, with a decision after the evidence is in."

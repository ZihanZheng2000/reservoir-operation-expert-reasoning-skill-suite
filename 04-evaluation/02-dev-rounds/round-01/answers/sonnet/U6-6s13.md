# Advice to the basin commission

## Bottom line (provisional)

- **Do not adopt any of the three schemes on the evidence as it stands. Keep the current rule in force for now.**
- **Drop Scheme C.** It is almost dominated by A. Its irrigation reliability (85%) and navigation shortfall (18 days) are the worst of all four, and its flood-risk gain (0.9% against 1.0%) is far smaller than the sampling noise of a 40-year record.
- **Carry A and B forward for further work, with different conditions.**
  - **A** keeps flood risk equal to the current rule and gains 0.3 billion kWh (+3%). It pays for this with irrigation reliability down 2 points and 2 more navigation-shortfall days.
  - **B** gains the most generation (+5%) and is better on irrigation (+3 points) and navigation (-3 days). Its flood risk rises from 1.0% to 1.3%, a 30% relative increase. That is the one indicator on which the city has a safety claim.
- B is the only scheme that looks attractive to the water-supply and navigation users. It is also the only one that raises downstream flood risk. Most of the decision therefore comes down to whether B's extra 0.3 percentage points of risk is real, and whether it can be removed by changing how B is operated.
- A gentler B (a partial raise, or a raise that is released early when a forecast flags a flood) may keep most of the benefit with no added risk. I would test that variant before any decision.
- This is a provisional judgment on a table with no uncertainty intervals. The conditions that would change it are listed in section 2.

## 1. How to compare the schemes and handle the trade-offs

### 1.1 Make the evidence comparable before ranking

The table is not yet decision-grade, for five reasons.

1. **No uncertainty intervals.**
   - A 1.0% annual exceedance probability in 40 years of record corresponds to about 0.4 events. 1.3% corresponds to about 0.5. The record cannot resolve a 0.3 percentage-point difference.
   - The numbers rest on the probabilistic model and the simulated scenarios: the tail of the flood distribution, how floods are scaled, and how forecast error is represented. A threshold-exceedance probability near 1% is a tail estimate and is highly model-dependent.
   - Ask for intervals on every indicator, using bootstrap or repeated scenario sets. Report the result as the probability that each scheme beats the current rule, not as point values.
2. **Paired comparison.**
   - All four schemes should be run on the same inflow sequences, initial levels, forecast errors, and demand series. Report differences scheme minus current rule, with intervals on the differences.
   - Differences evaluated on common random numbers are far tighter than differences between independent estimates.
   - Check that the simulator reproduces the historical record (levels, releases, downstream flows) before trusting the comparison.
3. **Robustness of the flood-risk indicator.**
   - Test with floods scaled beyond the historical range, different record windows (for example leave-one-year-out), a non-stationary flood regime, different forecast-error levels and correlations, and different scenario counts. Check whether the A and current-rule tie, and the B penalty, survive.
   - If the B penalty depends on a single large flood or a single parameter, it is not a stable result.
4. **Mechanism for each result.** Some numbers need explanation before they can be trusted.
   - **B is better than the current rule on irrigation and navigation despite a higher flood risk.** The likely mechanism is more water held through the flood season. That must be confirmed against the releases, not assumed.
   - **A raises generation yet loses on irrigation and navigation.** That suggests a different release pattern, possibly power-driven drawdown. The model should show it.
   - If a result cannot be explained by a mechanism, it may be a model artifact.
5. **Indicator definitions.**
   - What is the safe threshold at the city, and is it a flow, a stage, or a damage trigger?
   - What counts as irrigation reliability (the fraction of years the storage target is met, or a volume-based measure)?
   - Is navigation "insufficient flow" counted against a single minimum, and over which reach?
   - The table gives only one flood indicator, the annual exceedance probability. It says nothing about how far above the threshold the flow goes, for how long, or how fast the river rises.
   - Add severity and duration measures, a tail measure such as the expected exceedance in bad years (CVaR) or the maximum flow, the worst-year outcome for each scheme, and the exceedance probability for design-size floods.
   - Equal exceedance probability does not mean equal harm. Two schemes at 1.0% can differ greatly in the size of the exceedance.
   - Also report the benefit indicators in terms the decision-makers can value, such as revenue, shortfall volumes, and years with failures. Not just means.

### 1.2 Handle trade-offs: safety first, then a transparent comparison of the rest

1. **Treat downstream flood safety as a constraint, not as one objective to be traded for revenue.**
   - Set the acceptable-risk benchmark before looking at benefits. The natural benchmark is the current rule's risk, or the city's protection standard if that is stricter. The flood-control department should own that benchmark.
   - A scheme that breaches the benchmark is not eligible for a trade, whatever it gains elsewhere.
   - Applying this literally, B fails as specified (1.3% > 1.0%) unless the intervals show it is indistinguishable from the current rule, or the benchmark is explicitly relaxed by the body that owns it. A and C pass on this indicator, subject to intervals.
   - Dam safety is a second, separate safety constraint. It was not reported. A higher flood-season level reduces freeboard, so check the peak reservoir level against the dam safety limit for the design flood and the extreme flood.
2. **Check feasibility before any scoring.**
   - Can the higher level be lowered before a flood arrives? That depends on how much release capacity exists, how much forecast lead time there is, the downstream safe-release limit, and travel time.
   - Does a raised level leave enough room to absorb the design flood?
   - Can the reservoir refill afterwards, so the irrigation gain is real?
   - If drawdown depends on forecasts, check how reliable forecasts are at the lead time the action needs, and what happens when a forecast is wrong. The comparison should bracket the forecast value between a no-forecast run and a perfect-forecast run.
   - This is where B may be fixed or may fail.
3. **Among schemes that pass safety and feasibility, keep the non-inferior set and do not collapse to one score.**
   - The remaining objectives (power, irrigation, navigation) are in different units and are valued differently by different parties. Do not weight them into a single number to decide.
   - On the table, no scheme dominates the others. A and C lose to the current rule on two indicators. B is better on three and worse on one. C is almost dominated by A.
   - Show the commission the trade-off for each scheme in plain terms: for example, "A: +0.3 billion kWh, in exchange for 2 points of irrigation reliability and 2 more navigation days."
4. **If a ranking is needed, make it transparent and test it.**
   - Agree the criteria and the weights with the stakeholders first, and do not choose them to fit a preferred answer.
   - Run a stability test: vary the weights, drop a criterion, and use more than one ranking method. Report whether the order of A, B, and the current rule changes.
   - For uncertain weights, a stochastic sampling method that reports rank acceptability is more honest than a single set of weights.
5. **Look at who gains and who loses.** Hydropower gains in A and B. Irrigation and navigation lose in A and C. The city bears the extra risk in B. If a scheme transfers cost from the proposer to others, the decision involves compensation or sharing of the gain, which is a policy decision, not a modelling one.

## 2. Recommendation, basis, and conditions

### 2.1 Scheme by scheme

| Scheme | Provisional view | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Current rule | Keep as operating rule for now | It is the baseline and the only option with an operating record. Nothing in the table clears the bar to replace it. |
| A | Carry forward as a candidate; the most plausible "adoptable" option | Risk equal to the baseline (subject to intervals), +3% generation. Net cost to irrigation (-2 points) and navigation (+2 days), which look small but are unquantified. Worth pursuing only if those losses can be removed or compensated, for example by refill or release rules, or if the intervals show they are within noise. |
| B | Carry forward for redesign and further evidence, not for adoption as specified | Largest benefits for three of four objectives, but the only flood-risk increase. It cannot be adopted as specified under a safety-first rule unless the risk difference is shown to be noise, or the flood authority accepts the higher risk. If its risk can be returned to 1.0% or below by a modified rule (a forecast-triggered drawdown, a lower or time-varying ceiling, a firm upper bound), the modified B is the best candidate. |
| C | Drop | Lower generation than A and B, large irrigation (-7 points) and navigation (+6 days) losses, and a flood-risk improvement of 0.1 percentage point that is within noise. Almost dominated by A. |

### 2.2 Why the current rule stays for now

- The decision uses a safety-first logic: the benchmark is the current rule's own risk, and a scheme must show it does not exceed it.
- No uncertainty is reported, so it is not possible to say whether A's "equal" risk, B's increase, or C's decrease are real.
- A and C lose on irrigation and navigation, and B loses on flood risk. Nothing is clearly better on every indicator.
- Keeping the current rule is not a vote that it is optimal. It is the default until the evidence is adequate.

### 2.3 Conditions for moving to adoption

1. **Intervals and paired differences** on all four indicators, with a statement of what difference the evidence can and cannot resolve.
2. **Robustness** of the flood-risk ranking to extreme and scaled floods, non-stationarity, forecast error, and scenario count. Include dam-safety checks at the higher level.
3. **Severity, duration, and tail measures** of flood exceedance and of irrigation and navigation shortfalls, not only frequencies. Include the worst case for each scheme.
4. **A safety benchmark set by the flood-control authority** (for example, "no more than the current rule's risk, with a stated confidence"), written down before the final comparison.
5. **Feasibility demonstrated**: drawdown time versus forecast lead time, release capacity and downstream limits, refill, and the behavior when a forecast fails. Specify a fallback to a measurement-based rule if forecasts are unavailable or wrong.
6. **A variant search for B** (and for A), to see whether the safety penalty or the irrigation and navigation losses can be reduced: forecast-triggered prerelease, a stage-based or seasonally varying level, a hard ceiling.
7. **Mitigation or compensation** for any party that loses (for example, a dedicated irrigation storage target or a minimum navigation flow commitment), and an agreed way to share the generation gain.
8. **Staged introduction.**
   - If a scheme passes, start with a limited, reversible pilot: a temporary modification of the operating manual, for one or two flood seasons, with predefined triggers to revert.
   - Monitor releases, levels, downstream flows, and the indicators above.
   - Do a post-flood review, and update the rule only after the evidence supports it.

### 2.4 What would confirm or change this recommendation

- **Toward adopting B (or a modified B):**
  - Intervals showing the +0.3 point increase is within noise.
  - The increase disappears when the same B is run with a forecast-triggered drawdown.
  - The additional exceedances are small and short.
  - The flood authority accepts a risk at that level for the water-supply and navigation benefits.
- **Toward adopting A:** the irrigation and navigation losses fall within the intervals or are removed by rule adjustments, and the affected users accept the remaining difference.
- **Against any change:** the intervals are wide enough that no difference can be shown, the improvements disappear under stress tests, or a feasibility problem appears (not enough lead time or release capacity at the higher level).
- **Against B in particular:** the flood-risk increase is robust, or severity and duration are worse than frequency suggests.
- Key assumptions behind my view: the table's numbers are the best available estimates and are on a comparable basis; the current rule's 1.0% is an acceptable benchmark; the generation gains are of real value to the system; and no other constraint (ecological, legal, dam safety) rules out the options.
- Not given, and not assumed: electricity prices and revenue, the city's protection standard, the cost of irrigation or navigation shortfalls, and the consequences of a threshold exceedance. These should be obtained.

## 3. Who should take part and how

### 3.1 Who

| Party | Role |
|---|---|
| Basin commission | Convenes, owns the final decision, and sets the process and the order in which questions are settled. |
| Flood-control authority of the city (and the agency responsible for flood defence and emergency management) | Defines the unsafe outcome, the acceptable-risk benchmark, and the downstream safety limits. It should hold a safety gate: no scheme proceeds to a pilot without its sign-off. |
| Dam-safety regulator and the reservoir operator | Check dam safety at the higher level, the release capacity, the feasibility of drawdown, and what can actually be executed in real time. |
| Hydropower company | Proposer. Provides generation data and operating constraints. Should be an input provider, not the evaluator of its own proposal. |
| Irrigation district | States the storage target and the timing and volume of the supply need, and checks the reliability indicator. |
| Navigation authority | States the minimum flow and reach that matter, and checks the indicator. |
| Hydro-meteorological and forecast agency | Reports forecast skill by lead time, and which actions forecasts can support. |
| Power-system or grid operator | Advises on the value and timing of the generation. |
| Environmental and water-resources agencies | Raise any ecological or legal constraints that are not in the table. |
| Downstream communities and the public | Informed about residual flood risk and given a route to comment. |
| Independent technical reviewers | Review the study (statistics, model, scenario generation) separately from the proposer and from the modelers. |
| Upstream or downstream reservoir operators, if any | Needed if the reservoir is part of a cascade. |

### 3.2 How

1. **Fix the process before the numbers.** The commission convenes the parties and agrees the objectives, indicator definitions, benchmark, and decision rule (including the safety-first rule) before the schemes are ranked. This protects the decision from being steered by the outcome.
2. **Joint fact-finding.** One shared model and dataset, open to all parties. Every party can check the indicators that affect them. The hydropower company should not control the model that evaluates its proposal.
3. **Independent review** of the study design and results. Intervals and robustness tests are done and checked before the commission sees a ranking.
4. **Rounds of consultation.** The parties state their requirements and what loss is tolerable. Disagreement, dissent, and conditions are recorded. The commission decides, and the record shows who agreed to what.
5. **Clear roles.**
   - The flood-control authority and the dam-safety regulator should hold gates on safety.
   - The other agencies advise on trade-offs and conditions.
   - The commission decides among the schemes that pass the gates.
   - Say explicitly who decides, who has a veto, and who only advises.
6. **Staged screening.**
   - First screen on safety and feasibility.
   - Then evaluate the remaining schemes in detail.
   - Then do the consultation, a pilot, and review.
   - Make the final adoption (an update of the operating manual) only after the pilot and review.
7. **After adoption.** Monitor, do a post-flood review after each event, and keep a defined way to revert if a threshold is exceeded or an unexpected outcome appears.

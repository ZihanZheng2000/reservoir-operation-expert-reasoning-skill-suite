# Recommendation: Two-reservoir prerelease decision

## 1. Provisional recommendation

Choose alternative (a), but run it as a staged, moderate prerelease rather than a full drawdown. The two reservoirs play different roles and should not get the same treatment.

- **Upper reservoir: start a moderate prerelease now.** Raise release from 1,500 to about 2,000 m³/s, in steps. The aim is roughly 200 million m³ of free storage (about 65-70%) by the time the flood arrives, about 30 h from now.
- **Lower reservoir: do not raise its release now.** Hold it at 2,500 m³/s, which is already a prerelease. Re-decide at the 12 h forecast, and again later if needed.

This is "(a) in form, (b) in intensity for the lower dam". The reasons for rejecting (b) and (c) are in section 3.

Confidence is moderate. It is conditional on the assumptions in section 2 and on the triggers in section 5.

## 2. Assumptions (not given in the question)

- The "free share" is measured against the flood-limit level. The reservoirs cannot be drawn below it, so each reservoir's maximum extra headroom is its flood-control storage minus its current free volume.
- The lower reservoir's current inflow is not given. I assume about 1,800 m³/s: the upper dam's current release of 1,500 lagged 18 h, plus the tributary at 300.
- The lower dam's release must stay at or below 6,000 m³/s minus the local inflow between the lower dam and the city. That local inflow is unknown and assumed small.
- Current inflow to the upper reservoir (1,200 m³/s) continues until the flood arrives.
- Release changes are allowed at reasonable ramp rates, and a modest water-supply or hydropower loss from prerelease is acceptable. Ramp limits and water-supply value are not provided, so this is my assumption.

## 3. Reasoning

**Free storage.**
- Upper: 40% of 300 = 120 million m³ free, 180 million m³ of possible headroom.
- Lower: 85% of 500 = 425 million m³ free, only 75 million m³ of possible headroom.
- Total free is 545 million m³, against a forecast of 150-400 million m³ plus the tributary's storm volume.

**The upper reservoir cannot hold the flood alone.**
- Its 120 million m³ free is below even the low-end forecast of 150, and well below the central 250 and the high 400.
- It will have to pass roughly 130-280 million m³ downstream during the event.
- Any free space it gains before the flood directly cuts the release it must make while the tributary is also peaking.

**The upper reservoir is already drawing down slowly.**
- Release is 1,500 and inflow is 1,200, so net is -300 m³/s, about 26 million m³ per day.
- Over 30 h that gives about +32 million m³ free, reaching about 152 million m³.
- Raising release to about 2,000 m³/s gives net -800 m³/s. Over 30 h that is about +86 million m³, reaching about 206 million m³ free.
- Reaching the full 300 million m³ free would need release of about 2,900 m³/s for 30 h. That is feasible, but it costs more water and is not justified until the forecast narrows.

**The lower reservoir's extra prerelease is of little value.**
- Release of 2,500 against an assumed inflow of about 1,800 is -700 m³/s, about 60 million m³ per day.
- That uses up its 75 million m³ of headroom in about 30 h anyway.
- Raising the release further only reaches the flood-limit level sooner, which then forces release to be cut back to match inflow. So (b)'s extra benefit is small.
- The lower reservoir has the largest buffer and the shortest lag to the city (10 h). It is therefore the best place to wait for information.
- Its large free volume (425 million m³) can absorb the upper dam's pass-through plus the tributary storm. As a rough check, the upper dam releasing 4,000 plus a tributary at 1,800 gives inflow of 5,800, against a lower release of about 5,000. The excess of about 800 m³/s would take about 150 h to fill 425 million m³, so this is comfortable.

**Timing favours acting on the upper dam first.**
- The earliest flood arrival at the city is about 30 + 18 + 10 = 58 h from now, so the city is not at immediate risk.
- Upper-dam water takes 18 + 10 = 28 h to reach the city, and the upper dam has the least storage and the least lead margin.
- If we wait for the 12 h forecast (option c), only 18 h remain before inflow starts. Reaching the same free storage then needs about 2.5 times the net release rate, about 4,000 m³/s, which arrives at the lower dam at the same time as the storm.
- That is feasible but leaves little margin, because the tributary can add 1,500 and hydrograph timing is unknown.

**Asymmetry of regret.**
- A prerelease can be stopped or reversed within hours. The only loss is some stored water, up to about 90 million m³ more than the status quo for my proposed upper-dam step.
- A shortage of flood storage cannot be reversed once the flood arrives.
- If the low-end forecast of 150 verifies, the moderate step costs little, because the upper reservoir still has room.

**Why not (c).**
- The next forecast is unlikely to remove the central problem, which is that the upper dam's free storage is less than the forecast volume.
- It costs 12 h of cheap drawdown and the cushion of the lead time.
- (c) becomes acceptable only if the water-supply loss is shown to be severe and the tributary forecast is confirmed benign.

**Why not full (a).**
- Pulling the upper reservoir to the full 300 million m³ free, with the lower dam also drawn down, would spend water that is not clearly needed.
- Volume alone is not the problem. The risk is the combination of an unknown peak and the tributary coincidence, so the step should be staged against new information.

## 4. Immediate actions (next hour)

1. **Confirm the actual states.**
   - Current levels and storage in both reservoirs.
   - The lower reservoir's actual inflow, to replace my assumed 1,800 m³/s.
   - Ramp and drawdown limits.
   - The local inflow below the lower dam and the city's current flow.
2. **Upper dam.** Increase release from 1,500 to about 2,000 m³/s in steps, within ramp limits. Recompute free-storage projections each hour.
3. **Lower dam.** Hold at 2,500 m³/s and track the time at which it reaches the flood-limit level. At that point set release equal to inflow, and do not drop below the flood-limit level.
4. **City and emergency services.** Alert them that a flood is possible and that release patterns may change. Current flows are far below 6,000 m³/s, so no action is needed from them yet.
5. **Information requests.**
   - A tributary rainfall-runoff forecast, with its peak, timing and volume.
   - An interim forecast update at about 6 h rather than waiting 12 h.
   - The expected hydrograph shape of the main flood, especially the peak and its timing, not only the 72 h volume.
6. **Set explicit triggers** (section 5) and agree who decides at each, with a record of this decision and its assumptions.

## 5. What would change the recommendation

**Move toward a larger upper prerelease (up to about 2,900-4,000 m³/s) and also a lower prerelease if any of these occur:**
- The forecast central volume rises above about 300 million m³, or the upper end exceeds 400 million m³.
- The tributary forecast shows a peak of 1,000-1,500 m³/s that coincides with the upper dam's pass-through. In that case the projected lower inflow peak exceeds the allowed lower release for more than a few hours.
- A storage test fails: free lower storage at flood time is less than 1.3 times the cumulative excess of (upper release lagged 18 h + tributary - allowed lower release) over the event. The 1.3 cushion is my judgement and should be calibrated by the operators.
- The local inflow below the lower dam is large, so the allowed lower release is well under 6,000 m³/s.

**Move toward stopping or reducing the upper prerelease (to approach option c):**
- The forecast central volume falls to about 150 million m³ or lower, with a benign tributary forecast.
- Water-supply or hydropower constraints prove binding.
- Rain observations show the flood is weaker or later than forecast.

**If the flood arrives earlier than 30 h,** shift quickly to the maximum feasible upper release, and treat the lower reservoir as the buffer.

## 6. Most important missing information and its effect

1. **Tributary forecast and the main-flood hydrograph (peak and timing).** This is the most important gap. With only a volume, I cannot tell whether peaks coincide at the lower dam. It could move the answer toward a stronger upper prerelease or toward waiting.
2. **Lower-reservoir inflow and local downstream inflow.** These set the lower dam's allowed release and its real net drawdown. They could change the lower reservoir's role from buffer to constraint.
3. **Drawdown limits and the cost of lost water.** If flood-limit level is a hard floor, the lower prerelease is nearly moot, as shown above. If supply loss is severe, option (c) with early triggers becomes more attractive.
4. **Forecast skill.** If the 150-400 range is a calibrated interval, central planning at 250 with a trigger at 300+ is reasonable. If it is wider in practice, the upper dam needs more headroom now.

A single choice is supportable on current information only in its staged form. Moderate upper prerelease now is robust across the forecast range. The size of any further prerelease depends on the missing items above.

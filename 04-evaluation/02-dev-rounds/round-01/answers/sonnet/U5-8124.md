# Recommendation for the flood-control headquarters

## Bottom line

**Preferred alternative: a modified (a). Start prereleasing from the upper reservoir now. Do not add extra prerelease at the lower reservoir. Hold the lower reservoir at roughly inflow-matching release, and keep it from drawing below its flood-limited level. Decide on any further change at the 12-hour forecast.**

This is closest to (a), but with the effort concentrated at the upper dam. Alternative (b) is the wrong reservoir. Alternative (c) gives up 12 of the 30 hours of lead time at the one dam that lacks space.

The recommendation is conditional on the assumptions listed below. The most important of them is that no significant local inflow enters between the lower dam and the city.

## Reading the situation (numbers and assumptions)

| Item | Upper | Lower |
|---|---:|---:|
| Free flood storage now | 0.40 x 300 = **120 M m³** | 0.85 x 500 = **425 M m³** |
| Flood storage already occupied | 180 M m³ | 75 M m³ |
| Release vs. inflow | 1,500 vs 1,200: already drawing down about 300 m³/s | 2,500 vs about 1,800 (upper release 1,500 + tributary 300): already drawing down about 700 m³/s |

Assumptions:
- The current releases are discretionary and can be changed. They are not fixed commitments such as supply or power obligations.
- Flood storage is bounded by the flood-limited level. "Free share" cannot exceed 100%.
- The 300 m³/s tributary and the current upper release have been roughly steady.
- No significant local inflow enters between the lower dam and the city. This is not stated in the question.
- The city flow is therefore about the lower dam release, 10 h later. The current margin to the 6,000 m³/s safe flow is about 3,500 m³/s.
- A step change in release is acceptable. Ramping limits and the capacity of the river reach between the dams are not given.

Key arithmetic:
- **Volume.** The central forecast, 250 M m³ in 72 h, is a mean inflow of about 960 m³/s. The range is about 580 to 1,540 m³/s on the same basis. The peak is unknown and probably well above these means.
- **Whole system.** Free space is 120 + 425 = 545 M m³. That exceeds even the high case of 400 M m³. Volume alone is therefore not a city-level problem, provided the water is shared sensibly between the two reservoirs.
- **Upper reservoir alone.** It is the scarce one. 120 M m³ of free space against 250 M m³ expected is a shortfall of about 130 M m³. Against the 400 M m³ high case the shortfall is about 280 M m³.
- **Lower reservoir headroom.** It can add at most 75 M m³ of extra space, because it is already 85% free.
- **Upper drawdown at present rates.** Continuing at 1,500 m³/s for 30 h gives about +32 M m³, so about 152 M m³ free at flood arrival. That is still below the central case.

## Why this choice

1. **The upper dam is the time-critical, space-short reservoir, and acting early there costs little.**
   - Raising its release to about 2,500 m³/s for 30 h, with inflow still about 1,200 m³/s, draws it down about 1,300 m³/s net. That is about 140 M m³, giving about 260 M m³ free at arrival. That matches the central forecast of 250 M m³.
   - The extra water takes 18 h to reach the lower dam. The lower dam has 425 M m³ of space to catch it, and the city has about 3,500 m³/s of margin.
   - The release is therefore very unlikely to create a downstream hazard, unless a tributary storm arrives together with it (see the thresholds below).
2. **Extra prerelease at the lower dam has little to add.**
   - It is already 85% free and already drawing down at about 700 m³/s. Left alone, it reaches 100% free in about 30 h.
   - The upper release raised now arrives at the lower dam in 18 h and replaces the lower dam's current drawdown.
   - Alternative (b) buys at most 75 M m³ of space. That is well under the roughly 130 M m³ shortfall at the upper dam, and it leaves the actual shortfall untouched.
   - The lower dam can also create space quickly later. Its 8,000 m³/s capacity, limited to about 6,000 m³/s by the city, is large relative to the time available. Its 10 h travel time to the city is short, but less pressing than the 18 h upper-to-lower lag.
3. **Waiting 12 h (c) costs the most where time matters most.**
   - Waiting uses 40% of the 30 h lead time at the dam that needs about 100 M m³ more drawdown. Catching up later would need about 1,500 m³/s extra instead of about 900 m³/s extra, and the sudden extra release would meet the tributary storm and the arriving flood together.
   - The forecast is only updated every 12 h, and the flood arrival time has its own uncertainty. The decision cycle is also shorter than the 18 h upper-to-lower travel time, so there are two chances to adjust before today's upper release reaches the lower dam.
   - The cost of acting is asymmetric. If the flood is smaller than expected, the cost is some released water. Upper release above need is at most about 110 M m³ in the low case of 150 M m³. It can be cut back at the next update, and that loss is bounded and mostly recoverable if refill opportunity remains (assumed, not known). If the flood is larger, or earlier, or arrives with a tributary storm, lost lead time cannot be recovered.
4. **Reasons why the city is relatively protected under the current information:** the lower dam alone has 425 M m³ free and a 3,500 m³/s margin to the city's safe flow. The residual risk sits mainly in three places: (i) exhaustion of upper-dam space, (ii) a tributary storm coinciding with the flood, and (iii) a late or compressed peak.

## Immediate actions (next hour)

1. **Upper dam.** Raise release from 1,500 to about 2,000 m³/s now, and to about 2,500 m³/s over the next several hours, respecting ramping and the capacity of the reach to the lower dam (to be confirmed with the operators). The target is about 250 to 260 M m³ free at flood arrival (30 h). Recompute the target release immediately if inflow rises above 1,200 m³/s.
2. **Lower dam.**
   - Keep releasing about 2,500 m³/s. Match release to inflow once flood storage reaches 100% free, and do not draw below the flood-limited level.
   - As the upper release arrives (about +18 h), raise lower release to roughly match inflow, about 2,800 m³/s, so there is no net refill into flood space.
   - Keep the city flow well below 6,000 m³/s. At present it is about 2,500 m³/s.
3. **Information requests, to be answered before the 12 h forecast if possible.**
   - Tributary rainfall and flow forecast between the dams, and any local inflow below the lower dam.
   - Peak flow and timing, not only the 72 h volume.
   - The reliability of the 30 h onset time.
   - Release-change limits, reach capacity, and any minimum or fixed releases at both dams.
4. **Log and agree the triggers below** with headquarters, so the 12 h decision is a check against thresholds and not a fresh debate.

## What would change the recommendation

Check these at the 12 h update, or earlier if new information arrives.

| New information | Change |
|---|---|
| Forecast central volume at or below about 200 M m³ and upper bound at or below about 300 M m³ (with timing unchanged) | Stop raising the upper release. Hold the achieved free space, or fall back toward inflow-matching release. The reduced risk makes more drawdown unnecessary. |
| Forecast central volume at or above about 300 M m³, or upper bound at or above about 450 M m³, or arrival earlier than about 20 h | Escalate: raise the upper release to about 3,500 to 4,000 m³/s, as far as the reach and the city margin allow. Also draw the lower reservoir to 100% free, which adds at most 75 M m³ of space. This is where the system volume (545 M m³ free) becomes tight and (a) in full is justified. |
| Tributary forecast shows a storm approaching peak (toward 1,800 m³/s) at the same time as upper releases would arrive | Reduce or stagger the upper release timing. Use the lower reservoir's space first for the tributary peak. Check that the lower release can stay below about 5,400 m³/s (6,000 less a 10% margin, an assumed margin) with expected lower inflow. Rule of thumb: upper release + tributary peak should not exceed what the lower dam can absorb and still hold city flow within the safe flow. |
| Significant local inflow below the lower dam is found | The 3,500 m³/s margin shrinks. The lower dam's own prerelease then gains value, and a lower-reservoir drawdown (b) becomes worth reconsidering. |
| Upper-dam safety limits or release restrictions make the 2,500 m³/s release infeasible | Compensate with a larger share at the lower dam and an earlier upper-dam flood-control release. Escalate to the decision authority. |
| Next forecast shows the flood continuing past 72 h, or a second event | Treat 72 h volumes as lower bounds. Keep more space; do not refill. |

## Is the information enough for a single choice?

It is enough for a preferred action now, but not for a single, final answer on the size of the prerelease. The most important missing items, in order:

1. **Tributary storm forecast and local inflow below the lower dam.** These determine whether the 3,500 m³/s margin to the city is real and whether upper releases can pass downstream. They matter more than the volume range. If the tributary is likely to add the full 1,500 m³/s during the same storm, upper release and the tributary peak coincide in the lower dam's inflow.
2. **Peak size and timing.** Only a 72 h volume is given. A peaky hydrograph needs more upper-dam space at arrival than the mean rate suggests.
3. **Reliability of the 30 h arrival time.** The upper-dam prerelease target depends on it. A 12 h error moves the achievable drawdown by about 47 M m³ at 1,300 m³/s net.
4. **Operating constraints.** Ramping and reach limits, minimum releases, and the upper-dam safety level. A hard limit here could make the recommended release infeasible.

The recommended action is robust to the first three because it is cheap, reversible and early, and it can be reviewed at 12 h. The thresholds above are my provisional values, derived from the arithmetic here, not site-calibrated limits.

# Advice to the Basin Commission: raising the flood-season operating level

## Summary

My provisional advice is to keep the current rule for now and not adopt any scheme on the strength of this table. Take Scheme B forward as the one real candidate, but only as a redesigned and conditional option. Do not take Scheme C forward. Keep Scheme A only as a fallback.

The reasons are as follows.

- Flood risk should be handled as a constraint set by those who bear it, not as one more item to trade off.
- The table has no uncertainty intervals. The differences that matter most are small relative to what 40 years of data can resolve.
- B is the only scheme that improves three indicators. It is also the only one that worsens flood risk, and that is the indicator the commission is least able to trade away.

## Assumptions

- The "safe threshold" for city flow is a defined, agreed channel-capacity or damage-onset flow.
- "Raising the flood-season operating level" means a higher flood-season limit or target level. That reduces the empty flood-control storage available at the start of a flood.
- The four schemes differ only in operating rules. They do not differ in infrastructure, and they use the same inflow ensemble.
- All indicators come from the same simulation framework. The 40-year record is the basis for the models, and there is a synthetic or stochastic extension for scenarios.
- I do not know how the schemes are defined in detail, so I cannot explain why some results look odd. For example, A raises the level yet lowers irrigation reliability, while B raises generation and also improves irrigation and navigation. Possible reasons include different level schedules, different release rules, different transition dates, or model artefacts. This needs to be understood before the numbers are trusted.

## 1. How to compare the schemes and handle trade-offs

### 1.1 Make the evidence comparable and honest first

**Quantify uncertainty before any ranking.**
- Flood probabilities of 0.9%, 1.0% and 1.3% per year correspond to roughly 0.36, 0.40 and 0.52 expected exceedance years in 40 years. A 40-year record cannot discriminate between them empirically. The numbers come mostly from model structure and the synthetic extension, so the model and extension assumptions dominate them.
- Produce intervals for each indicator, and especially for the differences between each scheme and the current rule, using paired comparison on identical inflow sequences. Ranking absolute values is much weaker than ranking paired differences.
- Interval sources to cover:
  - sampling uncertainty (bootstrap or block bootstrap of years, and ensembles of stochastic inflows);
  - parameter uncertainty in the flood-frequency fit, including tail shape;
  - model-structure uncertainty (routing, forecast error, operator behaviour);
  - non-stationarity and climate sensitivity (wetter or flashier flood regimes, shifted seasonality);
  - changes in basin conditions such as land use and upstream regulation.
- The question for the commission is whether B's +0.3 percentage points of flood probability is distinguishable from zero. It is also whether the upper plausible bound is acceptable. The point estimate alone does not answer this.

**Check the indicators are the right ones and are fair to all schemes.**
- Flood probability to a single threshold hides severity. Add exceedance magnitude and duration, peak flow, number of affected people or assets, and the conditional loss given exceedance. Include rare extreme events (for example very large design floods) because a higher flood-season level erodes flood-control storage most when a large event arrives early or late in the season.
- Report dam-safety metrics separately. Examples are peak reservoir level, spillway use, and the margin to the surcharge or design level under the design and check floods. A higher flood-season level eats into this margin.
- Irrigation reliability needs a definition: the threshold storage, and the timing of the end-of-season date. Also check whether it is conditional on the water year. Add the depth of shortfall and its seasonal timing.
- Add dry-year and drought performance. The 40-year mean can hide a scheme that fails badly in rare dry years.
- Navigation needs days and also the time of year, because insufficient days in a critical period differ from the same number elsewhere.
- Generation should be converted to value, using prices that vary by season and an agreed firm versus non-firm treatment. Do not use the mean alone. The 0.2 to 0.5 billion kWh gain should be translated into revenue and net benefit only after the price assumptions are agreed. I do not know the price, so I will not estimate it.
- Check robustness to forecasts. If any scheme depends on inflow forecasts, test skill degradation. A scheme that works only with perfect forecasts is not a real option.

**Check the model against reality.**
- Validate the model reproducing historical floods and operations, particularly the largest events. Test whether simulated operator behaviour matches real behaviour. Check the sensitivity of rankings to the model structure.

### 1.2 Structure the comparison

**Step 1: gate on hard constraints.** A scheme is screened out if it violates any of these, regardless of benefits:
- legal and regulatory flood-control and dam-safety requirements (these are not negotiable by this commission);
- a flood-risk tolerance for the city. The city's flood department and the emergency authority should set this tolerance before they see how the schemes rank. It might say "no increase in annual exceedance probability, or in the 1-in-100 or similar event consequence, beyond the stated uncertainty", or it might set an absolute ceiling;
- minimum environmental or ecological flows and minimum navigation or irrigation floors, if those exist in law or agreements.

**Step 2: show a dominance check.** With the table as given:
- Relative to the current rule, A gains generation (+0.3), is equal on flood, and is worse on irrigation (-2 points) and navigation (+2 days). It is a pure transfer from irrigation and navigation to hydropower, with no flood gain.
- C gains a little on flood (-0.1 point, not distinguishable from noise as presented) and on generation (+0.2). It loses substantially on irrigation (-7 points) and navigation (+6 days). It is not an improvement overall and it hurts two stakeholders. It is also unclear why it is lower on flood risk and higher on generation but worse on water supply, so the design needs explaining. It should not be taken forward.
- B improves generation (+0.5), irrigation (+3 points) and navigation (-3 days). It worsens flood probability by 0.3 points, a 30% relative increase from a low base.
- No scheme dominates the current rule across all four indicators. B comes closest, and its single cost sits on the indicator with the most severe consequences and the most uncertainty.

**Step 3: treat the remaining trade-offs transparently.** I would use a layered approach, not a single weighted score.
- Present a performance table with intervals, a distribution (not only the mean) for each indicator, and the paired differences from the current rule.
- Use a multi-criteria method to explore only after constraints are met. Elicit weights from stakeholders openly, and test how the ranking changes across weight sets (sensitivity or "what weights would flip the decision"). Do not let the hydropower company, or any one party, set the weights.
- Prefer a monetary common unit only where defensible (generation revenue, expected flood damage, irrigation shortfall losses). Do not force all objectives into money. Navigation and flood-safety values in particular involve non-market values and equity.
- Treat flood risk asymmetrically. Expected annual damage is not enough, because low-probability high-consequence outcomes carry public-safety and trust costs. Consider risk-averse measures such as conditional value at risk, or worst-case behaviour across the model ensemble.
- Look at who gains and who loses. Generation gains accrue to the company and the grid, flood risk falls on city residents, and irrigation and navigation effects fall on farmers and shippers. If the winners cannot or will not compensate the losers, a scheme with a positive net benefit may still be unacceptable.

**Step 4: look for better designs, not only the four given.** The table suggests the real question is whether a higher level can be had without the flood cost. Options to test include:
- forecast-informed or conditional operation, so that the higher level is held only when inflow forecasts are low-risk and is drawn down in advance of forecast flood events;
- a staged level that rises later in the season, or only after the main flood period for the basin;
- a hybrid of B's irrigation and navigation benefits with A's flood neutrality. I do not know whether this is feasible, so it should be tested and not assumed;
- better downstream protection measures that raise the safe threshold or reduce consequences, such as warning, evacuation planning and floodplain measures. These would change the risk side of the trade-off, but they are a separate decision and cost.

## 2. Recommendation and conditions

### 2.1 Provisional recommendation

1. **Keep the current rule at present.** The table does not justify a change. The evidence on the key indicator (flood risk) is not decision-grade, and every alternative either worsens or leaves unchanged the flood indicator while worsening other indicators (A, C) or worsens flood risk (B).
2. **Take B forward as the candidate, but as a conditional or redesigned option, not as tabled.** The reasons are:
   - it is the only scheme that improves three of four indicators, including the two non-hydropower objectives that the irrigation district and navigation users care about, so it is the only one with a plausible path to broad acceptance;
   - its single cost is the flood-probability increase, which may be partly a matter of uncertainty and which may be mitigated by design (forecast-based drawdown, timing).
3. **Do not take C forward.** It worsens two objectives substantially for a flood benefit that cannot be distinguished from noise.
4. **Hold A as a fallback only.** It transfers benefits from irrigation and navigation to hydropower with no flood gain. It could be reconsidered only if compensation arrangements make the irrigation and navigation losses acceptable and if B fails the flood test.

### 2.2 What would confirm or change this

- **B becomes adoptable if:**
  - the intervals for B's flood-probability difference, and for consequence metrics, fall within the city's pre-agreed tolerance across the plausible model and climate range. The test is on the upper bound and on the tail, not the mean;
  - dam safety is confirmed as unaffected under design and check floods;
  - the reasons for B's irrigation and navigation gains are understood and robust (for example, they are not an artefact of the inflow ensemble or of an unrealistically good forecast assumption);
  - the net revenue gain is large enough, after agreed prices, to justify the added flood risk and operational burden.
- **B should be dropped or redesigned if** the flood increase is statistically real and above the tolerance, if it grows under wetter or non-stationary scenarios, or if the benefits are fragile. In that case the commission should keep the current rule, or ask for conditional or forecast-informed variants that hold the flood probability at or below its current value.
- **The recommendation flips toward keeping the current rule permanently** if no redesign meets the flood tolerance. It flips toward adopting B if the uncertainty analysis shows B's flood risk is indistinguishable from the current rule and within tolerance, since B is then better on the other three indicators.
- If the city's tolerance is "no increase at all", B fails as tabled. Then the realistic options are the current rule, or a redesigned B that holds the flood risk constant.

### 2.3 Conditions for any adoption

- **A written decision framework agreed before results are final:** the flood-risk tolerance, the indicators, the thresholds that would trigger rejection, and how disagreements are resolved.
- **Independent technical review** of the model, the uncertainty analysis and the scheme definitions, by a body not paid by the proposing party.
- **Phased or trial implementation, not permanent change.** For example, start with a conservative version (a smaller raise, or conditional on forecasts) for a fixed trial period, with monitoring.
- **Operational safeguards:** an automatic reversion to the current rule when forecasts or observed conditions exceed set triggers (for example, forecast heavy rain, wet antecedent conditions, high inflow), and clear authority for the flood department to override.
- **Monitoring and review:** track the actual indicators against predictions, review after a set period or after any significant event, and have a pre-agreed rule for stopping or reverting.
- **Compensation or benefit-sharing** if any party is made worse off (for example a share of added generation revenue to irrigation districts or flood-protection measures). The terms need to be agreed up front. I would not assume this is acceptable to all parties.
- **Dam safety and legal compliance review** by the dam-safety regulator, and regulatory approval for changing the flood limit level where this is legally required.
- **Transparency:** publish the analysis, the uncertainties and the decision rationale to the public, and have a communication plan for city residents about flood risk.
- **Re-assessment trigger** if hydrological conditions or climate assessment change materially.

## 3. Who should take part and how

I would structure participation by role, not give every party equal say on every question.

| Participant | Role in the decision | How they take part |
|---|---|---|
| Basin commission | Decision-maker | Sets the decision process, approves the framework, makes the final decision, and owns the accountability. |
| City flood-control department (and emergency management) | Holder of the flood-risk constraint, with veto-like standing on the flood tolerance | Defines the tolerance and consequence metrics before schemes are ranked, reviews flood results, and must be satisfied on flood safety. Has override authority in operations. |
| Dam-safety and flood-control regulator (national or provincial water authority) | Legal compliance and safety | Reviews dam-safety margins and design-flood performance, and approves any change to the flood limit level. This approval is not optional where legally required. |
| Hydropower company (proposer) | Applicant, a key stakeholder but not the judge | Provides generation and price data and operational constraints. It may propose variants but should not run or control the evaluation. It would carry operational duties and possibly cost-sharing. |
| Irrigation district and farmers' representatives | Affected party | Define the reliability metric and critical dates, review the irrigation results, take part in the weighting and compensation discussions, and take part in monitoring. |
| Navigation authority and shipping users | Affected party | Define the navigation metric and critical periods, review the results, and take part in the trade-off and compensation discussions. |
| Energy or grid regulator or system operator | Consulted | Advises on the value of the extra generation (firm or non-firm), and on any market or regulatory constraints. |
| Environmental and water-resources agency (ecological flows, water quality, fisheries) | Consulted, and may have legal standing | Checks environmental flow requirements and effects on ecosystems that are not in the table. This is a gap in the indicators. |
| Meteorological and hydrological forecasting service | Technical | Provides forecast skill information and supports any forecast-based operation, including the triggers. |
| Independent technical reviewers (academic or third-party engineers, ideally including a flood-frequency and uncertainty specialist) | Verification | Review the models and the uncertainty analysis, and test the findings. Do not take a position on the outcome. |
| City residents, floodplain communities and other downstream or reservoir-fringe communities | Affected public | Consulted through public information sessions, comment and representatives. Flood risk is borne by them, so they should see the risk information in plain terms. |
| Water-supply users (municipal or industrial, if any) | Consulted, since the question does not say whether they exist | Check if the schemes affect them. I am noting this as a gap and not asserting that they exist. |

How the process should run:
1. **Agree the framework first.** Stakeholders and the commission agree the objectives, indicators, flood tolerance, and decision rules before the schemes are ranked, so the criteria are not tuned after the results are known.
2. **Joint fact-finding.** Technical staff from all parties, and the independent reviewers, share data and models and agree the uncertainty analysis. Disputes over the facts should be settled by evidence, and disputes over values by the stakeholders and the commission.
3. **Separate evidence from value judgments.** Technical teams report evidence and uncertainty. Stakeholders express their priorities. The commission makes the final value judgment, and it records its reasons.
4. **Make roles clear.** The flood-risk tolerance and dam safety are constraints that the flood authority and the regulator define, and the commission should not trade them off against revenue. Other trade-offs among hydropower, irrigation and navigation are negotiated, with compensation where needed.
5. **Manage the conflict of interest.** The hydropower company benefits directly, so the analysis should be reviewed independently, and the company should not decide on the flood tolerance.
6. **Ongoing involvement.** The same group should take part in the monitoring and the review of any trial, and in decisions about reverting.

## Key open items (what I would ask for next)

1. Intervals for all indicators and for the paired differences from the current rule, with the method and ensemble used.
2. Precise definitions of each scheme (levels, dates, release rules, forecast use) and an explanation of the odd patterns in A and C.
3. Consequence metrics for flooding (magnitude, duration, damages) and rare-flood and dam-safety performance.
4. The definition of the irrigation and navigation indicators, including timing and dry-year behaviour.
5. Revenue values for the generation differences, with agreed price assumptions.
6. A climate and non-stationarity sensitivity analysis.
7. The city's flood-risk tolerance, set before the results are ranked.
8. Tests of conditional and forecast-informed variants of B.

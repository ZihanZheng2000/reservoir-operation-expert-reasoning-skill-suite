# Study design: staged (seasonal) flood-limited water levels versus a single fixed flood-limited level

## 1. Summary of the design and my provisional judgment

**Provisional judgment.** Staged levels are plausible for a monsoon reservoir, because large floods cluster in the core months and the early and late parts of the flood season carry a lower flood load. The likely gain is a higher level in the pre-monsoon and especially the late-monsoon stage. That should improve end-of-season refill, which matters for irrigation and hydropower. Whether it can be done safely at this site cannot be known from the data list alone. Three things decide it:

1. How large the downstream safe discharge at the town is relative to the outlet capacity, including local inflow between the dam and the town. This is the biggest unknown.
2. Whether drawdown at stage transitions can finish inside the available lead time, using the level–discharge capacity.
3. Whether late-season or early-season extreme floods exist in the 45-year record and in the regional flood regime.

I would therefore plan the study so that a negative result ("no safe gain at this site") is a valid outcome. I would not design it to prove staging works.

**Core structure.**
- The main question is answered with a **no-forecast staged rule** first: fixed dates and fixed levels per stage, evaluated against the current rule on the same inflows.
- Forecast use is a **second, separate layer** (see section 6). Eight years of 1–5 day forecasts is too short to carry a safety claim on its own.
- Safety is a **non-compensable constraint**. The staged rule must be no riskier than the current rule on every defined safety measure. Only then do I compare benefits.

**Assumptions I am making, which the student must confirm.**
- "Fixed flood-limited level" means one level held through the whole flood season, with a flood pool above it for flood regulation.
- The current rule includes gate or outlet operating logic for floods above the flood-limited level. This is the comparator and must be encoded exactly.
- Daily inflow is the only inflow resolution. This is a limitation for a monsoon basin (see section 3).
- The town's safe discharge or stage is known to the operating authority. It is not on the confirmed data list.

## 2. Research question and scope

**Main question.** At this reservoir, can a staged flood-limited level, with higher levels in the stages of the flood season when flood load is lower, raise water-use performance (refill reliability, storage at key dates, hydropower, irrigation supply, less spill of usable water) while keeping reservoir-side and downstream flood risk no higher than under the current single fixed level, including at stage transitions?

**Sub-questions.**
1. Is the flood regime seasonally differentiated enough to justify stages? That means different flood magnitude, volume, and timing by sub-period, and how variable the monsoon onset and withdrawal are from year to year.
2. For each stage, what is the highest level that is feasible (time to draw down, space to absorb the design flood, downstream limits) and that stays within an acceptable-risk benchmark?
3. What do the staged levels gain and lose relative to the current rule, across the full historical record and across larger synthetic floods?
4. Is the result robust to uncertainty in stage dates, flood magnitude, daily-to-peak conversion, rating and capacity, and non-stationarity?
5. (Extension) Do the 1–5 day forecasts add value on top of staging, for example by allowing a stage-level rise only when no significant flood is forecast?

**Out of scope unless the user adds it:** sediment, water quality, structural redesign, and cascade coordination. The question names a single reservoir.

## 3. Data audit and what to request

**Available.**
- 45 years of daily inflow.
- Level–storage and level–discharge-capacity curves.
- 8 years of archived forecasts with concurrent observations, at 1–5 day leads and updated daily.
- The current operating rule.

**Issues in the available data that I would settle first.**
- **Daily resolution understates flood peaks** in a monsoon basin with a flashy response. A 40 km reach likely has a travel time of hours, not days. Flood peak and the shape of the rising limb drive both the reservoir level and the downstream peak. I would:
  - ask for any sub-daily records, even for a subset of years, to fit a peak-to-daily-mean ratio by flood size and season;
  - if none exist, state the limitation and bracket it by scaling peaks over a plausible range as a sensitivity test.
- **Inflow definition.** Is it observed inflow or reservoir-reconstructed inflow (from level change plus release)? Is it naturalized for upstream abstraction or storage? Reconstructed inflow is smoothed by reservoir routing and misses peaks. I would check the 45-year series for changes in station, method, or upstream regulation (homogeneity test).
- **Forecast archive:** confirm what is forecast (inflow to the reservoir, or rainfall), whether it is a deterministic point forecast or an ensemble, and what the observations are. An 8-year record has only a handful of large events in the months where it matters. It is a short sample for error structure.
- **Alignment.** Put inflow, forecast, and observations on one time base, and check that issue time and valid time are handled correctly.

**Data not yet confirmed that I would ask for, in priority order, with a fallback if refused.**

| Data | Why it matters | Fallback if unavailable |
|---|---|---|
| Downstream safe discharge or stage at the town, and channel rating | Defines the downstream safety threshold and the maximum allowed release | Treat the threshold as an unknown parameter and report results as a function of it; do not assume a value |
| Local inflow and gauged flows between the dam and the town (and flood travel time) | The town sees reservoir release plus uncontrolled local inflow; a release that is safe locally may be unsafe at the town | Use a simple routing with a range of local-inflow fractions as sensitivity; flag as unverified |
| Historical operation records (levels, releases, gate use) | Validate the simulator and the encoded current rule against what really happened | Validate only on mass balance and the level–storage curve; state that operator discretion is unrepresented |
| Design floods and dam safety levels (design and check flood levels, spillway rule) | The upper-level feasibility check against the extreme flood | Derive stage-wise floods by frequency analysis and report the check as provisional |
| Irrigation demand schedule and hydropower characteristics (turbine capacity, head–efficiency, minimum flows) | Needed to convert storage into benefit | Use storage at key dates, spilled volume, and refill probability as proxies, and do not claim hydropower or irrigation gains in energy or area units |
| Regulations and approval authority for changing the rule | Implementability | List as a deliverable of the adoption discussion, not a modelling input |
| Sub-daily inflow or rainfall | Peak accuracy | See above |

If none of the "not yet confirmed" data can be obtained, the study is still feasible as a **screening study**. In that case the downstream threshold has to be handled parametrically, and the conclusion can only be a conditional recommendation.

## 4. Methods, in order

### Step 1. Baseline: encode and validate the current rule
- Write the current rule as an executable simulator on a daily (or finer, if available) step: water balance `S(t+1) = S(t) + inflow − release − losses`, level from the storage curve, release limited by the level–discharge-capacity curve, and the gate or outlet logic of the current rule above the flood-limited level.
- Add a simple routing to the town (travel time, attenuation, local inflow), with the town's safe discharge as a parameter or a given value.
- Check the simulator against history where possible: reproduce recorded levels and releases for past floods. If operation records are missing, say so, and note that the simulator then represents the rule as written and not operator practice.
- Run the current rule over all 45 years. Record, per year and per event, the maximum level, the peak release, the peak at the town, the flood pool used, the spill volume, the end-of-season storage, and the first date the pool returns to the flood-limited level. This is the baseline for every comparison.
- Diagnose the baseline weaknesses the study is meant to address: how often the fixed level holds water below what was needed (wasted water, failed refill), and whether any years under-protect. This tells whether the question is worth the effort and which stage is most promising.

### Step 2. Flood regime by sub-period (stage definition)
- For each calendar window across the flood season (for example 2–4 week windows), extract annual maxima of peak inflow and of 1-, 3-, and 5-day volumes. Look for a seasonal shape in magnitude and frequency.
- Fit frequency distributions per candidate stage with uncertainty bounds. With only 45 years, tail estimates (100-year and beyond) are very uncertain. Use stated confidence intervals, and use regional information or the official design flood if available, rather than reading the tail off the record.
- Study the variability of monsoon onset and withdrawal and of the date of the first and last large flood. This is a main risk of a fixed-date stage plan: if the core season arrives early or leaves late, a raised level may sit in place during a large flood.
- Test non-stationarity (trend or shift in annual maxima and in seasonal timing). If found, the stage design should be tested under both the stationary and the shifted regime.
- Candidate stage structures to compare: 2 stages (core and non-core), and 3 stages (early, core, late). Choose boundaries from the flood data, not just from calendar convention, but keep the number of free parameters small given the short record.

### Step 3. Feasible upper levels per stage (compute the ceiling, then search below it)
For each stage, derive an upper bound on the flood-limited level from physical and risk limits, before any optimization:
- **Time:** the level above the lowest stage level can be restored by the next-stage level within the lead time, at the release the downstream limit allows. Use the level–discharge-capacity curve and the safe discharge at the town. Use the lead time actually reliable at the reservoir (see Step 5 on forecast timing). With no forecast, use the natural warning time: how fast do large events build up in the inflow record?
- **Space:** the remaining flood pool at that stage level can absorb the stage's design flood (from Step 2), together with allowed releases, without exceeding the dam safety level.
- **Downstream:** releases needed to hold the level never push the town above its safe stage, including local inflow.
- **Transitions:** the rise at the start of a higher stage needs inflow to fill, and the fall at the start of a lower stage needs drawdown time. A stage plan can be locally feasible and infeasible across the boundary. Check each transition explicitly, with early/late onset shifts.
- Convert a given acceptable risk into a level ceiling or a required reserve for each stage where the risk is probabilistic.

Then, inside these ceilings, search the small parameter set (stage boundary dates and levels per stage, perhaps 4–7 parameters). Because the parameter space is small, a grid or simple evolutionary search is enough. Use full year-by-year simulation, not a surrogate. Keep the current rule's flood-limited level as the lower limit of each stage, so no stage is lower than today's level; that makes the staged rule a bounded departure from the current rule and easier to defend. Keep the current rule's gate logic above the flood-limited level unchanged. Otherwise the study would be confounding two changes.

### Step 4. Risk and benefit assessment (see section 5 for the detailed measures)
Evaluate each candidate and the baseline on the same inflows, same initial conditions, same constraints.

### Step 5. Forecast characterization (needed for the extension and for timing claims)
- Quantify forecast error by lead time (1–5 days), by flow class (especially high flows), and by month, using the 8 years of pairs. Report bias, relative error, a hit and false-alarm record for flood-sized events, and the timing error of the rise.
- Judge what actions each lead time supports. One to two days may support a final level adjustment and release scheduling. Three to five days may support pre-release. State whether the lead time is long enough for the drawdown a stage transition or a flood warning requires; if not, forecasts cannot underwrite a higher level.
- Operational value is not forecast skill. Do not assume a skilful forecast improves operation; measure it (section 6).

## 5. Assessing the effect on flood safety and on the other objectives

### 5.1 Safety measures (non-compensable)
Define unsafe outcomes in advance, with the operating authority if possible:
- **Reservoir side:** probability that the maximum level exceeds the safety threshold (the flood-control high level or the design level), and the required flood pool relative to the available pool.
- **Downstream side:** probability that the release or the routed flow at the town exceeds the safe discharge; the peak flow at the town; duration above the safe discharge.
- **Beyond a single exceedance probability:** because exceeding by a little and by a lot are different, add the magnitude of exceedance and its duration, and, if a loss function exists, a tail measure. If there is no damage information, use exceedance magnitude and duration as the severity proxies and say that they are proxies.
- **Risk over a whole event, not at one instant,** and in particular for events that straddle stage boundaries and for multi-peak events.
- **Benchmark for acceptable risk:** the current rule's risk under the same conditions, or the protection standard of the town if one exists. I would adopt "no worse than the current rule" as the base criterion, because a defensible absolute standard is probably not available, and the current rule's risk should be computed, not assumed.

### 5.2 Events to test on
1. **Historical replay** of all 45 years, event by event, under baseline and under each staged candidate. Not only annual totals.
2. **Stage-specific design floods and scaled floods.** Scale historical hydrographs to larger magnitudes within each stage (for example to return periods up to the design standard) with fixed shape, and also with alternative shapes (early peak, multi-peak, back-to-back floods). Use the frequency analysis from Step 2 with its uncertainty to choose the magnitudes.
3. **Stochastic ensembles** of inflow sequences preserving the seasonal pattern and the within-event hydrograph variety, to estimate risk with more events than the 45-year record offers. The generator itself must be checked (statistics, seasonal timing, dependence between successive floods, extremes) before it is used.
4. **Initial-state variations:** start each stage at its prescribed level and also at the level the previous stage left, including a late drawdown, because the state at the transition determines risk.
5. **Stage-boundary stress:** shift the onset and the withdrawal by ±1–3 weeks relative to the fixed dates to simulate years in which the monsoon is early or late.
6. **Worst case at the transition:** a large flood immediately after the level has been raised.

### 5.3 Other operating objectives
- **Water-use measures:** probability of refilling to the target level by the end of the flood season; storage on key dates (end of season, start of the irrigation season); mean annual spilled volume and the share of inflow spilled (floodwater utilization); in dry and normal years, not only on average.
- **Irrigation:** shortfall or reliability against the demand schedule if it is obtained; otherwise storage on key dates as proxy.
- **Hydropower:** generation from the head–discharge relation if the plant data are obtained; otherwise mean level or storage as a head proxy. Do not report generation numbers without plant data.
- **Show the distribution of gain** across years: median, quartiles, and the worst years, not just the mean. A gain concentrated in a few years, or a loss in the wettest years, matters.
- **Trade-off display:** keep safety and benefit measures separate and show the non-inferior candidates (the set of candidates not beaten on every measure). I would not collapse them into a weighted score. Weights would hide the safety constraint. If a final choice is needed, apply the safety constraint first and rank only the safe candidates on benefits, and test the ranking against changes in weights.

### 5.4 Attribution
For every claimed gain, show the mechanism: which stage, which years, how much of the gain is the extra stored volume that would otherwise be spilled or released before refill, and what the extra risk was in the events that tested it. If a gain comes mainly from a few dry years with no flood in the relevant stage, say so.

## 6. Role of the forecasts (extension, kept separate)
- Compare three information conditions on the same inflows, using the same staged rule: no forecast, archived forecast, and perfect foresight over the lead time. The perfect case bounds the possible gain. It is a reference, not an attainable rule.
- Candidate use: the staged level is raised, or kept raised, only if the forecast shows no flood-sized inflow within the lead time; or a drawdown to the stage level is triggered by a forecast.
- Because the error structure is estimated from 8 years, use conservative quantiles of the forecast, and check sensitivity to the error level, for example by inflating the errors by a range of factors. Also test a rule that falls back to measurement-based triggers (current level and inflow) when the forecast is missing or looks unreliable.
- Where the forecast archive does not cover a historical flood, I would generate synthetic forecasts with the fitted error model for the 45 years, check that the generated errors preserve the observed error statistics and the dependence across lead times and days, and say that the synthetic forecasts depend on that error model.
- A forecast-informed rule needs separate evidence. A positive no-forecast staged result should not be reported as forecast-informed, and vice versa.

## 7. Robustness and testing
- **Baseline identity:** every comparison under identical inflows, initial states, constraints, and simulator.
- **Out-of-sample:** select stage dates and levels on part of the record and test on held-out years (leave-one-year-out, or earlier years for selection and later years for testing). The risk of overfitting a few parameters to 45 years is real, and the extreme years matter most.
- **Sensitivity:** to stage dates, the downstream safe discharge (a sweep), local inflow, the peak-to-daily ratio, rating and storage-curve error, release capacity, and the forecast error level.
- **Non-stationarity test:** repeat the safety evaluation with a heavier-tail or shifted flood series.
- **Method check:** check the risk estimates by a second method (for example the stochastic ensemble versus the historical replay) and report where they disagree.
- **Uncertainty on the results:** bootstrap over years for the benefit measures and report confidence intervals on risk estimates. Zero exceedances in a finite sample is not zero risk. Report the sample size and an upper bound.
- **Transfer:** say that the result applies to this reservoir and this regime. Do not generalize to other Indian reservoirs from one case.

## 8. What results would be sufficient to recommend a change

I would fix these criteria before running the final comparison and have them agreed with the operating authority where possible. The numerical thresholds below are placeholders to agree on, not values I know.

A recommendation to change the rule requires all of the following:

1. **Safety no worse than the current rule, on every safety measure, with margin and evidence at the stage boundaries.** Under identical conditions, the staged rule must not increase reservoir-side exceedance, downstream exceedance at the town, exceedance magnitude or duration, or required flood pool. This must hold for the 45-year replay (no event that the current rule handled and the staged rule fails), for the scaled and stochastic floods up to the design standard, and for the boundary stress cases. The risk estimates need uncertainty bounds, and the bounds must not cross the benchmark in the unsafe direction.
2. **Dam safety under the stage levels.** In every stage, the design and check floods stay below the dam's safety level with the staged level as the starting condition, under the real or best-estimate gate rule.
3. **Feasibility shown, not assumed.** Drawdown and refill at each transition are achievable within the time, outlet capacity, and downstream limits, including late and early onsets. The needed actions are within the authority and operating tools of the operator.
4. **Benefit that is material and robust.** The gain in refill reliability, storage at key dates, or usable water (and in energy and irrigation if data permit) is larger than its uncertainty (bootstrap intervals exclude zero), holds in held-out years, is not driven by one or two years, and does not come with a worse outcome in dry years.
5. **Robustness.** The recommendation survives the sensitivity tests, especially the downstream threshold, the stage date shifts, the peak-to-daily ratio, and non-stationarity. If it fails any, the recommendation is narrowed (smaller level increases, or only one stage), not dropped silently.
6. **Mechanism explained and checked** against the specific years and events that show the gain and the risk.
7. **The data limitations are explicit.** If the downstream safe discharge or local inflow was not obtained, or if no sub-daily data exist, the recommendation is conditional on those items and says so. The recommended action may then be a "pilot with monitoring" or a "further assessment", not a final rule change.

**Conclusions the study can support by evidence level.**
- If criteria 1–4 hold but 5–7 are partial: recommend a limited staged rule (smaller increments, one stage) as a proposal to the authority, for a pilot with monitoring, an explicit fallback to the current rule, and a post-event review.
- If safety is met but the benefit is within uncertainty: report that staging offers no demonstrable gain at this site, and which condition limits it.
- If any safety criterion fails: do not recommend, and report which stage and event type fails. That is a finding about where the fixed level is needed.

**What would change my judgment.**
- A downstream safe discharge close to the maximum outlet capacity, a large uncontrolled local inflow, or a late-season extreme flood in the record would push toward "staging gives little or none".
- Strong seasonality with low flood load in the late stage, spare outlet capacity, and a drawdown time inside a reliable lead time would push toward a substantial gain in the late stage.
- Forecast skill on large events at 2–5 days would make a forecast-informed extension worth evaluating. Poor skill on large events would limit forecasts to a supporting role.

## 9. Work plan (order of work and the decision after each part)

1. **Confirm the scope and the data requests** with the supervisor and the operating authority. Decision: what the downstream threshold and demand data are, and whether the study is a screening study or a full study.
2. **Assemble and audit the data**, encode the current rule, validate the simulator, and run the 45-year baseline. Decision: is there a demonstrable weakness of the fixed level worth addressing? If baseline spill and refill failure are small, stop early.
3. **Flood regime by sub-period**, stage options, and design floods per stage. Decision: how many stages, and whether the seasonal differentiation is real.
4. **Feasible ceilings and the candidate search.** Decision: which candidates are physically and temporally feasible.
5. **Risk and benefit evaluation** on replay, scaled, and stochastic events, with boundary stress. Decision: which candidates pass the safety criteria.
6. **Robustness and holdout tests**, with intervals on every headline number.
7. **Forecast layer** (if the earlier steps support a staged rule): error characterization, three information conditions, conservative use, fallback.
8. **Write-up** with the recommendation graded by the evidence levels above, and, if moving toward adoption, a pilot proposal with consultation with the operating authority.

## 10. Limitations to state in the study
- Results are model-based hindcast and simulation results, not an observed operation.
- A 45-year daily record cannot establish extreme-tail floods. The tail relies on frequency analysis with wide intervals or on provided design floods.
- Daily data smooth peaks; the downstream threshold and local inflow may be assumed or sensitivity-tested.
- The eight-year forecast archive is a short basis for the forecast error model.
- A fixed-date stage plan depends on the monsoon calendar. Climate change or a shift in timing can change it, and a trigger-based or forecast-informed stage change could be a later study.
- Operator discretion, legal limits, and approval procedures are not represented unless the data are supplied.

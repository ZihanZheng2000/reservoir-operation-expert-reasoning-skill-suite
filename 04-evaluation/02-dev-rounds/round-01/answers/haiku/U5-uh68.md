# Reservoir Prerelease Decision: Two-Dam Cascade Under Forecast Uncertainty

## Recommendation

**Preferred alternative: (b) Only the lower reservoir starts prerelease now, with clear conditional triggers for upper-dam prerelease at the 12-hour forecast update.**

This is not a wait-and-see decision. It is a conditional commitment to lower prerelease, with escalation rules when new information arrives.

---

## Core Reasoning

### 1. System Timing Creates Asymmetry

The 18-hour travel time from upper to lower dam is the critical system parameter here. It decouples the flood risks at the two points:

- **Lower reservoir pressure window:** Uncontrolled tributary flooding arrives in 12 hours (at hour 30). The lower dam must have capacity and measured releases in place *before* tributary floods hit, because the tributary flow is unavoidable.
  
- **Upper reservoir pressure window:** Flood-control releases from upper don't reach lower until hour 48 (18-hour travel time after they leave the upper dam). Upper has 30 hours before its own flood arrives, then an 18-hour buffer before the consequence is felt downstream.

**Implication:** Lower is the binding constraint in the first 12 hours. It should prerelease now to build capacity for the uncontrolled tributary peak.

### 2. Forecast Uncertainty Favors Adaptive Decision-Making

The forecast range of 150–400 million m³ (±60% from central estimate) is large enough that waiting 12 hours for the next forecast significantly reduces uncertainty. At 12-hour update cycles, the forecast skill typically improves by one lead-time step. This is decision-relevant.

However, **waiting 24 hours (until after the 12-hour forecast) to begin any prerelease is too late for the lower dam**, because:
- Uncontrolled tributary flooding starts at hour 30.
- Lower needs 12–18 hours to build drawdown capacity.
- A 12-hour wait means only ~12 hours remain for lower to prerelease before tributary flows arrive.

**Therefore, lower should act now; upper should act contingently after the forecast update.**

### 3. Capacity Constraints Favor Lower-First Prerelease

**Upper reservoir:**
- Free flood-control capacity: 120 million m³ (40% of 300 million m³)
- Forecast system inflow: ~250 million m³ over 72 hours
- Tributary portion (uncontrolled): estimated ~50–100 million m³ (average 300–1,500 m³/s over variable duration)
- Upper's expected inflow: 150–200 million m³
- **Upper has a capacity deficit of 30–80 million m³** if the forecast is correct.

This is significant but not catastrophic. Upper will spill if it does not prerelease, but spillage itself is not a failure if the dam has adequate freeboard and spillway capacity. The greater risk is being forced into uncontrolled spilling rather than managed prerelease.

**Lower reservoir:**
- Free flood-control capacity: 425 million m³ (85% of 500 million m³)
- Expected inflow: ~100–150 million m³ from upper (delayed 18 hours) + tributary flow (immediate)
- **Lower has ample capacity**, but it must have *available* capacity *before* the tributary peak, which is imminent.

**Conclusion:** Lower is capacity-rich but time-poor. Upper is capacity-constrained but time-rich. This pattern favors lower prerelease now, upper prerelease later (if forecast confirms the upper capacity risk).

### 4. Alternative Analysis

**Option (a) — Both prerelease now:**
- **Risk:** Creates unnecessary release into the lower reach over the next 12 hours. Current downstream flow (~2,500 m³/s + tributary 300 m³/s) is well below the safe city flow of 6,000 m³/s. Large prerelease from both dams could push flows toward 4,500–5,000 m³/s unnecessarily.
- **Benefit:** Eliminates upper's capacity risk entirely. Maximum conservatism.
- **Timing mismatch:** Upper's prerelease helps lower only after the 18-hour travel time (hour 48+). It does not address the immediate uncontrolled tributary risk at hour 30.

**Option (c) — Decide after 12-hour forecast:**
- **Risk to lower:** Uncontrolled tributary peaks around hour 30–42. If lower waits the full 12 hours and does not prerelease, it has only 12–18 hours to build capacity *after* the new forecast arrives. This is feasible (lower can drawdown 150–200 million m³ in 12 hours at 3,500 m³/s release above baseline), but it compresses the margin.
- **Benefit:** Reduces forecast uncertainty before committing. If the updated forecast shows inflow <200 million m³, unnecessary prerelease is avoided.
- **Recommendation:** Defer for now, but only if lower prerelease begins *immediately after* the 12-hour forecast is received.

## Risk and Acceptable Safety (RP04-D, RP04-E)

**Acceptable risk benchmark:** Current operations keep both reservoirs well below FLWL. City flow is ~2,800–3,000 m³/s, which is comfortably below the safe 6,000 m³/s limit.

**Risk from option (b):**
- Lower prerelease increases downstream flows to ~3,500–4,000 m³/s in hours 0–18.
- This is still below 6,000 m³/s, so no downstream safety issue.
- Upper waits; if it fills unexpectedly (before the 12-hour forecast), spillage occurs, but spillage is a known outcome, not a surprise failure.

**Risk from option (c) deferred prerelease:**
- If the 12-hour forecast confirms inflow >300 million m³ *and* hydrograph peaks early (within 48 hours), lower must prerelease 4,000+ m³/s starting immediately. This is compressed and risky.
- If the forecast improves and shows <200 million m³, unnecessary prerelease is avoided—a benefit.

## Adaptive Logic and Immediate Actions (RP07-A, RP07-C, RP07-D)

**Now (hour 0):**
1. **Commit to option (b):** Lower reservoir increases release gradually from 2,500 to 4,500 m³/s over 4–6 hours (ramping controls discharge fluctuations), holding at 4,500 m³/s through hour 18.
2. **Prepare upper:** Increase surveillance. Stage upstream dam equipment and operators for possible prerelease activation. Prepare gates for increase to 4,000–5,000 m³/s.
3. **Prepare downstream:** Alert city/flood-risk areas that flows will increase to ~4,000 m³/s (still safe, but higher than current ~2,800). No evacuation needed yet.
4. **Set rolling update:**  Recompute water balance and risk at the 12-hour forecast update.

**Trigger at hour 12 (next forecast):**
- **If cumulative inflow forecast remains 250 ± 50 million m³ and hydrograph peaks in hours 48–72:**
  - Continue option (b). Upper remains on standby.
  - City flow stays ~4,000 m³/s; safe.
  
- **If forecast increases to 300+ million m³ or early-peak signal occurs (peak < 48 hours):**
  - Escalate to option (a)-like: activate upper prerelease to 4,500–5,000 m³/s immediately (hour 12–18 window).
  - Lower continues at 4,500 m³/s.
  - Combined effect: both reservoirs building capacity for the bulk of the flood volume, arriving at lower at hour 48+.

- **If forecast decreases to <200 million m³ or hydrograph weakens:**
  - Reduce lower's prerelease back to ~3,000 m³/s by hour 18 (a partial prerelease).
  - Preserve upper's full capacity, but keep it ready.

**Trigger at hour 30 (flood arrival at upper, uncontrolled tributary activation):**
- Measure actual tributary and upper inflows.
- If measured inflow to lower (from tributary) exceeds forecast by >20%, increase lower release to 5,500–6,000 m³/s (approaching safe city limit).
- If measured upper inflow exceeds forecast by >20%, activate upper at highest safe prerelease rate (up to 7,000 m³/s if lower can accept it).

## Key Assumptions

1. **Forecast skill:** Forecast improvement is expected between hour 0 and hour 12 (typical 12-hour lead-time skill is moderate to good for precipitation/flow systems).
2. **Hydrograph shape:** The single inflow forecast (250 ± 150 million m³) is interpreted as cumulative volume; the peak rate is not provided, so I assume a "typical" flood hydrograph with peak occurring in hours 36–60 (based on 30-hour lead time and 72-hour total duration).
3. **Tributary control:** The uncontrolled tributary (300–1,500 m³/s) has no gate control. Its behavior is driven by rainfall and cannot be preemptively released.
4. **Dam properties:** Both dams are assumed to have adequate spillway capacity, freeboard, and structural safety margins. The "free share of flood-control storage" is interpreted as the available space between current level and FLWL.
5. **Ramping and operations:** Release changes can be made gradually (over 4–6 hours for ramping) without violating downstream channel-stability constraints (typically 500–1,000 m³/s per hour). The question states release capacity (8,000 m³/s) but not these operational gradients; I assume they are standard practice.

## Information That Would Change the Recommendation

1. **Current storage levels:** If upper is already >90% full (not 60% as implied by "40% free"), option (a) becomes mandatory immediately.
2. **Hydrograph peak rate:** If the forecast specifies peak inflow >6,000 m³/s to upper (vs. average 964 m³/s), upper's risk increases and option (a) becomes safer.
3. **Tributary forecast:** If the 12-hour forecast includes a tributary-specific forecast (e.g., "tributary will peak at 1,200 m³/s at hour 36"), that sharpens lower's decision.
4. **Downstream hazard:** If the city or lower-reach area has a known bottleneck (e.g., levee failure probability increases above 2% at flows >5,500 m³/s), the 6,000 m³/s safe flow threshold might need revision downward.
5. **Spillway status:** If either dam's spillway is under maintenance or blocked, the capacity estimates change drastically.

## Summary

**Choose option (b) now: Lower reservoir prerelease only.** Increase lower release to 4,500 m³/s immediately (ramp over 4–6 hours). Keep upper on high alert. At the 12-hour forecast update, if inflow forecast >300 million m³ or peak timing shifts early, escalate to option (a) immediately. This strategy balances forecast uncertainty (waiting for hour-12 update reduces it), system timing (lower is time-constrained; upper is not), and feasibility (both can prerelease within their windows if triggered).

The city flow remains below 6,000 m³/s during this action, and both reservoirs retain large capacity margins for the range of forecast scenarios presented.

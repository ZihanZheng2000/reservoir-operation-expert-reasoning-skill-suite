# Forecast error and joint flood operation of Guxian and Luhun: study design

## 1. Short answer

**Provisional judgment on the size of the effect.** Treating the two deterministic forecasts as certain probably understates flood risk most in exactly the cases that matter: large floods. There are three reasons. (a) In this kind of data-driven inflow model, relative error usually grows with lead time and with flow magnitude, and large floods are thin in the training data. (b) The two catchments are adjacent and are usually hit by the same storm system, so the errors at Guxian and Luhun are probably positively correlated. Planning each reservoir from its own point forecast therefore underestimates the chance that both reservoirs are short of storage in the same event. (c) Volume errors build up over the hours of a flood, because hourly errors are strongly autocorrelated. Before any modelling, I expect the change to be small for moderate floods (the reservoirs have spare storage that absorbs the error) and large for floods near the scale of 18 September 2021, where a volume error of a few tens of percent is comparable to the storage left. The study must measure this, not assume it.

**Provisional recommendation on coordination.** Replace the single deterministic joint plan with a rolling, risk-based joint plan:

1. Generate inflow scenarios for both reservoirs, and for the uncontrolled area between the dams and Heishiguan, from the forecast/observation archive. The scenarios must keep the error dependence across lead times and between the two sites.
2. Each hour, choose one shared current release pair. Later releases may differ by scenario.
3. Constrain the joint plan by an acceptable probability of exceeding the safe flow at Heishiguan and, separately, by an acceptable probability of exceeding each dam's critical level. Dam safety is a hard constraint and is never traded against downstream benefit.
4. Share the joint holding so that the two reservoirs carry comparable risk, not equal levels or fixed proportions. The reservoir with more remaining storage relative to its forecast-error spread holds more of the peak. The other keeps a margin.
5. Stagger the two releases so that, after routing, they do not coincide with each other or with the interval-inflow peak at Heishiguan.
6. Keep the current rule as the measurement-based fallback, with explicit switching triggers.

This recommendation is provisional. It holds only if the study shows that (i) error-aware planning lowers the risk at Heishiguan and at the dams compared with the current deterministic practice, under the same conditions and out of sample, and (ii) the gain is larger than the precision the record allows.

## 2. The deciding questions

These are the questions whose answers would change the recommendation. The study is built around them.

1. **How large and how dependent are the forecast errors during large floods?** This means errors by lead time and flow class, the dependence between Guxian and Luhun, the autocorrelation across lead times, and timing (peak-phase) errors. If the errors are small or weakly correlated, deterministic planning with a modest margin may be enough. If they are large and correlated, joint scenario-based planning is needed.
2. **Where does the binding risk sit: at the dams or at Heishiguan, and how much of the Heishiguan flow is uncontrolled?** If the interval inflow below the two dams dominates the Heishiguan peak, release coordination can only shave part of it. The value of better forecasts at the reservoirs is then bounded, and the interval-inflow forecast error becomes a first-order risk source.
3. **Can the record resolve the differences claimed?** There is one well-documented extreme event (2021), and the archive probably holds only a few comparable large floods. The study must show that its risk differences are larger than their sampling uncertainty. Otherwise it should report bounded, conditional results.

## 3. Study design

### Step 1. Baseline: system, current practice, data alignment

- **Physical system.** For each reservoir, assemble the level–storage curve, the discharge capacity curve by outlet type at actual head, the characteristic levels (flood-limited level, design and check flood levels, crest), and limits on release change and ramping. Assemble the Heishiguan safe discharge and the warning and guaranteed flows. Do the same for the Yellow River control point that Heishiguan feeds, as far as I know Huayuankou. Releases that are safe for the Yiluo may still matter for the main stem.
- **Data alignment.** Put hourly observed inflows at both dams, interval flows, Heishiguan flows, operating records, and the forecast archive on one hourly timeline with consistent issue times. Each archived forecast needs its issue time and lead time.
- **Interval inflow.** Derive interval inflow at Heishiguan as observed Heishiguan flow minus routed dam releases. Check its share of peak and volume in each large event.
- **Executable baseline.** Encode the current practice as a simulator. It plans joint releases from the single deterministic forecast at each issue time, executes the first step, and updates at the next issue. Check this simulator against the 2021 record of levels, releases and Heishiguan flows before using it as the comparator.
- **2021 baseline conditions.** Record what the starting levels were on 18 September 2021, which was late in the flood season. Record whether actual operation was constrained by things the simulator leaves out: gate availability, instructions from the basin authority, main-stem conditions. An observed outcome that was forced by such constraints is not a fair baseline.

### Step 2. Forecast reliability and error structure

- **Use consistent hindcasts.** Check that the archived forecasts come from the same model version and configuration that will be used in operation. Data-driven models are often retrained. If they were, regenerate hindcasts with the current model using leave-one-event-out training. Otherwise the error statistics describe a different forecaster, and in-sample hindcasts understate error for large floods.
- **Error statistics.** Define relative error as e = (forecast − observed)/observed, by lead time (for example 1–48 h, set by the available horizon) and by flow class (for example below, near and above the Heishiguan warning-equivalent inflow). Report bias, spread and tails separately for:
  - peak magnitude,
  - peak timing,
  - event volume.

  Peak timing matters because at an hourly step a peak forecast 3–6 h early or late changes release timing as much as a magnitude error does.
- **Distribution form.** Test whether a normal fits within each class. If it does not, fit a skewed or mixture distribution, or use the empirical distribution. If the archive is too thin in the large-flood class, use a qualified-rate-based normal or a scaled-up spread as an explicit, labelled assumption, and bracket it with sensitivity runs.
- **Dependence.** Estimate the correlation of errors:
  - across lead times within each site,
  - between Guxian and Luhun at the same issue time,
  - with the interval-inflow error, if interval forecasts exist. If they do not, the interval error has to be built from rainfall-based estimates or bracketed.
- **Usefulness by lead time.** State over what lead time the forecast can support which action: prerelease needs volume skill tens of hours ahead; peak shaving needs magnitude and timing skill over the hours before the peak.

### Step 3. Scenario generation

- **How scenarios are built.** Generate inflow scenarios as forecast × (1 + e), with errors drawn jointly across lead times and sites. A t-copula or Gaussian copula over lead times and sites, with flow-class-conditional marginals, is one option. Conditional resampling of whole historical error trajectories from similar forecast situations is another, and it keeps timing errors intact. Build one scenario set at every issue time in each test event.
- **Faithfulness checks before use.** Check coverage of the observed hydrographs (the share of hours inside the 90% band), rank histograms, the reproduced cross-site correlation, and the reproduced volume-error distribution. Do this on held-out events, in particular on 2021 with 2021 excluded from fitting.
- **Stress events.** Scale the largest observed events, including 2021, to larger floods (for example 1.2× and 1.5× volume, and the design flood) with forecast errors attached. This tests behaviour beyond the record, which is necessary because the risk thresholds sit in the tail.

### Step 4. Response simulation

- **Reservoirs.** Run an hourly water balance for each reservoir with level-dependent outlet capacity, ramping limits, and gate rules.
- **Routing.** Route each release to Heishiguan with a calibrated lag-and-route or Muskingum model per reach, and add interval inflow. Calibrate on the observed events. In particular, check the travel times from each dam to Heishiguan (distance over wave speed, which depends on flow).
- **Timing cases.** Route cases where the interval peak arrives before, together with, and after the dam releases. A release decision that is safe only when the interval peak is late is not safe.
- **Rollout.** For every scenario, record the maximum level at each dam, the peak flow and the duration and volume above the safe flow at Heishiguan, the end-of-event levels, and the spill.

### Step 5. Risk definitions and acceptable levels

- **Reservoir risk.** For each dam, risk is P(Z_max > Z_threshold). The threshold is the level that the operating instruction sets as the boundary of normal flood operation, for example the level reached by the design flood routed from the flood-limited level. If the design or check level is used instead, both should be reported.
- **Downstream risk.** P(Q_Heishiguan > Q_safe), plus the expected exceedance volume and duration, and CVaR of the peak, so that the tail is not hidden by a probability alone.
- **System risk.** Report the union "any threshold exceeded" and the joint "both reservoirs above threshold", and keep the components visible. With positively correlated errors, the joint probability is higher than the product of the marginals. Assuming independence would understate the event in which neither reservoir can help.
- **Acceptable risk.** Use as the benchmark the risk of the current rule when it is evaluated under the same error scenarios. Any new rule must not exceed it at either dam or at Heishiguan, using the upper confidence bound of its risk estimate, not the central value. Where an explicit design standard exists, it also binds.
- **Answering the research question.** The forecast-error effect is measured as

  ΔR = R(current deterministic practice, evaluated under the error scenarios) − R(same practice with perfect forecasts).

  Decompose ΔR by switching the error on one source at a time: Guxian only, Luhun only, interval only, all sources with dependence, and all sources assuming independence. The last pair shows how much ignoring correlation would mislead. Report ΔR by event size class.

### Step 6. Candidate coordination strategies (judged first, then computed)

These strategies are structurally different, and all are kept for comparison:

| Strategy | Information used | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| A. Current practice | Deterministic forecasts, rolling | Baseline |
| B. Measurement-based rule | Current inflow and level only | No-forecast lower reference and fallback |
| C. Deterministic plan with a risk margin | Upper quantile (e.g. 80–95%) of the error distribution, chosen per lead time to meet the acceptable risk | Whether a simple safety margin is enough |
| D. Rolling scenario-based joint MPC | Scenario tree from Step 3; shared current release pair; chance constraints at both dams and Heishiguan; objective trades Heishiguan peak or exceedance against end levels and refill | Full error-aware coordination |
| E. Risk-sharing rule distilled from D | A few thresholds on remaining storage, forecast spread and interval-inflow forecast | An implementable rule, if D's behaviour can be summarized |
| F. Perfect forecast | Observed future inflows | Upper bound on forecast value |

**Coordination logic to settle before computing:**

- **Sharing the joint storage.** Allocate holding so that the marginal increase in exceedance risk per unit stored is comparable at the two dams, within each dam's own safety limit. In practice, the reservoir with the larger ratio of remaining flood storage to forecast volume-error spread takes more of the peak. Each dam's own level risk is checked whenever it is asked to hold water for the other.
- **Staggering the peaks.** Choose release timing so that, after routing, the two releases and the interval peak do not coincide at Heishiguan. Travel-time differences between the two reaches are the lever here.
- **Flood stages.**
  - Prerelease while the forecast and its upper band exceed the remaining storage. Prerelease is capped at what can be refilled under the low-side forecast, and must never raise Heishiguan above what the natural flood would have produced.
  - Detention on the rising limb and at the peak, within the chance constraints.
  - Recession: empty the flood-control zone back to the flood-limited level as soon as possible without pushing Heishiguan above the safe flow, then refill only after the flood period ends.
- **Correction and abort.** If observed inflow leaves the scenario band for a set number of consecutive hours, or the forecast is missing or late, revert to strategy B. If a prerelease is under way and the flood arrives early, stop the prerelease and switch to detention. This abort rule has to be stated in advance.
- **What can wait.** Decisions can wait for the next forecast update when that update arrives before the point of no return. For a prerelease, the point of no return is the last hour at which the needed volume can still be released within outlet and downstream limits. For detention, it is the last hour before the routed release would coincide with the interval peak.

**Methods.** Scenario MPC with a tree of tens of branches per issue time is computationally feasible for two reservoirs at hourly steps. Grid or evolutionary search fits the thresholds of C and E. Keep a noninferior set over dam risk, Heishiguan risk, and end-level or refill benefit. Choose a compromise only after the safety screen, and test whether the ranking holds across events and error scales.

### Step 7. Testing

- **Same conditions.** Run strategies A–F under identical inflows, initial levels, constraints and routing, in closed loop: each strategy's own releases update the state, and forecasts are re-issued hourly from the archive.
- **Out of sample.** Leave out whole events: fit the error model and the rule thresholds without the test event, and in particular with 2021 held out. Report results event by event and as trajectories, not only as aggregates.
- **Error scale.** Multiply the error spread by k = 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2. Find the error size at which the preferred strategy switches, and beyond which the forecast no longer helps (D or C stops beating B). Read the curve backward to state the forecast accuracy the acceptable risk requires.
- **Other sensitivity.** Vary initial levels (from the flood-limited level up to the observed 2021 starting levels), the dependence assumption (independent versus fitted), the number of scenarios, and the stress-scaled floods.
- **Mechanism.** Explain each result by its cause: which reservoir held what, how much came from peak staggering, and how much was lost to interval inflow.

## 4. Order-of-magnitude checks to carry through the study

All volumes below are illustrative. The reservoir storages and flood volumes must come from the reservoir characteristics provided.

- **Storage at stake versus forecast error.** A sustained net release change of 1,000 m³/s for 24 h moves 1,000 × 86,400 ≈ 86 million m³. If a large flood brings, say, 500 million m³ into one reservoir over three days, a 20% volume error is 100 million m³. That is more than a full day of 1,000 m³/s extra release. Compare this with the actual flood-control storage of each reservoir. If the error volume is a sizeable fraction of the remaining storage, the error changes the plan materially. If it is a few percent, a margin (strategy C) may be enough.
- **Accumulated error with autocorrelation.** For n hourly errors of spread σ and lag correlation ρ, Var(sum) = nσ²(1 + (n − 1)ρ). For n = 24 and ρ = 0.8, the factor is 1 + 23 × 0.8 = 19.4. The SD of the 24-h volume error is therefore √19.4 ≈ 4.4 times what an independence assumption would give. Between the two reservoirs with ρ = 0.5, Var(sum) = 2σ²(1 + 0.5) = 3σ², so the combined error SD is √1.5 ≈ 1.22 times the independent value. This is why dependence has to be fitted, not assumed away.
- **Travel time.** Travel time = reach length / wave speed. For example, 100 km at 3 m/s ≈ 9 h. That spans many hourly steps, so a release made now still arrives hours later, and a release cut now cannot remove water already in the channel. The actual reach lengths and calibrated celerities replace these numbers.
- **Monte Carlo precision.** For a risk p estimated from N scenarios, SE = √(p(1 − p)/N). For p = 1% and N = 10,000, SE ≈ 0.1 percentage points (10% relative). For p = 0.1%, about 100,000 scenarios are needed for similar relative precision. Scenario count is the minor problem, though.
- **Event-count precision.** The bigger limitation is the number of independent large events behind the error model. With n large events, the spread estimate has a relative SE of about 1/√(2n): with n = 8, about 25%. A risk difference between strategies that is smaller than what a ±25% error-spread change produces cannot be claimed as established. The error-scale sensitivity in Step 7 shows how large that band is.
- **Combined probabilities.** If the event risk at Guxian is p₁ and at Luhun is p₂, the union is at most p₁ + p₂. Under independence the joint is p₁p₂, but with positively correlated errors it can be close to min(p₁, p₂). For example, with p₁ = p₂ = 2%, the independent joint is 0.04%, while a strongly correlated joint can approach 2%. That is a 50-fold difference in the "both reservoirs overwhelmed" event.

## 5. What would confirm or change the recommendation

- **Confirms it.** The error spread for large floods is a sizeable fraction of the remaining storage, the errors are positively correlated across sites and lead times, and strategy D (or the rule E distilled from it) lowers Heishiguan and dam risk compared with A on held-out events, including 2021, with gains larger than the event-count uncertainty band.
- **Changes it towards the simple margin (C).** C performs within the uncertainty band of D. Then the simpler rule is preferable, because it is easier to approve and operate.
- **Changes the focus to interval inflow.** The interval inflow dominates the Heishiguan peak, so that no reservoir strategy keeps Heishiguan safe in the large events. The priority then shifts to forecasting the interval area and to downstream warning, and the reservoir coordination becomes secondary.
- **Changes it towards the fallback (B).** Forecast errors for the largest floods are so large or poorly characterized (too few events, forecast outside its training range) that D gives no reliable gain over B. The recommendation is then to keep measurement-based joint operation, use the forecast only for early warning and a capped prerelease, and invest in forecast improvement first.

## 6. Adoption

The operating basis of both reservoirs is the approved flood-control operation plan. As far as I know, Guxian and Luhun are operated jointly with the main-stem reservoirs under the Yellow River Conservancy Commission and its flood-control and drought-relief headquarters, with the provincial (Henan) flood-control headquarters involved. Any change would go through the annual flood-control operation plan approved by that authority. Until then, the current plan remains the legal basis.

A practical route has four parts:

- **Shadow operation.** Run the error-aware plan for one or more flood seasons beside the current practice, logging both recommendations, so it can be compared with what operators actually did.
- **Acceptance criteria.** Agree acceptance criteria with the authority in advance. For example: no increase in dam or Heishiguan exceedance risk at the upper confidence bound, and a stated reduction in Heishiguan exceedance in held-out events.
- **Reversion and logging.** Define reversion triggers (forecast missing, late, or diverging from observations; gate or power failure), name who decides, and log every decision for audit.
- **Warning and review.** Warn downstream emergency services and the public of any prerelease that is not caused by natural inflow. Train operators, keep the rule under version control, and review it after every major flood.

## 7. Assumptions stated

- The archive pairs forecasts and observations at the hourly issue times used in operation, and covers several large floods besides 2021. The number of large events is not given. The precision checks above are expressed so that they work with whatever count is found.
- No interval-inflow forecast is mentioned. If none exists, the interval error is represented by bracketing scenarios built from observed interval hydrographs, and the results are reported as conditional on that assumption.
- Reservoir characteristic levels, outlet capacities and the Heishiguan safe discharge are taken from the reservoir characteristics provided. None of their values are assumed here. All volumes and travel times in Section 4 are illustrative and will be replaced by the case values.
- Hydropower and water supply enter as end-of-event level and refill indicators, considered only after the safety constraints are met.

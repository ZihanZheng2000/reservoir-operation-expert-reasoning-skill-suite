# Forecast-Dependent Flood-Season Level Control at Three Gorges: Plan and Provisional Recommendation

## 1. Recommendation in brief

**Don't pick one fixed lead time. Build a daily rule that depends on the forecast state.** On each decision day, the rule asks how much storage above 145 m could still be released before a flood arrives. It answers using two inputs:

- a conservative upper quantile of the forecast inflow, one per lead time;
- the release that is really available at the dam, which is 35,000 m³/s minus the local inflow that joins below the dam, minus the 2-hour operating delay.

A lead time counts as "trusted" on that day only if adding it increases that guaranteed drawdown volume. A lead time also stops being usable as soon as its upper-quantile forecast reaches 35,000 m³/s; the rule then switches to pre-release. The allowable level for the day is 145 m plus the largest guaranteed volume, converted to a level. That level is then capped by a drawdown-rate limit and by an approved ceiling.

**The agency's risk tolerance enters in one place: the quantile.** A higher quantile is safer and gives a lower level. The agency gets a curve of "hydropower gain against upstream risk and downstream risk." The point where risk equals that of the current 3-day practice is the default choice.

**Provisional expectation, from arithmetic with assumed numbers (Section 3).**
- Under typical falling-stage inflow (about 25,500 m³/s), forecasts out to about 4–5 days add usable drawdown capacity. Days 6–7 add little.
- A plausible ceiling is about 149–150 m if the local inflow below the dam is small.
- If local inflow takes a few thousand m³/s of the 35,000 m³/s allowance, the ceiling is only about 147 m. Then the current 148 m may already be at the limit.
- When forecast inflow approaches about 30,000 m³/s, only 1–3 days of lead are usable and the allowed level falls toward 145 m.

**How the gain should be presented.** Most of the hydropower gain comes from higher head, not from catching spilled water. A few metres of storage (about 17 × 10⁸ m³ for 3 m) is small compared with seasonal spills of about 500 × 10⁸ m³. The gain should be presented that way, not as "recovering the spill."

**What could change this.** Three findings would change the recommendation:
- the measured local inflow below the dam;
- the measured upper-tail error and the miss rate for inflows above 35,000 m³/s at each lead time;
- routing the design floods from the raised starting levels.

If design floods routed from about 150 m break the Jingjiang 1-in-100-year protection target, the ceiling must stay at or below 148 m, whatever the forecasts can support.

## 2. The three questions that decide the answer

1. **How much storage above 145 m can physically be released in L days without the protected reach exceeding 35,000 m³/s?** This is a mass-balance question. It depends on inflow during the drawdown, local inflow below the dam, the 2-hour delay, and any drawdown-rate limit. It sets a hard ceiling that no forecast can raise.
2. **How wrong can the forecast be over the drawdown window, and how often does it miss an inflow above 35,000 m³/s at each lead time?** What matters is the error in the cumulative inflow, which grows faster than a single-day error because errors are correlated across lead times. This decides which lead times add capacity at a given risk level.
3. **What is the acceptable risk, and can the data measure it?** The benchmark is the risk of current 3-day practice, computed by exactly the same method. The 2019 window (53 decision days, probably only a handful of flood arrivals) cannot measure risks of a few percent on its own.

## 3. Feasibility arithmetic: why the answer is likely 4–5 days and about 149–150 m

**Assumptions** (to be replaced with the supplied level–storage relation and the hindcast error statistics):

- Storage per metre just above 145 m is A ≈ 5.6 × 10⁸ m³/m. This is a planning value. It is consistent with the 221.5 × 10⁸ m³ flood storage spread over 145–175 m (average 7.4 × 10⁸ m³/m), with the area smaller near the bottom of that range.
- Unit conversion: 1,000 m³/s held for one day is 0.864 × 10⁸ m³, which is about 0.154 m of level.
- The current inflow is the falling-stage mean of 25,500 m³/s, held flat over the lead window.
- The relative forecast error at the 90% upper quantile grows with lead time. I assumed σ = 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20 and 23% for days 1 to 7. These figures are illustrative only and must be replaced with fitted values.
- The release limit at the dam is 35,000 m³/s minus local inflow Q_loc. On day 1, only 22 of 24 hours are usable because of the 2-hour delay.

**Drawdown capacity.** Day by day, the capacity is (35,000 − Q_loc − Q̂⁹⁰_j) × Δt.

| Lead L (days) | Upper-quantile inflow (m³/s) | Daily net release, Q_loc = 0 | Cumulative level, Q_loc = 0 | Cumulative level, Q_loc = 3,000 (assumed) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 27,130 | 7,870 (7,210 after the 2-h delay) | 1.1 m | 0.7 m |
| 2 | 28,110 | 6,890 | 2.2 m | 1.3 m |
| 3 | 29,090 | 5,910 | 3.1 m | 1.7 m |
| 4 | 30,070 | 4,930 | 3.8 m | 2.0 m |
| 5 | 31,050 | 3,950 | 4.5 m | 2.2 m |
| 6 | 32,030 | 2,970 | 4.9 m | 2.2 m (net release on this day is 0 or below; adds nothing) |
| 7 | 33,010 | 1,990 | 5.2 m | 2.2 m (adds nothing) |

What the table shows:

- **The method reproduces current practice.** With no local inflow, a 3-day lead gives about 3.1 m, which matches the current 148 m. That is a useful check on the method.
- **Longer leads add less and less.** Each extra day adds less because the upper-quantile inflow rises toward the release limit. The useful lead ends where the daily net release falls to zero. With no local inflow that point is just beyond 7 days here. Past about 5 days, each extra day adds less than 0.5 m and depends on the least reliable forecasts. In practice the cut-off will come earlier, because real errors probably grow faster than assumed.
- **Local inflow below the dam is decisive.** At 3,000 m³/s it cuts the ceiling to about 147 m. Lead times beyond about 5 days then add nothing.
- **Correlation inflates the cumulative error.** For n days with daily spread σ and correlation ρ, the variance of the sum is nσ²(1 + (n − 1)ρ). For 3 days at ρ = 0.7, that is 7.2σ² instead of 3σ², so the standard deviation is about 1.55 times larger. For example, at 28,000 m³/s inflow with σ = 10%, the 3-day volume standard deviation is about 2.68 × 2,800 m³/s-days. That is about 6.5 × 10⁸ m³, or about 1.2 m of level, compared with about 0.75 m if the errors were independent. The real error model must be fitted to the cumulative inflow over each lead window, not built from independent daily errors.
- **In a rising situation the allowed level shrinks quickly.** If inflow is 30,000 m³/s and rising, the net release is at most about 5,000 m³/s on day 1 and close to zero by day 3. The allowed level is then about 145.5–146 m. Holding a high level is only defensible in recession or low-flow conditions, which is also when it pays.

**Rate and equipment limits.** I don't have the bank-stability drawdown-rate limit or release-ramping limits from the brief. They must be obtained from the operating rules and applied as a cap. If the rate limit is tighter than about 1 m/day, it binds before the lead-time limit for short leads.

**Hydropower plausibility.** I assume the head at 145 m is roughly 75–85 m. Raising the mean level by 1–2 m then raises output per unit of turbined flow by about 1.5–2.5%. The one-off storage gained (about 5.6–11 × 10⁸ m³) is a few percent of one season's spill. Claims of large spill recovery should be rejected.

## 4. The decision rule to build and test

**State each day:** the level Z_d, the 1–7-day forecasts Q̂_{d,1..7}, recent observed inflow and local inflow.

1. **Flood check.** For each lead j, compute the upper quantile Q̂^(q)_{d,j}. Let j* be the first lead where it reaches or exceeds 35,000 m³/s, if there is one.
   - If j* exists, use **pre-release mode**. The target is 145 m by day j*. Release at the lesser of the following: the volume still to be removed divided by the time left, and (35,000 − Q_loc). If the target can't be met by day j*, release at the full allowable rate and log a feasibility warning.
2. **Holding mode** (no lead time is flagged). Compute the guaranteed drawdown volume for each lead L ≤ L_max(q):

   V(L) = Σ_{j=1..L} max(0, 35,000 − Q_loc,j − Q̂^(q)_{d,j}) · Δt_j, with Δt_1 = 22 h.

   - A lead L is **trusted** if, in the hindcast verification, its miss rate for inflows above 35,000 m³/s and its cumulative-error tail for this forecast-magnitude class are no worse than the calibrated quantile assumes. Otherwise it is excluded.
   - Then Z_allow = 145 + max over trusted L of V(L)/A, capped by the drawdown-rate limit and by the approved ceiling Z_cap.
3. **Falling limb.** Refill toward Z_allow only when observed inflow is falling and no lead time is flagged. Keep releases at or above the minimums for power and navigation, as current rules require.
4. **During a flood.** Hand over to the existing flood-control rules from the actual level.
5. **Abort and fallback.**
   - **Point of no return.** For a held excess volume V_h, the point of no return is the last day on which V(remaining lead) is at least V_h. If the next forecast update reduces V below V_h, begin pre-release immediately.
   - **Forecast missing or late.** Revert to the 3-day rule. That means drawing down at the allowable rate until the level is within the 3-day envelope (148 m or lower).
   - **Forecast diverging from observations.** If observed inflow exceeds the day-1 upper quantile for two consecutive days, treat the lead times beyond 3 days as untrusted until the forecast is recalibrated.

**Trust is defined per day, not per season, and its cost is lopsided.**
- A false alarm (forecast above 35,000 m³/s, actual below) causes an early pre-release. That costs head and therefore power, but not safety. Refill is not a concern here, so the cost is small.
- A miss costs safety.

The trust criterion should therefore be built mainly on misses and under-forecasts of cumulative inflow. Over-forecasts should be treated as a power cost.

## 5. Uncertainty and error model

- **Error definition.** Use the relative error e_{d,j} = (Q̂_{d,j} − Q_{d+j})/Q_{d+j} for each lead j = 1..7. Keep one sign convention throughout.
- **Stratify the errors:**
  - by lead time;
  - by forecast magnitude (for example below 20,000, 20,000–30,000, and above 30,000 m³/s), because errors probably grow at high flow;
  - by limb (rising or falling), if the sample allows.
- **Fit the cumulative error** over each window 1..L directly from the hindcast pairs, or fit a joint model across leads (a copula or multivariate normal on transformed errors). Check autocorrelation across successive issue days, because one flood can be under-forecast for several days in a row. That is the main risk mechanism.
- **Generate scenarios** by applying sampled joint error vectors to the observed inflows. This gives synthetic forecast sets that can be run in closed loop. Check the scenarios against the hindcast: coverage, tail, and lead-to-lead correlation.
- **Other sources.** Make the local inflow below the dam an explicit uncertain input. Its forecast, or a high percentile of it, enters Q_loc. Treat errors in the level–storage relation and level measurement (a few decimetres) as a margin on A·ΔZ. I would leave out the uncertainty in discharge capacity because the release limit is set downstream, and I would state that exclusion.
- **Hindcast consistency.** Confirm that the 2019 forecasts and any calibration years come from the same forecast system and configuration used operationally (same issue time, same model version). If not, calibrate only on comparable years.

## 6. Risk definitions, benchmark and the risk–benefit curve

- **Upstream risk.** At each flood arrival (observed inflow first exceeds 35,000 m³/s after a quiet spell), did the level exceed 145 m plus a measurement tolerance? Report three things:
  - the rate per flood arrival;
  - the rate per season, 1 − (1 − p)^k for k arrivals if they are independent, otherwise counted directly;
  - the excess volume at arrival (mean and tail), because a 0.2 m shortfall and a 3 m shortfall are not the same failure.
- **Upstream consequence check.** Route the 2019 floods, and design floods up to the 1-in-100-year Jingjiang standard and the check flood, from the worst level reached at arrival. Check that the 100-year design flood still keeps the downstream control target, and that the maximum level stays below the design and check levels. "Failed to reach 145 m" is only a proxy; this routing shows what it costs.
- **Downstream risk.**
  - Days on which release exceeds 35,000 m³/s, including days when release plus local inflow at the protected reach exceeds it.
  - The extra volume above 35,000 m³/s.
  - A second rule: a pre-release must never make flow in the reach higher than the flow without the reservoir would have been.
  - Report the increase over the baseline, because large floods exceed 35,000 m³/s under any rule.
- **Benchmark.** Encode current 3-day practice (145–148 m, the same pre-release and refill rules) and run it through the same simulator with the same forecasts and synthetic error scenarios. Its risks (R0_up, R0_down) are the acceptable levels at "no worse than today."
- **Risk tolerance sweep.** Run quantiles q = 50, 70, 80, 90, 95 and 99%. For each one, report:
  - upstream risk, downstream risk and the excess volume at arrival;
  - mean level, power output, and spill.

  Plot power gain against risk, with one curve per maximum lead (3, 4, 5, 7, and adaptive). The agency picks a point on the curve. Points with the upper confidence bound of their risk at or below R0 are "no worse than today." Points above R0 show what extra power the extra risk buys.
- **Selection.** Screen first: any option whose upper confidence bound on upstream or downstream risk exceeds the chosen tolerance is dropped, whatever its power. Then compare the remaining options in a side-by-side table (level statistics, power, spill, both risks, excess volume) and name the dominated options.

## 7. Evidence: what 2019 can and cannot show

- **Closed-loop simulation.** Run every policy day by day over 1 June–23 July 2019 at a daily step. The policy's own releases update the level. Use observed inflows as truth and issued forecasts as information. First check the simulator against the recorded 2019 levels and releases where the real operation followed the rules.
- **Reference cases.** Compare the policy against four references:
  - fixed 145 m (no forecast);
  - current 3-day practice;
  - candidate fixed leads;
  - the adaptive rule;
  - perfect forecasts, as an upper bound that could not be deployed.

  The gap between the adaptive rule and perfect forecasts is the value still available from better forecasts.
- **Sample size.** 53 decision days and probably only a few flood arrivals cannot resolve risk differences. With k arrivals and zero failures, the 95% upper bound on the per-arrival failure rate is about 3/k: 100% for k = 3, and 30% for k = 10. Risk must therefore be estimated by Monte Carlo:
  - error scenarios applied to 2019 and to any other years of inflow and hindcast the Bureau can supply;
  - with N = 10,000 runs, the standard error at p = 1% is about 0.1%.

  2019 itself should be shown as a trajectory demonstration, not as proof of a risk level.
- **Out-of-sample testing.** Calibrate the quantile and the trust criteria on years other than 2019 if those hindcasts exist. If only 2019 is available, use leave-one-event-out cross-validation and state that the result is in-sample.
- **Stress tests.**
  - a large flood immediately after a missed forecast;
  - back-to-back floods;
  - 2019 floods scaled to the 1-in-100-year volume;
  - the forecast error inflated by 1.5 times;
  - local inflow at a high percentile;
  - a 2-hour delay that becomes 6 hours (a communication failure).
- **Mechanism.** Explain the result through the table in Section 3: where the daily net release falls to zero for each forecast class and limb. That explains why particular lead times are trusted on particular days.

## 8. Adoption

- **The legal ceiling.** The 148 m ceiling and the 3-day condition sit in the approved operating rules for the reservoir. A higher ceiling or a longer lead would need a revision approved by the water administration. As far as I know, that means:
  - the Ministry of Water Resources, with the Changjiang Water Resources Commission issuing dispatch instructions;
  - consultation with the flood control headquarters and the operator (China Three Gorges Corporation).

  Until approval, current practice remains the legal basis and any new rule runs only in shadow.
- **Trial path.**
  1. Agree acceptance criteria in advance with the flood-control authority: risk no higher than R0 at the upper confidence bound, design-flood routing still meets the Jingjiang standard, and the fallback triggers above.
  2. Shadow operation for one or more seasons: compute the adaptive level daily beside actual operation and log it.
  3. A documented temporary trial with a modest ceiling (for example 149 m) and the 3-day rule as automatic fallback.
  4. Post-season review before any further increase.
- **Who must be informed.** Downstream (Jingjiang) flood-control authorities, navigation authorities (35,000 m³/s is also the maximum navigable flow, and pre-releases change flows quickly), the grid dispatcher (scheduled generation changes), and emergency services for any pre-release.
- **Audit log for each day.** The forecast used, the quantile, the trusted leads, Z_allow, the release, and any fallback.

## 9. What would confirm or change the recommendation

- **Local inflow below the dam.** If it is regularly 2,000–5,000 m³/s, the guaranteed volume falls by about 0.3–0.8 m per day of lead. The recommendation then shrinks to "keep 148 m, but use longer leads only to pre-release earlier." If it is small, about 149–150 m is plausible.
- **Cumulative upper-tail error at days 4–7.** If it is wider than assumed (σ above about 20% at day 4), trusted leads collapse to about 3 days and the gain is marginal. If it is narrower, leads of 5–6 days become useful in recession.
- **Repeated under-forecasting of one flood across several issue days.** If the hindcast shows it often, raise the quantile or shorten L_max for the rising limb.
- **Design-flood routing from raised levels.** If it shows the 1-in-100-year standard is not maintained, cap Z at the level where it is, whatever the forecast skill.
- **Drawdown-rate or bank-stability limits.** If they are tighter than about 1 m/day, short-lead capacity is capped and the ceiling falls accordingly.

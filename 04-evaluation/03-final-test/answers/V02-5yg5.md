# Study design: real-time hourly release schedules for long floods at Daecheong

## 1. Recommendation in brief

Build and test a **two-layer scheduler** that matches how operators already work: each hour it issues a schedule for the next several hours, and only the first step is carried out.

1. **Planning layer: a rolling-horizon scheduler.** This is model predictive control in the form operators already use. Every hour it solves a small optimisation over a horizon of about 24–48 h, much longer than the 6-h rainfall forecast. Inflow over the first 6 h (plus the basin lag) comes from the forecast. Beyond that it uses a small set of **conditional continuation scenarios**: recession, a second rise, and a severe second peak. The first release step is shared across all scenarios; later steps may branch. The plan also carries a **terminal-storage guide** that says how much flood-control space must be free at the end of the horizon, given the current state. The guide is derived offline. Dam safety and the downstream safe discharge are hard constraints. Operator-style limits are built in: release steps in the increments operators use, a minimum holding time between changes, no up-down reversals on the rising limb, and ramp limits.
2. **Explanation and fallback layer: a staged rule that operators can read.** This rule is distilled from the scheduler's first-step decisions. Its stages are pre-release, rising-limb detention, peak, recession/evacuation and refill, and it switches stage on observed level, inflow and its trend, and forecast rainfall. It explains why the scheduler does what it does. It is also the fallback when the forecast is missing, late or clearly wrong. If it performs almost as well as the scheduler, it may be the product operators actually adopt.

The study must show two separate things, and the design gives each its own evidence:

- **Control:** closed-loop hindcasts of the three floods, out of sample, plus stress tests. These are compared with historical operation, the current rule, a rule that uses no forecast, a scheduler that sees only the 6-h forecast and has no terminal guide, and a full-event perfect-foresight optimum.
- **Acceptance:** schedule-form metrics, a blind comparison by operators, and a shadow-operation trial. Acceptance has to be measured. It cannot be assumed because a schedule "looks smooth".

This recommendation is provisional. Section 9 lists what would change it.

## 2. The deciding questions

Three questions decide the design. They are answered first and in the most depth.

**Q1. The forecast covers 6 h but floods last more than 8 days with up to three peaks. What should the schedule assume about inflow beyond the forecast?**
This is the main technical question. A scheduler that sees only 6 h ahead is myopic. On a rising limb it holds water to keep releases low, because it cannot see that the pool will be needed later. After a peak it is slow to evacuate, because it cannot see a second peak. Provisional answer: carry an explicit beyond-horizon representation (continuation scenarios plus a terminal-storage guide) and test how much it adds against a scheduler without it. Section 3 explains why the critical period is **between** peaks, not within one.

**Q2. What makes a schedule acceptable to operators, and how will the study measure it?**
Provisional answer: acceptance has four parts.
- **Form:** few, round-number changes, no oscillation, and monotone on the rising limb.
- **Stability:** the plan does not swing between successive hourly issues.
- **Explainability:** each release can be tied to a rule-like reason.
- **Institutional fit:** the schedule respects the operating manual, the approval process for releases, and downstream warning lead times.

Each part gets a metric (section 7) and an operator test (section 8).

**Q3. With only three floods, can the evidence separate a better method from luck?**
Provisional answer: not statistically, on the historical events alone. Three events, with about five or six peaks between them, support trajectory-level, mechanism-level comparisons and leave-one-flood-out checks, not probabilities. Exceedance probabilities and robustness claims have to come from forecast-error Monte Carlo runs and from scaled or recombined synthetic floods. The study should say so plainly.

## 3. What the given numbers already imply

All arithmetic uses only the facts given. Symbols stand in for values the study will take from the dam's records.

- **The flood pool is about 6.5 h of 200-year net inflow.** Let V_FC be the flood-control storage. Then V_FC ≈ (Q_in,200 − R) × 6.5 h. So under 200-year conditions, **each hour of delay in raising the release uses about 1/6.5 ≈ 15 % of the pool.** Hourly decisions therefore matter, and a schedule that lags by even 1–2 h can use a third of the pool.
- **Within a single peak the forecast is almost long enough.** The 6-h rainfall forecast, plus the rainfall-to-inflow lag of the upper Geum catchment (to be estimated from the data; call it L h), gives an inflow lead time of about 6 + L h. That is close to or longer than the 6.5-h fill time. Within one rising limb, then, the operator can roughly see the pool filling in time to act. **Across the event the forecast is much too short.** It covers 6/192 ≈ 3 % of an 8-day flood, so whether a second peak will come is unknown at the time the evacuation decision after the first peak has to be made.
- **Whether the pool can be emptied between peaks.** Suppose a fraction f of the pool is used in peak 1, and the net evacuation rate after the peak is k times the 200-year net filling rate. Here k = (R_allowable − Q_in,recession) / (Q_in,200 − R). The time needed to recover the space is f × 6.5 / k h.
  - Example: f = 0.8 and k = 0.25 give about 21 h.
  - If the observed gaps between peaks in 2016, 2018 and 2020 are shorter than this, the pool cannot be fully recovered whatever the method. The value of a method then lies in how much it pre-releases and how early it evacuates, within the downstream limit.
  - The study computes f, k and the inter-peak gaps from the three records as its first quantitative step, because this check sets how much any scheduler can gain.
- **Upper bound on any gain (mass balance).** No schedule can store more than V_FC. It also cannot release more over a period than the downstream-safe rate times the available time. Any claimed reduction in peak release has to fit within V_FC plus the extra early release that the lead time allows: ΔR × (6 + L) h × 3600 s/h. Results that exceed this bound point to a simulator error.
- **Noise in the derived inflow.** Inflow is derived as ΔS/Δt + outflow, so level-measurement error turns directly into inflow noise. A 1-cm level error over each 10 km² of water surface is 10⁵ m³, or about 28 m³/s in an hourly step. The actual surface area at flood levels has to be taken from the elevation–area curve. Hourly derived inflow will therefore be noisy and can go negative. It must be smoothed (for example with a short moving window that conserves volume, or a Kalman-type state estimate) before it is used as "truth" or to fit forecast errors. The smoothing must not shave the peaks.
- **Correlated forecast errors.** Over a 6-h horizon, hourly errors are strongly correlated. For n = 6 hourly errors of equal spread σ and correlation ρ = 0.8 (an illustrative value, to be fitted), the variance of the 6-h forecast volume error is 6σ²(1 + 5 × 0.8) = 30σ². Treating the errors as independent gives 6σ², which understates the standard deviation by a factor of √5 ≈ 2.2. Error scenarios must therefore keep the correlation across lead times.
- **Resolution of the simulations.** With N Monte Carlo forecast-error realisations per event, an exceedance probability p has standard error √(p(1−p)/N). For p = 1 % and N = 1,000 that is about 0.3 %, which is enough to tell 1 % from 2 % but not from 1.3 %. The study uses N ≥ 2,000 per event and scenario and reports these errors.

## 4. Data, system description and baseline

**Assemble and check:**
- hourly storage, level, outflow and derived inflow for the three floods, aligned in time;
- elevation–storage and elevation–area curves;
- outlet and spillway discharge capacity as a function of head, with the gate rating and the number of gates, and the case of one gate out of service;
- turbine capacity;
- characteristic levels: flood-limited level for the flood season, design flood level, maximum flood level and dam crest;
- release ramp limits and the minimum release (in-stream flow);
- the downstream safe discharge, the control points used by the river-flood forecasting office, and the travel time from the dam to each control point.

The brief does not give these; the operator has them. **Assumption to confirm:** downstream tributaries that are not controlled join the Geum below the dam. As far as I know, Yongdam Dam lies upstream on the Geum. If so, part of the inflow is a scheduled upstream release, and that part is known further ahead than 6 h. It should be split from the natural inflow so it is not treated as forecast error.

**Rainfall forecasts.** Collect the 6-h rainfall forecasts that were actually issued during the three events, and the issue times. Check whether archived forecasts exist from the same system and configuration that would be used operationally. If they do not exist, synthetic forecasts must be built (section 5) and the study must say so.

**Executable baseline.** Encode the current operating manual and practice as a simulated rule: release bands by level and inflow, ramping and approval steps. Then check it. Run the rule and a pure water-balance simulator on the observed inflows, and confirm that the simulator reproduces the observed storage trajectory when driven by the observed outflow. A closure error well under 1 % of V_FC is the target.

**Historical operation is context, not a controlled counterfactual.** Record for each event:
- the starting level;
- the forecasts and warnings the operators actually had;
- downstream constraints at the time (high river stages, construction, upstream releases).

If a historical decision was forced by a constraint the model leaves out, comparing against it is unfair. Every candidate is therefore run from the **same initial level** as history, and also from a range of starting levels (the flood-limited level and ±1 m around it, adjusted to the dam's operating range) to show how much the result depends on the start.

**Diagnose the current practice.** For each event and peak, measure:
- how far the release lagged behind the inflow;
- how much pool was used at each peak;
- how much was recovered between peaks;
- whether peak releases came close to the downstream limit.

This tells the study where the gains can be: earlier rising-limb release, faster evacuation between peaks, or smoother schedules.

## 5. Forecasts and uncertainty

1. **Inflow forecast.** Turn the 6-h rainfall forecast into an inflow forecast over 6 + L h with a simple hourly rainfall–runoff model calibrated on the three events. Candidates are a lumped conceptual model or a unit-hydrograph model with state updating from observed inflow. Keep it simple: three events cannot support a complex data-driven model, and any data-driven variant must be tested by leaving one event out. Add the known upstream release as a separate, deterministic component.
2. **Verify by lead time.** Use the 1–6 h archived forecasts, or the hindcast model driven by observed rainfall if no archive exists. Measure, for lead times 1, 2, …, 6 + L h:
   - error mean and spread, separately for rising limbs, peaks and recessions;
   - timing error of the peak;
   - volume error.
   Errors should be expected to grow with lead time and with flow, and to be skewed (larger under-forecasts on rising limbs). The study must not assume one stationary normal error.
3. **Error model.** Fit relative errors by lead time and flow regime. Keep their correlation across lead times with a copula or a lag-correlated model. Generate forecast-error realisations by perturbing the observed inflow with these correlated errors, then check that the generated scenarios match the observed error statistics, coverage and correlation.
4. **Beyond the forecast horizon (the answer to Q1).** Build **continuation scenarios** conditional on the current state, from three sources:
   - **recession:** a fitted recession curve from current inflow;
   - **renewed rise:** inflow shapes taken from the second and third peaks of the historical events, scaled to the current state;
   - **severe continuation:** a design-flood-type second peak, for example the volume of a 100- or 200-year multi-day flood less what has already arrived.

   Give them weights or treat them as a robust set. Three events cannot calibrate probabilities, so robust treatment (meet safety under every scenario) is the conservative default, and the weights are tested as sensitivity.
5. **Non-forecast uncertainty.** Include:
   - level-measurement error, which affects both the derived inflow and the state the scheduler starts from;
   - outlet discharge-coefficient uncertainty at partial gate openings;
   - initial level.

   State that wind set-up and storage-curve error are excluded unless the dam's safety margins are tight enough to need them.

## 6. The schedulers to compare

All schedulers run in the same hourly closed loop. At each hour they read the observed state and the forecast available at that hour, issue a schedule, apply the first hour of it, and then update the state with the **observed** inflow, not the forecast. The schedulers differ only in decision logic and information.

| Code | Scheduler | Information | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | Historical operation | what operators had | context (not a controlled baseline) |
| C | Current rule, encoded | observed state | baseline under identical conditions |
| M | Measurement-based staged rule (no forecast) | observed level, inflow, trend | lower reference; candidate fallback |
| D6 | Rolling scheduler, 6 + L h deterministic forecast, no tail, no terminal guide | forecast only | shows the cost of myopia |
| S | **Proposed** rolling scenario scheduler with continuation scenarios + terminal-storage guide + operator-form constraints | forecast + tail scenarios | main candidate |
| R | Staged rule distilled from S | observed state + forecast rainfall | explainable form / fallback |
| P | Full-event perfect-foresight optimisation | the whole observed event | upper bound, not deployable |
| Pₕ | Perfect-foresight rolling scheduler at horizons 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 h | perfect within horizon | value of horizon length |

**Design of S. Judge the priorities first, then compute.** The priorities are:

1. **Hard constraints:**
   - level ≤ the dam-safety limit (the design flood level, with a margin for level-measurement error);
   - release ≤ outlet capacity at the actual head;
   - |R_t − R_{t−1}| ≤ the ramp limit;
   - release ≥ the in-stream minimum;
   - release ≤ the downstream safe discharge, unless the dam-safety limit would otherwise be breached. In that case a documented emergency branch takes over and does not trade one off quietly against the other.
2. **Primary objective:** minimise the peak release and the hours and volume of release above the downstream safe or warning discharges at the control points, routed with travel time and local inflow.
3. **Secondary objective:** return to the flood-limited level by the end of the event, and protect water supply by not ending far below it, using a refill check against the low-side recession scenario.
4. **Operator-form terms:**
   - penalties on the number and size of release changes;
   - release values limited to the operator's increments (for example, the gate-step discharges);
   - a minimum holding time of 1–3 h between changes, to be set with the operators;
   - a monotone non-decreasing release on the rising limb unless the forecast falls sharply.
5. **Hydropower**, last, only once the first four are met.

Keep safety as constraints. Weight only the benefit and form terms, and report the trade-off curve between peak release and number of changes rather than one weighted score.

**Terminal-storage guide.** This is the key to Q1. Derive offline the free space that must remain at the end of the horizon, as a function of current inflow, inflow trend and time since the last peak. Two routes:
- (a) the space needed to absorb the severe continuation scenario at the downstream-safe release;
- (b) a fit to the storage trajectories of the perfect-foresight optimum P on the training floods.

Route (a) is conservative and explainable; route (b) is data-hungry. Use (a) as the default and (b) only as a check.

**Pre-release limits.** A pre-release on forecast rainfall is capped by two tests:
- the reservoir can still refill to the flood-limited level under the low-side forecast;
- the pre-release does not raise downstream flow above what the natural flood would have produced at the control points.

**Fallback and abort rules.** These are written into S, not added afterwards. Switch to rule M, or hold the current release, when:
- the forecast is missing or late by more than one cycle;
- the observed inflow departs from the forecast by more than a threshold (for example, outside the 90 % band for two consecutive hours);
- equipment or gates are out of service.

Under-forecasts are corrected by stepping the release up over a few hours rather than with one jump, unless the dam-safety margin needs it.

**Computation.** Each solve has 24–48 hourly steps × a few scenarios and is a small linear or quadratic program. It solves in seconds, so no "optimisation tool" expertise is needed in the control room. Operators see only the schedule and the reason codes.

**Distilling R.** Fit a shallow decision tree or a short threshold table to S's first-step decisions on training data, with stage switches on level, inflow and its trend, and the 6-h forecast rainfall. Check how often R's release departs from S's by more than one release increment, and whether R alone gives similar control. If it does, R is the simpler product to adopt and S becomes the planning aid behind it.

**Number of tuned parameters.** Keep S and R to about 3–5 tuned parameters in total: the scenario weights or robustness level, the change penalty, the holding time, and the terminal-guide margin. Tune them on two floods and test on the third, rotating through all three, so that the three events are not overfitted.

## 7. Evaluation of control

**Closed-loop hindcasts.** Run all schedulers on the three floods from the same starting levels, with the observed inflow as truth. For S, D6 and R:
- run with the archived or synthetic 6-h forecasts;
- run with N ≥ 2,000 correlated forecast-error realisations;
- run under each tail weighting.

**Metrics for each event and each peak:**
- **Dam side:** maximum level and its margin to the safety limit; peak use of the flood pool (fraction of V_FC).
- **Downstream:** peak release; peak flow at each control point after routing with local inflow; hours and volume above the safe and warning discharges.
- **Between peaks:** pool recovered before the next peak (fraction).
- **End of event:** final level relative to the flood-limited level; water foregone or stored.
- **Monte Carlo:** probability of exceeding each threshold, with its standard error.
- **Gaps:** the gap to P (how much of the attainable gain each scheduler captures) and the Pₕ curve. If Pₕ stops improving beyond about 6–12 h, the 6-h forecast is not the binding limit and the effort belongs in the rule logic. If Pₕ keeps improving to 24–48 h, the tail representation and longer forecasts are where the value lies.

**Trajectories, not only totals.** Plot release, level and inflow for each scheduler on each event. Explain each difference by its mechanism: earlier release on the rising limb, faster evacuation after a peak, avoided overshoot, or fewer reversals. With so few events, the mechanism is the main evidence.

**Out-of-sample test.** Leave one flood out for every tuned element: the rainfall–runoff model, the error model, the scenario weights and the distilled rule.

**Stress tests:**
- scale each flood to 100- and 200-year volumes, keeping the hydrograph shape;
- shift the second peak earlier, closing the gap between peaks;
- recombine peaks from different events into new multi-peak sequences;
- start from higher initial levels;
- inject a forecast failure: a large under-forecast during a rising limb, and a false alarm before a peak that does not come;
- take one gate out of service;
- vary local inflow downstream so that it arrives before, with, and after the dam release at the control point.

The schedule must stay safe in all of these, and the study reports where each scheduler breaks down.

**Acceptance criteria for control, agreed in advance with the operator.** For example:
- no case where S exceeds the dam-safety limit in any test;
- in the leave-one-out runs, S does no worse than C on peak downstream flow and hours above the safe discharge on every peak (not only on average);
- S captures a stated share of the C-to-P gap.

## 8. Evaluation of acceptance

1. **Form and stability metrics,** computed for H, C, S and R:
   - release changes per day;
   - median and maximum change size;
   - up-down reversals per event;
   - share of releases on the operator's standard increments;
   - **plan churn:** how much the release planned for hour t+k changes between successive hourly issues, for k = 1…6. Operators lose trust in a schedule that keeps rewriting its next hours;
   - lead time between a planned increase first appearing in the schedule and its execution, since this is the warning time downstream gets;
   - distance from the historical operator release, where historical operation was not constrained.
2. **Interviews and elicitation before design.** Hold structured sessions with dam operators and the people who approve releases. Record the rules they actually follow: increments, holding times, approval and notification lead times, what they consider unacceptable (for example, raising releases at night without warning, or reversals), and how they currently use the 6-h forecast. These become constraints in S, not after-the-fact checks.
3. **Blind comparison.** Show operators the schedules from H, C, S and R for anonymised event segments, as they would see them hour by hour. Ask them to rate acceptability and say which they would issue, and record their reasons. Look for systematic objections.
4. **Shadow operation.** Run S and R in real time beside current practice through at least one flood season. Operators see the suggested schedule, and decisions stay under the current rule. Log every hour: the suggestion, the actual release, any disagreement and its reason, and forecast availability. This is the strongest acceptance evidence available without changing operation.
5. **Adoption pathway.** As far as I know, Daecheong is operated by K-water. During floods, dam releases are coordinated with, and approved by, the Geum River Flood Control Office under the Ministry of Environment. The legal basis is the dam's operating rules and the annual flood-season operating plan. Downstream warnings go through the flood control office and local governments. These institutional roles should be confirmed with the operator. The study should:
   - agree the control and acceptance criteria in advance with the approving body;
   - keep the current manual as the legal basis during shadow operation;
   - specify the reversion triggers (forecast missing, late or diverging; equipment failure), who decides to revert, and what is logged for audit;
   - set out training, version control of the scheduler and its parameters, and a review after each flood;
   - define how downstream users and emergency services are warned of man-made release increases, using the schedule's look-ahead to give earlier notice.

## 9. Key assumptions and what would change the recommendation

| Assumption or open fact | If it turns out otherwise |
|---|---|
| Inter-peak gaps in the three floods are long enough to recover a meaningful share of the pool at the downstream-safe release (section 3) | If recovery is impossible, the gain comes almost entirely from rising-limb pre-release and peak shaving. The terminal guide matters less, and the study should focus on pre-release limits and refill risk |
| Archived 6-h rainfall forecasts exist for the three events | If not, all forecast-based results rest on synthetic errors and must be labelled so. Shadow operation becomes the main real-forecast test |
| The downstream safe discharge and control-point travel times are known and stable | If local tributary inflow dominates the control-point peak, the dam's leverage is smaller. The study should report the residual risk the dam cannot control |
| The Pₕ curve keeps improving up to 24–48 h | Confirms that the beyond-horizon treatment is the core contribution. If it flattens at about 6 h, a well-tuned staged rule (R or M) may match S, and the simpler rule should be recommended |
| Operators accept a computed schedule if it is rule-shaped and explained | If the blind tests show they will issue only rule-table decisions, deliver R as the product and keep S as the offline tool that designs and updates R |
| The upstream reservoir's releases are known ahead | If they are not shared in real time, they add to the inflow uncertainty and the case for coordinating with that operator becomes part of the study |

**What would confirm the recommendation:**
- S beats C and D6 on every held-out peak without breaching any safety limit, and captures most of the gap to P;
- R reproduces most of S's benefit;
- operators rate S and R at least as acceptable as their own historical schedules;
- shadow operation shows low disagreement and low plan churn.

**What would overturn it:**
- S's advantage disappears out of sample;
- S's advantage depends on the tail weights;
- S needs schedule shapes operators reject, even with the form constraints.

In that case the recommendation falls back to an improved staged rule (M or R) with a terminal-space guide, which is simpler to approve and use.

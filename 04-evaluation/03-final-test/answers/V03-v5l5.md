# Real-Time Flood Release Decisions at Lishimen Reservoir: Plan and Provisional Recommendation

## 1. Recommendation in brief

1. **Release rule.** Re-decide the release every hour with a short rolling horizon of about 3 to 12 h. The exact length should match the basin response time that the data show. In each hour, carry out only the first decision.
   - Treat dam safety as a hard constraint. Treat the 1000 m³/s downstream limit as a chance constraint. Pursue storage use only inside the space those two constraints leave.
   - Drive the rule with a **conservative upper quantile** of a short-range inflow forecast built from the six rainfall gauges and observed inflow. Do not drive it with the forecast mean.
   - Pair the rule with a **measurement-based fallback rule**. The fallback uses only current level and current inflow. It doubles as the baseline, and the operator reverts to it whenever the forecast is missing or wrong.
2. **Stage logic.** The plan has four parts:
   - **Rising limb:** raise releases early toward 1000 m³/s, because the ramping limit makes a late response expensive.
   - **Peak:** hold releases at or below 1000 m³/s while the projected upper-quantile peak level stays below 180.69 m.
   - **Switch:** move to the dam-safety branch (releases above 1000 m³/s, as early and as low as possible) once the projection crosses that level.
   - **Recession:** empty the flood-control zone back to 176.04 m as fast as the 1000 m³/s limit allows, ready for a following typhoon flood.
3. **Forecast error.** Measure how forecast error degrades outcomes by running the same policy under five information conditions on the same events:
   - no forecast (the fallback rule);
   - the realistic rainfall-driven forecast;
   - synthetic forecasts with increasing error;
   - error that is serially correlated and grows with flow;
   - perfect foresight.

   The output is a set of degradation curves and a **break-even forecast accuracy**: the error level beyond which the forecast-based plan is no better than the fallback.
4. **Choosing one plan.**
   - Screen every candidate against the dam-safety standard, using the upper confidence bound of its risk.
   - Keep the noninferior set on downstream exceedance and storage use.
   - Pick from that set with a stated and tested preference.
   - Validate on events held out from tuning, including the 2004 Rananim flood.
   - The final choice is the agency's and the approving authority's, not the model's.

This is a provisional recommendation. It rests on assumptions about the outlet capacity, the ramping limit, the basin response time and the meaning of the two flood-capacity figures, listed in Section 9. Section 9 also says which findings would change it.

## 2. The deciding questions, with first numbers

Three questions decide what kind of policy is defensible. Each is answered first with order-of-magnitude arithmetic from the brief.

### Q1. How much flood storage is there, compared with a 100-year typhoon flood? Can a 100-year flood be held to 1000 m³/s at all?

- **Storage per metre.** The flood-control capacity is either 31.4 or 35.7 million m³ (the sources differ). Spread over the 4.65 m between 176.04 m and 180.69 m, that is about 6.8 to 7.7 million m³ per metre of rise.
- **What the two capacity figures probably mean.** They differ by 4.3 million m³. At about 6.8 million m³/m, 4.3 million m³ is roughly 0.63 m of level. That is almost exactly the 0.65 m between the upper flood-control level (180.69 m) and the design flood level (181.34 m).
  - A plausible reading: 31.4 million m³ is the space from the flood-limit level to the upper flood-control level, and 35.7 million m³ is the space to the design flood level.
  - This is a hypothesis. Compute V(180.69) − V(176.04) and V(181.34) − V(176.04) directly from the level-storage curve and use those values. The working difference between the two readings, about 0.6 m, is as large as the whole margin between the two safety levels, so it cannot be left unresolved.
- **Storage as runoff depth.** Over 296 km², 1 mm of runoff is 0.296 million m³. The flood-control capacity therefore absorbs only about 106 to 121 mm of runoff beyond what is released.
- **The release limit as runoff depth.** A release of 1000 m³/s removes 3.6 million m³ per hour, which is about 12.2 mm of runoff per hour.
- **What one hour of peak rain means.** The test event's maximum basin-average rainfall was 30.8 mm in one hour. If it all ran off, that is about 9.1 million m³, or an average flow of about 2530 m³/s. This matches the largest recorded hourly inflow (2703 m³/s) in order of magnitude: the basin passes intense rain to the dam quickly and with little attenuation.
  - One such hour, stored, is about 25 to 29% of the flood-control capacity and about 1.2 to 1.3 m of level rise.
  - One such hour equals about 2.5 h of release at 1000 m³/s.
- **What decides feasibility.** Total event volume does not decide whether 1000 m³/s can hold. Over 48 h, a release of 1000 m³/s removes about 173 million m³ (about 584 mm). What decides it is how much runoff arrives above about 12 mm/h in the peak hours. Two illustrative cases (the actual hydrograph is not given in the brief):
  - 6 h averaging 25 mm/h of runoff gives about 77 mm above the release rate. That is about 23 million m³, which fits within the capacity.
  - 10 h at the same intensity gives about 128 mm, or about 38 million m³, which exceeds either capacity figure.
- **Provisional conclusion.** For a flood near the 100-year size, holding releases at or below 1000 m³/s while staying below 180.69 m may be physically impossible. This is consistent with the design itself:
  - The upper flood-control level is the 20-year level. In the usual Chinese design logic, floods above the downstream protection standard are released by outlet capacity to protect the dam, and the level is allowed up to the design flood level.
  - For the test event, the two risks cannot both be made small. The real decisions are **when** to give up the downstream limit, and **how to spread** the excess release so the downstream peak is as low as possible.
- **First check in the study.** Route the test event and every large historical event under perfect foresight. Find the minimum achievable peak release that keeps the level at or below 180.69 m, and the minimum achievable peak level that keeps releases at or below 1000 m³/s. If perfect foresight cannot meet both, no forecast-based policy can, and the trade-off has to be presented as a choice.

### Q2. How much does the ramping limit and the outlet capacity at the current level cost if the response waits for observed inflow?

- **Values not given.** The brief gives neither the ramping limit nor the discharge curve. As a planning assumption only, take a ramping limit of 200 m³/s per hour.
- **Cost of ramping from a standing start.** Going from 20.4 m³/s (generation flow) to 1000 m³/s then takes about 5 h.
  - If inflow jumps to about 1000 m³/s at the start of that window, the water stored while the release catches up is about 0.5 × 980 m³/s × 5 h × 3600 s/h ≈ 8.8 million m³.
  - That is about a quarter of the flood-control capacity, spent before the peak arrives.
  - With a 100 m³/s/h limit, the catch-up would take about 10 h and store about 17.6 million m³, about half the capacity.
- **Outlet capacity at the flood-limit level.** The level-discharge curve may show that outlet capacity at 176.04 m is below 1000 m³/s. If so, the reservoir cannot release 1000 m³/s until it has risen, and some early storage is forced.
- **Why this matters for forecasts.** Ramping and capacity at low head are where a forecast of only a few hours has most of its value: it lets releases start rising before the inflow arrives. Both values must be confirmed before any policy is designed.

### Q3. What lead time and accuracy can a forecast from the six gauges realistically give, and how does its error behave in large floods?

- **Expected lead time.** No rainfall forecast is operational, so useful lead time is roughly the time from rain falling over the basin to the inflow it produces at the dam. For a steep 296 km² basin with a 35 km main stream, I expect a few hours, perhaps 2 to 6 h. This is an assumption; measure it from the 22 events as the lag between rainfall centroid and inflow peak.
- **Why error correlation matters.** Errors in such forecasts are typically serially correlated, and that matters more than their size per hour. Illustration, assuming a relative error of 20% at an inflow of 2000 m³/s (σ = 400 m³/s per hour):
  - The error in the 6-h forecast volume has a standard deviation of √6 × 400 × 3600 ≈ 3.5 million m³ if hourly errors are independent.
  - With a lag correlation of ρ = 0.8, the variance becomes 6σ²(1 + 5 × 0.8) = 30σ², and the standard deviation becomes √30 × 400 × 3600 ≈ 7.9 million m³.
  - That is about 1.1 m of level and about a quarter of the flood-control capacity.
- **Implication.** Treating errors as independent would understate volume uncertainty by a factor of about 2.2 and make the policy look safer than it is. The error model must keep the correlation, and the policy must keep a margin of that order.

## 3. Problem setup and baseline

### Data assembly and checks
- **Align records.** Put hourly rainfall from the six gauges and hourly inflow for the 22 events on a common time base. Compute basin-average rainfall by Thiessen weights or a similar method.
- **Treat inflow as derived.** Observed inflow at a reservoir is usually back-calculated from level change and outflow, so it is noisy at an hourly step. Check for negative or oscillating values, and smooth consistently with the level-storage curve. Record how this was done, because the same series will later be the "truth" for scoring forecasts.
- **Evaporation.** Sparse evaporation records do not matter within a 48-h typhoon flood. Neglect losses during events and state this.
- **Facility description.** From the curves, tabulate:
  - storage at 176.04, 180.69 and 181.34 m;
  - outlet capacity at each level, by gate if possible;
  - the ramping limit and its source (dam-slope or downstream bank safety, gate mechanics, or regulation);
  - minimum release (0 or 20.4 m³/s; the brief allows either, so ask which applies during floods).
  - Then resolve the 31.4 versus 35.7 million m³ question (Q1).
- **Downstream control point.** The brief states the 1000 m³/s limit at the dam. Ask the agency whether it applies to dam release alone or to flow at a downstream section that also receives local inflow. If a downstream point governs, add the travel time and local inflow. Local inflow can arrive before, with, or after the release, and the policy must hold in each case. Until that is answered, apply the limit to dam release.

### Baseline (executable comparator)
- **Ask for the actual rule.** Obtain Lishimen's current flood dispatch rule from the agency.
- **Interim encoding if it is unavailable.** Encode the usual conventional form and label it as presumed:
  - Pass inflow while inflow is at or below 1000 m³/s.
  - Hold releases at 1000 m³/s while the level is below 180.69 m.
  - Release by outlet capacity (up to inflow) once 180.69 m is reached.
  - Return to 176.04 m in the recession.
  - Apply the release bounds and the ramping limit throughout.
- **Same conditions for every comparison.** The baseline needs no forecast. Run it on exactly the same events, initial level (176.04 m) and constraints as every candidate.
- **Historical operation is context only.** Historical releases for the 2004 event are not a fair comparator, because the actual starting level and the information operators had may differ. Record them, but do not use them as the baseline.

### Event set and initial state
- **Initial level.** Start every event at 176.04 m, as the brief specifies. As a sensitivity test, also start at levels drawn from what the record shows at flood onset.
- **Splitting the 22 events.**
  - Hold the 2004 test event out of all forecast training and policy tuning.
  - Use leave-one-event-out validation for the rest.
  - Classify events by magnitude. Of 22 events spanning roughly 5-year to 100-year floods, probably only a handful exceed the 20-year standard. Results for large floods rest on those few events and must be reported event by event, not only as averages.

## 4. Forecast and uncertainty

### Building the short-range inflow forecast
- **Inputs.** Recent hourly rainfall at the six gauges and their basin average, observed inflow, and antecedent rainfall.
- **Lead times.** Forecast at 1 to N hours, where N is set by the measured response time.
- **Model choice.** With only 22 events, a data-driven model is at real risk of overfitting, especially for the largest floods, which are the ones that matter. Fit a simple conceptual or unit-hydrograph model as the reference. Add a data-driven model only if it beats the reference out of sample on the large events.
- **Fit check.** Score each model by lead time with peak error, peak-timing error, volume error and NSE, all computed on held-out events. Report the large events (above about 20-year) separately.

### Error model
- **Error definition.** Use relative error, e = (forecast − observed)/observed, at each lead time. Keep this sign convention throughout.
- **Structure to represent:**
  - spread growing with lead time;
  - spread growing with flow (the brief says errors are largest for big floods), for example σ(lead, Q);
  - bias, which in rising limbs is typically underforecasting of peaks;
  - correlation across lead times and successive issue times.
- **How to fit it.** Use an AR(1) process on the relative errors, or a copula across lead times with fitted marginals.
- **Check before use.** Confirm coverage of the 90% band on held-out events, check rank or PIT histograms by flow class, and confirm that the largest events fall inside the generated range.
- **If data are too few.** If the large-flood sample is too small to fit these terms, derive σ from the accuracy actually achieved, for example the share of peaks within ±20%, and state that this is an assumption.

### Synthetic forecast scenarios for the degradation study
- **How to generate them.** Superimpose generated error series on the observed hydrographs: forecast at time t for t+k = Q(t+k) × (1 + e(t,k)).
- **Error grid.** Combine σ at 1-h lead in {0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40%}, growth with lead time, lag correlation ρ in {0, 0.5, 0.8}, and an underforecast bias in {0, −10%}.
- **Number of realizations.** About 2000 per event and setting. To estimate a 5% exceedance probability with a standard error near 0.5 percentage points, n ≈ p(1 − p)/se² = 0.0475/0.000025 ≈ 1900. Probabilities near 1% need about 4000 realizations for the same relative precision.
- **Scaled floods.** Scale the largest historical events, keeping their shape, up to the 100-year design flood. This tests the dam-safety branch beyond the observed range and does not rely on only one or two real large floods.

### Other uncertainties
- **Outlet capacity.** The discharge coefficient (a few percent), and the possibility of one gate unavailable.
- **Level measurement error.** This matters because the level rises about 0.13 to 0.15 m per million m³. Level-measurement error appears as error in the back-calculated inflow.
- **Initial level.** Covered by the sensitivity test in Section 3.
- **Scope.** Include gate unavailability and capacity error as stress tests. State that wind set-up and wave run-up are outside this study unless the dam-safety authority requires them.

## 5. Release policy design

### Safety and risk definitions (settled before computing)
For each event and plan, under the error ensemble, compute four measures:
- **Dam-side risk:** P(Zmax > 180.69 m), and also P(Zmax > 181.34 m). The 181.34 m limit is a hard limit and must not be crossed in any realization of floods up to the design flood.
- **Downstream risk:** P(max release > 1000 m³/s), plus the severity of exceedance: peak release, hours above 1000 m³/s, and volume above 1000 m³/s. Probability alone hides the difference between 1050 m³/s for one hour and 2000 m³/s for six hours.
- **Tail of the level outcome:** mean Zmax in the worst 5% of realizations (CVaR), because the agency will care about how bad the bad cases are.
- **Storage use**, made concrete in two directions:
  - **Idle storage:** flood-control storage still unused at the hour of peak release, counted only in realizations where release exceeded 1000 m³/s. This storage could have reduced the downstream peak.
  - **Premature filling:** storage occupied before the inflow peak beyond what the perfect-foresight plan occupies at the same hour. This storage was spent too early and was not available for later inflow.
  - Also report time to return to 176.04 m after the event, and water lost below 176.04 m by any pre-release.

All of these are risks **conditional on the event**, given forecast error. They are not annual probabilities, and they must not be compared with the 20-year and 100-year design frequencies without that conversion.

### Acceptable-risk benchmark
- **Dam side.** The candidate must not be worse than the baseline rule, judged on the upper bound of the estimate. It must never exceed 181.34 m for floods up to the design flood. For floods within the 20-year standard, it should essentially never exceed 180.69 m.
- **Downstream.** For floods within the 20-year standard, releases above 1000 m³/s should be essentially zero. That is what the standard promises.
- **Above the standard.** The benchmark is a lower peak release and less exceedance volume than the baseline, with the dam side unchanged.
- **Who sets the numbers.** The agency and the dam-safety authority must confirm these benchmarks. The brief does not supply numerical tolerances, so the study should present results for a range of tolerances rather than choose one silently.

### Physical asymmetry the policy should exploit
- **Releasing up to 1000 m³/s from the flood-control zone is almost free.** Water stored above 176.04 m must be released after the flood anyway. While the level is above 176.04 m, releasing up to 1000 m³/s costs downstream nothing and costs no conservation water.
- **The real costs are:**
  - **Pre-release below 176.04 m.** Releasing 1000 m³/s against 300 m³/s inflow draws down about 2.5 million m³ per hour. A false alarm lasting 6 h loses about 15 million m³, about 12% of the initial storage of 121.8 million m³, taken from irrigation and supply. Cap pre-release at the volume that a low-side inflow scenario would refill by the end of the event, and never raise downstream flow above what the natural flood would have produced.
  - **Release above 1000 m³/s.** This is a man-made downstream flood and is allowed only when the dam-side projection requires it.

### Stage logic (executed hourly)
1. **Watch.** Trigger on basin rainfall intensity or forecast inflow crossing thresholds, both calibrated on the 22 events. When triggered, start ramping toward the release needed to keep the projected upper-quantile level at 176.04 m. Use pre-release below 176.04 m only within the refill cap above.
2. **Rising limb and detention.** While the projected upper-quantile peak level, with releases held at 1000 m³/s, stays below 180.69 m minus a margin:
   - release min(1000, outlet capacity, what the ramping limit allows);
   - otherwise follow inflow so the reservoir is not filled early.

   The margin is set from the error model, at roughly one standard deviation of the forecast volume error over the horizon, which is about 1 m by the Q3 arithmetic.
3. **Switch to dam protection.** If the upper-quantile projection exceeds 180.69 m even at 1000 m³/s, raise releases above 1000 m³/s **now**, by the smallest amount that brings the projection back to the limit. Spreading the excess over more hours lowers the downstream peak compared with waiting until the level is reached and then releasing by full capacity.
   - **Point of no return:** the hour after which the remaining space to 180.69 m is smaller than the forecast excess volume that the ramping limit still allows to be released. Past it, a large release becomes unavoidable.
   - Issue downstream warnings when the projection first approaches the switch, not when the switch happens.
4. **Peak and recession.** After the observed peak, keep releasing at up to 1000 m³/s until the level is back at 176.04 m. If the 1000 m³/s limit allows, this should take about 31 to 36 million m³ ÷ 3.6 million m³ per hour, roughly 9 to 10 h after inflow drops well below release. Remaining storage must not be kept as water: typhoon floods can follow each other closely.
5. **Correction and fallback.** If observed inflow exceeds the forecast upper band for two consecutive hours, recompute at once with the latest data. Correct gradually where the ramping limit binds. If forecast inputs fail, for example two or more gauges are missing or the forecast is late, revert to the measurement-based rule.

### Model and solution method
- **System representation.** A single reservoir at an hourly step:
  - water balance S(t+1) = S(t) + [I(t) − R(t)] × 3600 s;
  - the level-storage curve;
  - outlet capacity as a function of level;
  - release bounds and the ramping limit.

  This is a small problem: one storage state and a horizon of a few hours.
- **Candidate policy families, from simple to complex:**
  - **(a) Baseline.** The current or presumed rule described in Section 3.
  - **(b) Parametric forecast-informed rule.** The stage logic above, with a few parameters: trigger thresholds, the forecast quantile used, the margin, and the pre-release cap. Calibrate it by grid or evolutionary search on the training events.
  - **(c) Scenario-based rolling optimization.** Over a few dozen forecast scenarios, choose the release sequence that minimizes downstream exceedance and premature filling, subject to dam-side constraints in all or nearly all scenarios. The first-hour release is shared across scenarios, later hours may differ by scenario, and only the first hour is carried out. Dynamic programming or a small nonlinear program solves this within seconds.
  - **(d) Perfect-foresight optimization.** The upper bound, which cannot be deployed.
- **Which to prefer.** If (b) closes most of the gap between (a) and (d), prefer (b). It is transparent, easier to approve and easier to operate by hand when systems fail. Adopt (c) only if it gives a clear additional gain on held-out events.

## 6. How much forecast error degrades the outcomes

Run policies (a) to (d) on the same events, initial state and constraints, under each information condition.

1. **No forecast.** The measurement-based fallback.
2. **Realistic forecast.** The rainfall-driven forecast actually built, evaluated out of sample.
3. **Synthetic forecasts.** Each setting on the error grid in Section 4.
4. **Perfect foresight.**

**Outputs:**
- **Degradation curves.** For each event class (within 20-year, above 20-year), plot dam-side risk, downstream exceedance probability and severity, and the storage-use measures against forecast error σ, with one curve per error correlation ρ.
- **Share of forecast value achieved.** For each outcome, the fraction of the no-forecast-to-perfect gap that the realistic forecast recovers.
- **Break-even accuracy.** The error level at which the forecast-informed policy stops beating the fallback, and the stricter level needed to meet each dam-side tolerance. This tells the agency what forecast accuracy to require before it relies on forecasts. If the realistic forecast lies beyond break-even for large floods, the recommendation becomes "use the measurement-based rule, improved by early ramping triggered by observed rainfall".
- **Explanation of each result.** Attribute each effect to a mechanism, using event trajectories and not only averages:
  - Underforecast leads to late ramping, premature filling and forced late exceedance.
  - Overforecast leads to unnecessary pre-release (water loss) or unnecessary releases above 1000 m³/s (man-made flooding).
- **Expected shape.** The gain from forecasts will probably come mostly from the rising limb (ramp anticipation) and the switch timing. It will probably shrink fastest with correlated underforecast bias in large floods. The study must confirm this or overturn it.

## 7. Choosing one operating plan

1. **Safety screen first.** Drop any candidate that does either of the following on any held-out event or scaled design flood:
   - exceeds 181.34 m in any realization;
   - has a 95% upper bound of P(Zmax > 180.69 m) above the baseline's (or above the tolerance the authority sets).
   This screen is not offset by any benefit.
2. **Noninferior set.** Among survivors, keep those not dominated on:
   - downstream exceedance (probability, peak, volume);
   - storage use (idle storage, premature filling, return time);
   - water lost by pre-release.
3. **Side-by-side table.** Tabulate each surviving option on each measure, with Monte Carlo confidence intervals and the baseline row. State which options are dominated.
4. **Stated preference.** Choose with a preference rule the agency states and can defend. A simple lexicographic rule is easier to approve and to explain after a flood than a weighted index:
   - first minimize downstream exceedance volume within the dam-side constraint;
   - then minimize premature filling;
   - then minimize water loss.

   If a weighted method is used, report how the ranking changes when weights shift by ±20% and when the ranking method changes.
5. **Robustness.** Confirm the choice holds:
   - across the held-out events, including the 2004 test event;
   - across starting levels;
   - with one gate out of service;
   - across the forecast-error settings that bracket the realistic forecast.

   A plan that wins only under the realistic error estimate but loses if error is 50% larger should give way to a more robust one.
6. **Can the data resolve the differences?** With 22 events and probably only a few above the 20-year standard, differences between candidates on large-flood performance will rest on scaled and synthetic events. Report:
   - which differences exceed their Monte Carlo standard errors;
   - which differences hold consistently across the few real large events;
   - which differences fall inside the noise.

   Do not claim a ranking that the evidence cannot support.

## 8. Rananim test event (20040812): what to do with it

- **Hold it out.** Keep it out of all training and tuning.
- **Runs.** From 176.04 m, run the baseline, the selected plan, the other noninferior candidates and perfect foresight, each under about 2000 error realizations at the realistic error level and at bracketing error levels.
- **What to report:**
  - hourly level and release trajectories (median and 5 to 95% band);
  - Zmax distribution against 180.69 m and 181.34 m;
  - peak release and hours and volume above 1000 m³/s;
  - when the switch to the dam-protection branch occurred, and how many hours of warning it gave downstream.
- **Feasibility first.** First establish, by perfect-foresight routing, whether the event could have been passed with releases at or below 1000 m³/s and the level at or below 180.69 m. The arithmetic in Q1 suggests probably not for a near-100-year flood. If not, the test measures how well each plan spreads the unavoidable excess and protects the dam.

## 9. Assumptions and what would change the recommendation

| Assumption | Why it matters | What would change it |
|---|---|---|
| 31.4 million m³ is the space to 180.69 m and 35.7 million m³ the space to 181.34 m | Sets the dam-side margin, which is worth about 0.6 m | Level-storage curve shows otherwise: recompute every margin |
| Ramping limit of the order of 100 to 200 m³/s per hour | Decides how much a few hours of forecast are worth | A loose ramping limit shrinks forecast value, and a simple measurement rule may suffice |
| Outlet capacity at 176 m may be below 1000 m³/s | Forced early storage | If capacity is ample, the early-ramp benefit is smaller |
| Basin response of a few hours | Sets the useful forecast lead time | A longer response time favours forecast-informed rules; a very short one favours the fallback rule |
| The 1000 m³/s limit applies to dam release | Whether downstream routing and local inflow must be modelled | A downstream control point with local inflow tightens the release limit and requires routing |
| Current rule is the conventional form presumed in Section 3 | Sets the baseline | The agency's actual rule replaces it |
| Risk tolerances to be set by the authority | Decides the safety screen | Tighter tolerances push toward more conservative quantiles and earlier switching |

**What would confirm the recommendation:**
- The realistic forecast recovers a clear share of the perfect-foresight gain on held-out large events.
- It stays ahead of the fallback when forecast error is increased moderately.

**What would overturn it:**
- The realistic forecast's large-flood error lies beyond break-even.
- Correlated underforecast bias removes the gain.

In either case, adopt the measurement-based rule with rainfall-triggered early ramping, and treat forecast development as a separate, later project.

## 10. Adoption

- **Approval.**
  - The operating plan changes the reservoir's flood dispatch rules. In China, flood dispatch of a reservoir of this size is carried out under the annual flood-season operation plan and the reservoir's dispatch regulations.
  - These are approved by the water administrative department with jurisdiction and executed under the flood control and drought relief headquarters with jurisdiction. The relevant levels are county, municipal or provincial, depending on how Lishimen is classified.
  - Until a revised rule is approved, the current rule remains the legal basis for every release.
- **Trial.**
  - Run the new plan in **shadow mode** beside the current rule for at least one flood season. Log the release the plan would have recommended next to the release actually made each hour.
  - Agree acceptance criteria with the safety authority before the season. Examples:
    - no recommendation that would have breached 180.69 m within the 20-year standard;
    - forecast band coverage close to nominal on observed floods;
    - recommended switches to the dam-protection branch that are no later than the current rule's.
- **Reversion triggers.** Return to the current or fallback rule when any of the following occurs:
  - the forecast is missing or late;
  - two or more rain gauges fail;
  - observed inflow is outside the forecast band for two consecutive hours;
  - a gate fault or loss of power occurs;
  - the level gauge fails.

  The duty dispatcher decides and logs the reason. The log keeps inputs, forecasts, recommended and actual releases, and overrides, for review after each event.
- **Warnings.**
  - Downstream communities, emergency services and the county or municipal flood headquarters must be warned before any planned release above 1000 m³/s. The new plan should advance that warning, because the switch is decided on a projection.
  - Large ramp-ups below 1000 m³/s also need notice to downstream river users.
- **Training and control.** Operators need training on the stage logic and the fallback rule. Each rule change needs version control and an after-event review, which feeds back into recalibration as new floods are recorded.

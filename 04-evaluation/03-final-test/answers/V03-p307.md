# Lishimen Reservoir: Hourly Flood Releases Under Inflow Uncertainty — Study Design

## 1. How I read the problem

The question has two parts and one deliverable:

1. **Operating question.** How should hourly releases be set during a typhoon flood when future inflow is unknown? On a small, steep catchment, the useful warning comes mostly from rain that has already fallen and has not yet reached the reservoir. Any forecast beyond that window depends on rainfall forecasts that do not exist yet or are very uncertain.
2. **Sensitivity question.** How much do forecast errors (size, bias, timing, lead time) reduce flood safety? Upstream safety means reservoir level and dam safety. Downstream safety means peak release and how long the channel's safe discharge is exceeded. Errors can push the two in opposite directions.
3. **Deliverable.** One operating plan the agency can defend. It has to be simple enough to run hour by hour in a control room, it has to stay safe when the forecast is wrong, and its rules have to be traceable to the evidence.

So the study is not "find the best optimizer." It is "find a release policy whose performance degrades gracefully as forecast error grows, and show the evidence." A forecast-driven optimal policy that looks best with perfect information but fails badly under realistic errors is the wrong answer for this agency.

## 2. Assumptions (stated, not invented)

The brief does not give the following. I assume the agency can supply them. If it cannot, they become the first data gaps to close.

- **Reservoir physical data:** the level–storage curve, the spillway/gate rating (discharge capacity as a function of level and gate opening), outlet and power-tunnel capacities, and gate operating limits (how fast and how often gates can change).
- **Regulatory levels and limits:** the flood-limit (pre-flood) level, the flood-control high level, the design and check flood levels, the dam crest, and any upstream inundation or land-acquisition level. Downstream, the safe discharge of Shifeng Creek at the control point(s), and whether there is a significant uncontrolled area between the dam and the downstream town.
- **Existing regulation procedure:** the current flood operating rules (staged releases keyed to reservoir level and/or inflow). These are the baseline.
- **The 22 observed "inflows" are probably back-calculated** from the reservoir water balance (storage change plus outflow). If so, they are noisy at hourly scale, especially at low flows and near gate changes. I will treat them as observations with error, not as truth.
- **Records of actual releases and levels during Rananim (2004)** may or may not exist. If they do, they allow a comparison with what was actually done. If not, the baseline is the simulated existing rule.
- **No operational quantitative precipitation forecast (QPF) is assumed.** Rainfall forecast skill enters only as a scenario.
- **Design-flood hydrographs** (for example 20-, 50-, 100-year and check floods) from the reservoir's design documents are assumed obtainable for stress testing. Otherwise I scale observed events. That is a weaker substitute, and I would label it as one.

## 3. Study design

### Step 1 — Data audit and event library

- Check the six-gauge hourly rainfall for 1987–2020: gaps, gauge changes, double counting. Compute catchment-average rainfall (Thiessen or inverse-distance weights) and check how sensitive it is to the weighting choice.
- For each of the 22 events: pair rainfall with inflow, check the event water balance (runoff coefficient within a physically plausible range), and smooth or flag back-calculation noise in the inflows.
- Characterize the events: peak, volume, time to peak, rise rate, and the lag between rainfall centroid and inflow peak. **The lag sets the "free" forecast lead time that observed rainfall gives.** It is the single most important number in the study, because it tells us how much forecasting can help at all.
- **Hold out Rananim (2004)** from all calibration and tuning. It is the independent test. If Rananim is the largest event in the record, say so explicitly: the test is then an extrapolation, not an interpolation.

### Step 2 — Inflow forecast model (the "what we would know" model)

- Build an event-scale rainfall–runoff model that runs hourly from observed rainfall. A conceptual model such as Xinanjiang (standard in humid southeast China) or a simpler unit-hydrograph/loss model is enough, with state updating on observed inflow. For a small catchment with 22 events, a conceptual model is more defensible than a data-hungry machine-learning model. An ML or error-correction layer can be tested as a comparison, not assumed.
- Calibrate and validate with leave-one-event-out cross-validation on the 21 non-test events.
- Produce forecasts at lead times of 1, 2, 3, ... up to about 12 hours in two modes:
  - **(a) observed rainfall only.** Future rain is set to zero or to persistence. This is the realistic "no QPF" forecast.
  - **(b) perfect future rainfall.** This is the upper bound of what a good QPF could add.
- The hindcast errors from (a) and (b), by lead time and flow level, are the **empirical forecast-error model**. They describe error size, the tendency to underpredict peaks, and timing errors.

### Step 3 — Reservoir and downstream simulation model

- Hourly mass balance with the level–storage curve, the gate/spillway rating, and the operating constraints:
  - release no higher than physical capacity at the current level;
  - maximum change in release per hour (gate operation and downstream bank-safety limit);
  - no release above inflow before the flood-control level is reached, if the rules require this;
  - minimum release.
- Downstream: if there is meaningful uncontrolled local inflow or travel time to the control point, add simple routing (Muskingum or lag) and local inflow from the gauges. Otherwise the downstream metric is reservoir release against safe discharge.
- Verify the model against any observed level/outflow records. Otherwise verify that the existing rule reproduces documented behaviour.

### Step 4 — Candidate release policies

Every policy is compared under the same hydrology and constraints.

| Policy | Role | Information used |
|---|---|---|
| P0 Existing regulation procedure | Baseline; what the agency does now | Current level and inflow only |
| P1 Perfect-foresight optimization (deterministic, whole event) | Upper bound, not operable | True future inflow |
| P2 Tuned rule (parameterized level/inflow-triggered release stages, thresholds optimized by simulation over the event set) | Simple, no forecast needed | Current level, inflow, recent rainfall |
| P3 Rolling-horizon (MPC) with deterministic forecast | Forecast-informed operation | Forecast from Step 2, re-optimized each hour |
| P4 Rolling-horizon with error-aware forecast (conservative quantile, scenario set, or chance constraint on the level limit) | Forecast-informed operation that hedges against error | Forecast plus the error model |

P2–P4 have hard limits on top: release never exceeds downstream safe discharge unless the reservoir is above a defined emergency level, and above that level the dam-safety rule overrides everything.

**Objectives and metrics (reported separately, not collapsed into one weighted score):**

- **Upstream:** maximum reservoir level; margin to the flood-control high, design and check levels; probability of exceeding each.
- **Downstream:** peak release (or peak flow at the control point); hours and volume above safe discharge; number and size of release ramps.
- **Conservation:** level or storage at the end of the event compared with the flood-limit level. Releasing water ahead of a forecast flood that does not arrive costs irrigation and water supply.
- **Robustness:** regret against P1, and the spread across error realizations.

Combined objectives are used only to tune P2–P4, using a lexicographic order: dam safety first, then downstream, then conservation. This avoids arbitrary weights, and the agency can see and question the order.

### Step 5 — Forecast-error experiment (the core of the second research question)

Run P3 and P4 (and P2 as the forecast-free reference) with forecasts degraded in controlled ways, using Monte Carlo with many realizations per event.

- **Error magnitude:** from zero (perfect) through the empirical hindcast error level to about 1.5–2 times that level.
- **Bias:** systematic under- and over-forecast of volume and peak, tested separately. Underforecast threatens the reservoir: it releases too little, too late. Overforecast threatens downstream and storage: it causes unnecessary pre-release and a possible failure to refill.
- **Timing error:** peak arriving early or late by a few hours.
- **Lead time:** usable horizon from 1 hour to about 12 hours.
- **Error structure:** errors that grow with lead time and are autocorrelated (sampled from the Step 2 hindcast residuals, for example by block bootstrap). Do not use independent white noise, which understates the danger.

Outputs:

- Response surfaces of each metric against error size and lead time.
- The **break-even error level** above which forecast-informed operation (P3/P4) is no better than the forecast-free tuned rule (P2).
- The **value of lead time**: how much each extra hour of reliable forecast lowers the peak level or the downstream exceedance.

These directly answer "how much does forecast error affect flood safety upstream and downstream."

### Step 6 — Stress tests beyond the observed record

The 22 events are a small sample and may not include a flood near the design level. Stress-test the shortlisted policies with:

- design-flood hydrographs;
- observed events scaled upward;
- the Rananim rainfall with altered timing;
- a two-peak typhoon sequence, where the reservoir has not drawn down before the second burst;
- starting levels above the flood-limit level, as when a flood arrives soon after another.

A policy is unacceptable if it causes an exceedance of the design or check level that the existing rule would avoid.

### Step 7 — Independent test on Rananim (2004)

Apply the frozen policies to Rananim under three conditions: perfect foresight, the realistic no-QPF forecast, and the Monte Carlo error ensemble. Report the distribution of peak level and peak release, not just one run. If historical operation records exist, compare against them. Then check whether the conclusions from the 21 calibration events hold on the held-out event.

### Step 8 — Selection of the operating plan

Choose the plan in this order:

1. **Feasibility gate.** It never exceeds the dam-safety levels in any tested realization, including stress tests. It exceeds the flood-control high level with probability no greater than the existing rule's.
2. **Downstream.** Among feasible policies, choose the lowest downstream exceedance (hours and volume above safe discharge, then peak).
3. **Conservation.** Among near-ties, prefer the policy that returns to the flood-limit level without wasting storage.
4. **Operability.** Prefer the simpler policy when performance differs by less than the uncertainty band.

## 4. Provisional recommendation

The provisional plan is a **rule-bounded, rolling-horizon release scheme with an error-aware forecast** (P4 bounded by a tuned P2 fallback). It works like this:

- **Every hour,** update the inflow forecast from observed six-gauge rainfall and the latest inflow. Use a conservative quantile (for example an upper percentile from the empirical error model) for the reservoir-safety check, and the median for downstream release planning.
- **Pre-release** (releasing above inflow, or drawing below the current level) only when the conservative forecast volume over the usable horizon would carry the reservoir above the flood-control high level. Cap any pre-release at downstream safe discharge, and limit its volume to what the recession can be expected to refill. This protects irrigation and water supply against false alarms.
- **During the rise,** hold releases at or below downstream safe discharge and ramp them smoothly within the gate-change limit, so that available storage absorbs the peak.
- **If the forecast is missing or clearly failing** (for example, observed inflow runs well above the forecast), switch automatically to the tuned level/inflow rule (P2). Above a defined emergency level, dam safety takes absolute priority and releases go to whatever is needed, regardless of the forecast.
- **On the recession,** release at a controlled rate to return to the flood-limit level within a set time, ready for a following typhoon.

**Why this plan.** On a small, flashy catchment the reliable lead time is short and set mostly by the rainfall-to-inflow lag. A scheme that uses observed rainfall and hedges with a conservative quantile gets most of the attainable benefit. Bounding it by a forecast-free rule means a bad forecast can never make operation worse than a sound rule would. It is also explainable to the agency and to downstream stakeholders: every release can be traced to a level, a forecast and a limit.

**Key assumptions behind it:**

- The rainfall–inflow lag is a few hours, enough for anticipatory release to matter.
- Downstream safe discharge is a binding constraint in large floods.
- The reservoir has enough controllable outlet capacity at moderate levels to act on a forecast.
- The empirical error model from 21 events roughly represents typhoon errors.

**What would confirm it:**

- In Step 5, P4 beats P2 and P0 on downstream exceedance at the empirical error level, without a higher upstream exceedance probability.
- The advantage holds on Rananim and in the stress tests.

**What would change it:**

- **If the lag is very short or the hindcast errors are large** (the break-even error level falls below the realistic error level), forecasts add little or nothing. The recommendation becomes the **tuned forecast-free rule (P2)**, with forecasting reported as a future upgrade and its value quantified by the lead-time curves.
- **If outlet capacity at low levels is too small** to pre-release meaningfully, the question reduces to choosing staged release thresholds. Again recommend P2.
- **If overforecast (false-alarm) costs to irrigation turn out to be dominant,** raise the pre-release trigger quantile, or forbid drawdown below the flood-limit level.
- **If a usable QPF becomes available,** rerun Step 5 with its real error statistics before extending the horizon. Do not extend it on assumption.
- **If Rananim exceeds anything in the calibration set,** treat the test result as provisional extrapolation, and lean more heavily on the design-flood stress tests when deciding.

## 5. Limitations to state in the report

- 22 events, all typhoon/storm events from one small catchment, give a thin basis for an error model. Tail behaviour, meaning the largest floods, is the least certain and the most important.
- Back-calculated inflows contain noise that feeds into both calibration and the error model.
- Simulated forecast errors are a model of reality. The real operational error after deployment must be monitored, and the plan revisited after each major flood.
- The study gives a plan for the flood-control season. It does not resolve the trade-off between flood-limit level and conservation storage across the season, which needs a separate analysis.

## 6. What the agency receives

- An hourly operating procedure: decision table plus rolling-horizon rule, fallback rule and emergency override, with numerical thresholds.
- Tables and curves showing upstream and downstream safety metrics against forecast error and lead time (the evidence for the second research question).
- The Rananim test results with uncertainty bands, compared with the existing rule (and with actual operation, if records exist).
- A short statement of assumptions and the conditions under which the plan should be re-examined.

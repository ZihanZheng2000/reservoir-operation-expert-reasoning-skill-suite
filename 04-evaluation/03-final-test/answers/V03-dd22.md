# Lishimen Reservoir: hourly flood releases when inflow is not known in advance

## 1. Recommendation in brief

**Provisional operating plan for the agency.** Adopt a staged hourly rule that runs on measurements. It uses the current level, the measured inflow, and the inflow already "committed" by rain that the six gauges have recorded but that has not yet reached the reservoir. Committed inflow is estimated at a conservative upper quantile, not at its central value. Forecasts of future rainfall are not used to set releases during the event. Before the rain arrives, the only forecast-based action allowed is a pre-release, and it is capped. The cap is the volume that would refill under a low-rainfall outcome, and the pre-release must not push downstream flow above what the natural flood would have produced. Each stage (pre-rain, rising limb, peak, recession) has its own release target. Releases switch from protecting downstream to protecting the dam at a level trigger. Releases are recomputed every hour, and a fixed fallback applies whenever gauge data fail.

**Reasons.**
- No reliable inflow forecast exists, and any forecast will carry large errors. For a small, steep typhoon catchment, the only near-certain lead time is the delay between rain falling on the catchment and that rain reaching the reservoir as inflow. That delay is a few hours (to be measured; see Section 3).
- A rule built on that delay works without a forecast system. Releases depending on it can be audited hour by hour, and it degrades in a known way.
- Forecast-dependent optimization goes into the study as a benchmark and as a test of when forecasts would become worth adopting. It is not the plan.

**Provisional answer on forecast error.** The study expects the effect of forecast error to be asymmetric:
- **Underforecasts are the dangerous direction for safety.** Releases start late, storage fills on the rising limb, and the reservoir is later forced to make large releases near the peak. Those releases can coincide with the local downstream peak.
- **Overforecasts mainly cost water and cause man-made downstream rises.** Unnecessary pre-release loses irrigation storage and raises downstream flows nobody needed.
- Downstream safety is expected to be more sensitive to timing errors than reservoir level is. The flood-control storage buffers the dam against volume errors, but it does not protect against releasing at the wrong hour.

These are hypotheses to be tested, not findings. The study measures them as curves of upstream and downstream exceedance risk against error size, bias and lead time (Section 5).

## 2. The questions that decide the plan

1. **How much lead time does already-fallen rain give, compared with the time a useful release needs?** If the rain-to-inflow delay is short compared with the time needed to release a meaningful volume within the downstream safe discharge, the plan has to rely more on storage and less on anticipation. Section 6 has the arithmetic.
2. **Beyond what error does a forecast stop improving safety over the measurement rule, and how far is any plausible forecast from that limit?** This error limit decides whether forecast-informed releases belong in the plan now, later, or never.
3. **Which safety limit governs, and how are upstream and downstream combined?** If downstream safe discharge binds in most large typhoon floods, the plan is mostly about timing and smoothing the release. If the dam-safety level binds, it is mostly about keeping storage free.

The study design below answers these three first and in the most depth.

## 3. Data, system and baseline

**Assemble and align (hourly):**
- Rainfall from the six gauges for 1987–2020, converted to catchment-average rainfall. Use Thiessen weights or similar, and check the result against the observed inflow volumes.
- Observed inflows for the 22 flood events.
- For each event: reservoir level, release and gate records, and the operating decisions actually made.
- Reservoir curves: level–storage, and discharge capacity by outlet and gate at the actual head.
- Characteristic levels: flood-limited level, design and check flood levels, crest.
- Downstream control points and their safe discharges or warning stages.
- Constraints for irrigation, water supply and hydropower.

None of these values is given here. They must come from the agency.

**Check the inflow record.** Observed hourly reservoir inflows are often back-calculated from level change plus outflow. If so, they carry level-measurement noise that is amplified at hourly steps. The study should check for oscillation, smooth the series consistently, and treat this noise as part of the "observed" error. Otherwise the error model will blame the forecast for measurement noise.

**Measure the response time.** For each of the 22 events, measure the lag from the centroid of catchment rainfall to the inflow peak, and the rise time from the start of the rise to the peak. Fit a distribution to the lag. This lag sets the useful lead time the plan can count on, and it is the first number the study needs.

**Encode the current rule as the baseline.** Write the current operating rule (the existing operating regulations and the annual flood-season plan) as an executable hourly rule. Every candidate is compared with it on identical inflows and identical starting levels.

**Treat the Rananim operation with care.** The historical Rananim operation is context only, not a counterfactual. First establish:
- the starting level;
- whether releases were constrained by gate availability, power supply, or downstream instructions;
- what information the operators had at each hour.

A good or bad historical outcome may have been forced by these factors rather than by the rule.

**Hold Rananim out.** The Rananim flood is the test case, so it must be excluded from calibration of the rainfall–runoff model, the error model and the rule parameters. Otherwise the test is in-sample.

## 4. Simulator and representation

- **Hourly water balance.** Storage advances each hour by inflow minus total release. Level comes from the storage curve. Release is limited by gate capacity at the current head, by ramping limits, and by a minimum release for supply. Check the simulator against recorded level and release trajectories for the non-test events before using it.
- **Fast rainfall–runoff model.** A simple conceptual or unit-hydrograph model driven by the six gauges, calibrated on 21 events (Rananim held out) and checked by leave-one-event-out. It is used in two ways:
  - to estimate committed inflow from rain already fallen;
  - to turn the 34-year rainfall record into many more synthetic inflow events than the 22 observed.
  
  The second use matters because 22 events contain only a handful of large typhoons.
- **Downstream reach.** Route the release to each control point. Estimate travel time as distance over wave speed, using local data. As an illustration only: a control point 20 km downstream with a wave speed of 2–4 m/s gives about 1.4–2.8 h. Add local inflow between the dam and the control point, estimated from the same rainfall model. In a small typhoon basin that local inflow can peak before the dam release arrives. Route three timings and check that the decision holds in each:
  - local peak earlier than the release;
  - local peak at the same time as the release;
  - local peak later than the release.
  
  Travel time works both ways. It gives an hour or two to cut releases before the local peak arrives, but releases already made will still arrive later.
- **Initial level.** Include it as an uncertain input. In August, irrigation demand may keep the level at or below the flood-limited level, or inflow may have pushed it above. Test a range starting from the flood-limited level.

## 5. How forecast error is represented and tested

No forecast product exists, so the study builds forecasts with controlled errors and measures their effect.

**Two information types, kept separate:**
- **(a) Committed-inflow estimate.** The rainfall–runoff model is run on observed rain with zero future rain. Its error comes from model and rain-measurement uncertainty, and the 22-event hindcast gives it directly. Fit the error distribution separately by lead hour (1, 2, … up to the response time) and by flow regime. Errors in high flows are what matter, and they are usually larger and skewed.
- **(b) Future-rain inflow forecast.** This information does not exist yet, so its errors are synthetic. Perturb the true inflow (observed, or synthetic from the rainfall model) with relative errors whose standard deviation grows with lead time. Test levels of 0 (perfect), 10, 20, 30, 40 and 60%. Also test bias levels (−30%, 0, +30%) and timing errors (peak early or late by 1–3 h).

**Keep the errors dependent.** Forecast errors in consecutive hours are strongly correlated, and treating them as independent badly understates the error in volume.
- Example with an hourly relative error of σ = 0.2, pairwise correlation ρ = 0.8, and n = 6 hours of roughly equal flow.
  - Variance of the 6-hour sum is nσ²(1 + (n − 1)ρ) = 6σ² × 5 = 30σ², not 6σ².
  - The relative error of the 6-hour volume is σ√30/6 ≈ 0.91σ ≈ 18%, not σ√6/6 ≈ 0.41σ ≈ 8%.
  - Independence would understate the volume error about 2.2 times.
- Generate errors with an autocorrelated process or a copula fitted across lead hours. Check that the generated scenarios reproduce:
  - the hindcast error statistics by lead time;
  - the lag-1 correlation;
  - the coverage of the large events.

**Information conditions compared on identical events and starting levels:**

| Policy | Information used | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Current rule | Current state | Baseline, acceptable-risk benchmark |
| Measurement rule | Level, inflow | Lower bound of a no-anticipation plan |
| Committed-inflow rule (recommended) | Plus rain already fallen, at a conservative quantile | Candidate plan |
| Forecast-informed rolling optimization | Synthetic forecasts at each error level, re-optimized hourly, only the first hour executed | Tests forecast value |
| Perfect-foresight optimization | True future inflow | Upper bound, not deployable |

The forecast-informed rolling optimization is a closed-loop test. The policy's own releases feed back into the state, so it shows what an operator using an imperfect forecast would actually do.

**Main output: the forecast-value curve.** Plot each policy's upstream and downstream risk against forecast error σ. The point where the forecast-informed policy stops beating the committed-inflow rule is the maximum useful error σ*. If the curve shows the best strategy switching at some error size (for example, pre-release worth doing only below a given error), report that threshold explicitly.

## 6. Feasibility arithmetic (order of magnitude)

All values below are illustrative and must be replaced by Lishimen's own.

**Flood volume.** Volume = runoff coefficient × rainfall × area. For every 100 km² of catchment, 300 mm of typhoon rain at a runoff coefficient of 0.8 gives 0.8 × 0.3 m × 100 × 10⁶ m² = 24 × 10⁶ m³. Compare this with the storage between the flood-limited level and the design flood level.

**Pre-release volume.** Pre-release volume = (release − inflow) × time.
- A net release of 300 m³/s held for 6 h releases 300 × 21,600 ≈ 6.5 × 10⁶ m³.
- Held for 12 h it releases about 13 × 10⁶ m³.
- During the rain, the release is capped at roughly the downstream safe discharge minus local inflow, and inflow is already high, so net release is small.

Most useful pre-release must therefore happen **before** the rain arrives, and that depends on typhoon warnings rather than inflow forecasts. Whether such warnings reach the agency (in China they are normally issued by the meteorological service) is an assumption to confirm with the agency.

**Effect of an inflow error on level.** Level error = volume error ÷ reservoir surface area. For example, a 20% underestimate of a 24 × 10⁶ m³ flood leaves 4.8 × 10⁶ m³ unplanned. On an assumed flood-level surface area of 5 km², that is about 1 m of extra rise. Comparing this with the margin between the design flood level and the dam-safety trigger tells the study whether reservoir-side risk from forecast error is material.

**Time for release to matter.** If the rain-to-peak lag is about 3–8 h (an assumption to be measured), committed-inflow information can move only (Q_out,max − Q_in) × lag of volume before the peak. The study computes this per event and compares it with the volume of each flood above the safe downstream discharge. If it is a small fraction, anticipation adds little and storage does the work.

**Equipment and operating limits.** Every candidate must hold under the following, and Rananim is re-run with each:
- one gate out of service;
- loss of mains power during the typhoon;
- hourly gate-change and ramping limits;
- discharge capacity at the actual head;
- level-measurement error of a few centimetres.

**Successive floods.** Typhoon rain often comes in two bands. Route a second flood arriving 12–48 h after the first, from the level the plan leaves after recession.

## 7. Safety definitions and acceptable risk

**Upstream (reservoir side).**
- P(maximum level > design flood level), and P(maximum level > check flood level) for scaled extreme floods.
- Extent and duration of inundation above any upstream backwater limit in the reservoir area, if such a limit exists.

**Downstream.**
- P(peak flow at the control point > safe discharge).
- Volume and duration above the safe discharge.
- Man-made exceedances: hours when the release made downstream flow higher than it would have been with no dam.

**Other objectives.**
- End-of-event level relative to the flood-limited level (water kept for irrigation and supply).
- Spill.
- Lost generation.

**Combining upstream and downstream.** Dam safety is not tradeable. Downstream protection applies up to the downstream protection standard. Above that, the rule switches to dam-protection mode. This follows normal Chinese practice, but the actual standards must come from the approved design documents. Report both risks separately and also as a union ("either fails"). Do not collapse them into one weighted score.

**Acceptable-risk benchmark.** Use the current rule's risk on the same synthetic event set. A candidate is admissible only if the **upper** end of its Monte Carlo confidence interval does not exceed the current rule's risk, for both upstream and downstream. Benefits are compared only among admissible candidates.

**Risk over a horizon.** A per-season probability p becomes 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ over n years. For example, p = 0.05 per year gives 1 − 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 0.40 over ten years. Report risk in this form so the agency reads it consistently.

## 8. Can the data resolve the differences?

**Historical events alone cannot.** A policy that fails in 0 of 22 events still has a 95% upper bound of about 3/22 ≈ 14% per event. Hence the synthetic events built from the 34-year rainfall record and from scaled events.

**Monte Carlo precision.**
- At p = 0.01, N = 1,000 runs gives a standard error of √(0.01 × 0.99/1000) ≈ 0.003, a relative error of about 31%.
- N = 10,000 runs gives about 10%.
- Hourly water-balance runs are cheap, so use 10⁴–10⁵ scenarios per condition.
- Compare policies on the same scenarios (common random numbers), so that differences are paired and their standard error is much smaller than that of each risk alone.

**Testing protocol.**
- Leave-one-event-out over the 22 events.
- Rananim as a held-out test, shown as full hourly trajectories, not only as summary statistics.
- Sensitivity to:
  - error size, bias and timing;
  - the chosen conservative quantile (for example 50, 70, 80, 90, 95%);
  - starting level;
  - scaling of events to design and check magnitudes.
- For each result, explain the mechanism: did the plan reduce risk by releasing earlier, by smoothing, or by keeping storage? A risk number alone is not enough.

## 9. The operating plan delivered to the agency

The study calibrates the rule's thresholds on the synthetic set, excluding Rananim. The rolling optimization is used only to show how close the simple rule comes to it. The structure of the rule is fixed in advance:

1. **Typhoon watch, before rain.**
   - If the level is above the flood-limited level, draw down to it. The flood-control zone must be empty before the flood, not held as water.
   - Make any further pre-release only within the refillable cap.
   - Keep the release at or below the downstream safe discharge, and below the flow the natural flood would have produced.
2. **Rising limb.** Each hour, set the release from:
   - current level;
   - measured inflow;
   - committed inflow at the calibrated upper quantile.
   
   Target the largest release that keeps the routed flow at the control point within the safe discharge, net of the estimated local inflow, and that respects the ramping limits. Cut the release ahead of a forecast local peak when travel time allows.
3. **Peak and dam-safety switch.** When the level crosses a trigger, switch to dam-protection mode and release up to full capacity. The trigger is set so that the remaining storage can no longer absorb the upper-quantile committed inflow plus a design-flood allowance. Warn downstream before this switch.
4. **Recession.** Return to the flood-limited level as fast as the downstream safe discharge and ramping allow. Hold this until rain has stopped and committed inflow is falling. Only after that, refill for irrigation according to the season.
5. **Correction and fallback.**
   - If measured inflow exceeds the committed-inflow estimate for two consecutive hours, raise the quantile or step the release up gradually. Do not jump to a large release in one step unless the dam-safety trigger is reached.
   - If gauges fail or data stop arriving, revert to the measurement-only rule.
   - Log every hourly decision, its inputs, and any deviation from the rule.
6. **Forecast branch, deferred.** A forecast-informed branch enters the plan only when a real forecast has been verified on hindcasts against the error limit σ* from the study. Those hindcasts must come from the same system and configuration that would be used in operation.

## 10. Adoption

**Approval.**
- The change goes into Lishimen's reservoir operating regulations and the annual flood-season operation plan.
- These are approved by the water resources authority and the flood control and drought relief headquarters with jurisdiction. In China that is at county or municipal level, with provincial oversight for a reservoir of this importance. The agency should confirm which level applies.
- The current approved rule remains the legal basis until approval.

**Trial.**
- Agree acceptance criteria with the dam-safety authority in advance: no increase in either risk at the upper confidence bound.
- Run the new rule in shadow mode beside the current rule for at least one flood season, recording what it would have done each hour.

**Warning and records.**
- Downstream governments, emergency services and the public must be warned of any man-made release changes and of the dam-safety switch.
- Operators need training on the stage triggers.
- The rule needs version control.
- Hold an after-event review of every flood above a set size.

## 11. Key assumptions and what would change the plan

- **Lead time.** If the measured rain-to-peak lag is long (more than about 8–10 h), committed-inflow anticipation becomes more valuable, and a quantile-based anticipatory release could be extended. If the lag is very short (about 2 h or less), the plan reduces to measurement-based storage management.
- **Forecast value.** If the forecast-value curve shows a gain beyond Monte Carlo noise at error levels a realistic forecast could reach, a forecast-informed pre-release branch becomes worth developing. Its inflow forecasts must first be built and verified on hindcasts.
- **Which limit binds.** If downstream safe discharge rarely binds, because the flood storage absorbs the large events, the plan's main lever becomes the starting level and recession speed, not hourly timing.
- **Rananim as a stress case.** If Rananim's held-out test shows the committed-inflow rule exceeding the current rule's risk on either side, the quantile or the dam-safety trigger must be tightened before any proposal.
- **Inflow record quality.** If the observed inflows prove too noisy for hourly error fitting, error statistics should be fitted on 2–3-hour volumes, and the hourly rule should be smoothed accordingly.

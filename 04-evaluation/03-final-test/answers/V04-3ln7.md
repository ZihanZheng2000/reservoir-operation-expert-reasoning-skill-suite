# V04 – Joint flood operation of Guxian and Luhun under forecast uncertainty

## 0. Framing and what the brief does and does not support

Each reservoir starts at its flood-limit level (FLL) and must end there, within a tolerance. That makes the event close to a **fixed-volume problem**. By the end of the event each reservoir must have released roughly all of its event inflow (Guxian about 253 Mm³, Luhun about 154 Mm³). So operation can only change **when** that water leaves, not **how much** leaves. The extra freedom is the drawdown margin: down to 3 m below FLL for pre-release, and up to the safety levels for temporary storage. Forecast error matters because it changes how much water actually arrives, compared with the plan that was built on the forecast.

The brief leaves some points open, so I assume the following and will check each:

- **A1. Safety thresholds.** "Dam safety" means keeping the maximum level below a defined threshold for each reservoir, such as the design or check flood level, or an operating upper limit. The brief does not give these values. I need them from the agency.
- **A2. Downstream safe flow.** The Heishiguan objective is to minimise the peak. There may also be a safe-discharge threshold that should be a hard constraint. I need to ask.
- **A3. Local inflow.** Local inflow between the dams and Heishiguan can be estimated for 2021 as observed Heishiguan flow minus the routed observed releases. It is uncontrollable, and in real time it is not forecast.
- **A4. Rainfall input to the forecast.** The deterministic forecasts used **2021 precipitation as the forecast basis**. If this is observed rainfall, the forecasts assume perfect rainfall. They then understate the error an operator would face in real time. I treat the archive errors as the better guide to operational error, but I must confirm what the archive forecasts were driven by.
- **A5. Luhun has no error archive.** The archive covers only Lushi (Guxian's inflow). For Luhun/Dongwan there is no archive, so transferring the Lushi error model is an explicit assumption. It must be stress-tested and not presented as evidence.
- **A6. Interpolation smooths peaks.** The observed series were linearly interpolated to hourly. This can clip short peaks and shift timing slightly. On a 6-h decision step that is probably minor. Still, peak values used for verification should be checked against the original records.

What the brief **cannot** support: general claims beyond this one design event, and any precise estimate of error at high flows from the archive alone. Flows above 1000 m³/s are rare in the archive, but the design event peaks at about 2400–2700 m³/s. So the large-flood error behaviour is **extrapolated**, and the answer must say so.

## 1. Plan of work

### Step 1 – Build and verify the deterministic system model

1. **Reservoir mass balance on a 6-h step**, with an hourly internal sub-step. Inputs are the level–storage curves and the release capacity as a function of level, Qmax(Z).
2. **Routing from each dam to Heishiguan.** Use Muskingum or lag-and-route for the Luohe and Yihe reaches. Calibrate on observed releases and Heishiguan flows (2021, plus other events if the yearbook has them). Then add local inflow.
3. **Verification.** Replay the observed 2021 releases and check that simulated levels match observed levels, if available, and that simulated Heishiguan flow matches observed flow. Also check the volume closure. Without this step, none of the later level numbers can be trusted.
4. **Forecast check.** Compare the deterministic forecast for 2021 with the observed inflow, for both reservoirs. Check peak error, peak timing and volume error, **not just NSE**. An average NSE above 0.95 is dominated by the many moderate flows. It says little about peak magnitude or flood volume, which are what drive maximum level.

### Step 2 – Characterise forecast error (Q1 and Q2 groundwork)

Use the Lushi archive to diagnose the error structure along four lines.

- **Magnitude dependence (heteroscedasticity).** Bin errors by forecast or observed flow. The expectation is that absolute error grows with flow. Relative error may stay constant or shrink, and there may be **systematic underestimation of peaks**, which is typical of data-driven models trained mostly on low flows.
- **Bias** by flow class and by rising versus falling limb.
- **Temporal dependence.** Check the autocorrelation of errors across consecutive steps or lead times. Persistent errors accumulate into a **volume** error, and storage responds to volume.
- **Timing error** of peaks, if the archive has enough events.

Then build a **conditional error model**:

- Transform the data (Box-Cox or NQT) so that variance is roughly constant.
- Make the conditional mean and spread functions of flow (and of lead time, if available).
- Add an AR(1) or similar term in time.
- Alternatively, use quantile regression on flow, or a copula of forecast and observation.

**The sparse high-flow tail** is the hard part. The plan has three parts:

- (a) fit the error model on all data in a transformed space, so that the tail borrows strength from the body of the data;
- (b) measure how sensitive the result is to the choice of extrapolation (for example, constant relative error versus error that keeps growing with flow);
- (c) add explicit **stress-test scenarios**, such as peak underestimated by 10/20/30% or peak arriving 6/12 h early.

I will report the high-flow error as a **range across defensible assumptions**, not as one fitted value.

**Joint error between the two rivers.** Both basins are hit by the same storm system, so their forecast errors are probably positively correlated. With no Luhun archive, I treat the cross-correlation as a scenario parameter with values ρ = 0, 0.5 and 0.8. If both reservoirs are under-forecast at once, the Heishiguan peak rises and both dams lose margin together.

### Step 3 – Uncertainty propagation (Q1)

1. **Generate inflow ensembles** for the 2021 event: the deterministic forecast plus sampled errors from the Step 2 model. Use about 1000 or more members per error model and check that the results converge. Keep the sampled flows physically sensible: non-negative, and with plausible volume.
2. Run each member through the reservoir and routing model under **two operating modes**:
   - **Open-loop:** the release schedule optimised on the single deterministic forecast is applied unchanged to the true inflow. Releases are still capped by Qmax(Z) and the ramping rule. Where needed, emergency rules apply, such as releasing at capacity above a safety level. This is the "current practice" exposure.
   - **Closed-loop / rolling-horizon:** every 6 h the forecast is updated and the release plan re-optimised. This is closer to real operation and shows how much of the open-loop risk adaptive operation removes.
3. **Outputs for each reservoir:**
   - the distribution of maximum water level: mean, the 90/95/99th percentiles, and the probability of exceeding the safety threshold;
   - the distribution of the end-of-event level against FLL, with the probability of missing the tolerance;
   - the minimum level against the FLL − 3 m limit.

   For Heishiguan: the distribution of peak flow and the probability of exceeding the safe flow.
4. **The answer to Q1** is the shift between the deterministic-forecast result and these distributions. The expected (mean) change in maximum level may be modest. The upper quantiles and exceedance probabilities matter most for safety, and I expect these to move much more.

### Step 4 – Test the "usual simple assumptions" (Q2)

Repeat Step 3 with the error models usually assumed:

- (i) i.i.d. Gaussian error with constant standard deviation, unbiased;
- (ii) i.i.d. constant relative error, unbiased;
- (iii) possibly fixed ±x% peak scaling.

Compare these with the conditional model (heteroscedastic, autocorrelated, biased at high flow, cross-correlated). Compare upper quantiles of maximum level, exceedance probabilities and end-level miss rates, using the **same random seeds and same ensemble size**.

I also isolate each feature in turn: add heteroscedasticity alone, then autocorrelation, then bias, then cross-correlation. This shows which simplification drives any understatement. It is more useful to the agency than a single "yes".

### Step 5 – Joint release optimisation (Q3)

**Decision variables:** releases from Guxian and Luhun at each 6-h step over the event horizon.

**Objectives:**

1. Minimise the Heishiguan peak (the routed sum of both releases plus local inflow).
2. Minimise Guxian's maximum level, or its use of flood storage.
3. Minimise Luhun's maximum level.

The two reservoir objectives can be kept separate or combined, for example as the maximum normalised storage use across the two. Keeping them separate shows the trade-off between the two dams.

**Constraints:**

- Water balance in each reservoir.
- 0 ≤ release ≤ Qmax(Z_t).
- |R_t − R_{t−1}| ≤ ΔR for each reservoir.
- Z_t ≥ FLL − 3 m.
- |Z_end − FLL| ≤ tolerance.
- Z_max ≤ safety level (A1).
- Any minimum release.
- Heishiguan ≤ safe flow, if one is set (A2).

**Solver:** A multi-objective evolutionary algorithm (NSGA-II or III) fits this problem, because the level-dependent capacity and the routing make it nonlinear. The problem is small (about 2 × 20–40 variables), so the search is tractable. Constraints are handled by penalties or repair, especially the end-level condition. As a cross-check, solve a single-objective version (minimise the Heishiguan peak subject to level caps) by LP or DP. This confirms the evolutionary front is near-optimal.

**Making the optimisation robust to forecast error.** This is the main link between Q1/Q2 and Q3. I would compare three formulations:

- **(a) Deterministic:** optimise on the single forecast. This is current practice.
- **(b) Robust / stochastic:** optimise objectives over the ensemble, using for example the 95th percentile or CVaR of maximum level, and chance constraints such as P(Z_max > safety level) ≤ α and P(end-level miss) ≤ β.
- **(c) Rolling-horizon:** re-optimise every 6 h using (a) or (b).

Then evaluate all three on the **same independent ensemble**. Without that, the deterministic plan would look better than it really is.

**Coordination logic I expect the optimiser to find, and will test explicitly:**

- **Use the ~20 h offset.** Guxian peaks about 20 h before Luhun. After allowing for each reach's travel time to Heishiguan, Guxian's high releases should be timed to pass Heishiguan **before** Luhun's routed water arrives. Guxian then cuts back toward its minimum while Luhun's water passes (compensating operation). If the travel-time difference narrows or reverses the 20 h gap, the logic changes. That is why routing calibration matters.
- **Pre-release within the 3 m drawdown margin**, mainly at Luhun, whose peak comes later, during Guxian's early phase. This must stay within the ramp limits and keep the downstream flow lower than the later peak would be.
- **Sharing storage.** Guxian has the larger flood volume, and both reservoirs have more than 1 billion m³ of flood storage. Neither event volume is close to that storage. So the binding limits are probably **release capacity at lower levels, the ramp limits and the end-level requirement**, not total storage. I will check this directly in the results.
- **The recession phase must empty the stored water** to reach FLL by the end. This puts a sustained release on the falling limb. It must not create a **second Heishiguan peak**, especially from local inflow plus both drawdowns at once. The two reservoirs' drawdowns should therefore be staggered as well.
- **Response to forecast error.** Under-forecast peaks consume flood storage that was planned to stay empty. A robust plan keeps a safety margin of storage at the reservoir with the larger expected error or the less certain forecast. On the current evidence that is Luhun, because it has no error archive.

### Step 6 – Choosing one option defensibly

1. **Hard screen first** (non-negotiable, set by the agency, not by weights). Discard any option that breaches the following under the risk-based test, i.e. under the ensemble, not just the deterministic forecast:
   - a dam-safety chance constraint, P(Z_max > safety level) ≤ α;
   - the drawdown limit;
   - the ramp and capacity limits;
   - the end-level tolerance at the agreed reliability.

   Dam safety is a constraint, not something to trade off against downstream benefit.
2. **Trade-offs among the options left.** Show the Pareto front of Heishiguan peak against each dam's upper-quantile maximum level. Identify the **knee point**: the place where pushing the Heishiguan peak lower starts to cost a lot of reservoir level.
3. **Multi-criteria ranking with sensitivity.** Rank the options with a transparent method (for example TOPSIS or a weighted sum). Use **stakeholder-elicited weights**, and report how the ranking changes across a range of weights. If entropy or CRITIC weights are used, present them only as a cross-check. They measure how much the criteria vary, not how important they are.
4. **Robustness check.** Prefer the option with the lowest worst-case regret, or the lowest CVaR, across the error-model variants from Step 4 and the cross-correlation scenarios. A choice that holds whatever error model is assumed is more defensible than one that is optimal for a single assumed model.
5. **Record the decision**: the thresholds, α and β, the weights, the chosen option and why the runner-up was rejected. This lets the agency defend the choice later.

## 2. Provisional judgments

**Q1 – How much does forecast error change expected water levels?**

**Provisional answer:** The mean maximum level probably shifts only modestly relative to the deterministic plan. The **upper tail** (95th–99th percentile of maximum level, and the chance of breaching the safety level or missing FLL at the end) will widen considerably, and more for the reservoir with the larger peak error.

- **Why:** for a large flood, the error in peak and volume is large in absolute terms. Persistent errors accumulate into storage, and the open-loop plan does not respond to them.
- **What would confirm or change it:** Step 3 results.
  - If the error at high flows turns out to be small in absolute terms, or volume error is small, the effect is small.
  - The effect will be larger if the deep-learning model systematically underestimates peaks, or if the archive forecasts used forecast rather than observed rainfall (A4).

I cannot give a number before running the analysis. The brief gives no level–storage values and no high-flow error statistics.

**Q2 – Do simple assumptions understate the risk?**

**Provisional answer: Yes, very likely.** An i.i.d., unbiased, constant-variance error model:

- (i) under-represents absolute error at high flow (heteroscedasticity);
- (ii) lets errors cancel over time, when in reality they persist and add up to volume error;
- (iii) misses the tendency of peaks to be under-forecast;
- (iv) ignores the joint under-forecasting of both rivers, which hits the Heishiguan peak and both dams together.

These features push in the same direction, toward higher tail levels.

**Key caveat:** the archive is thin above 1000 m³/s. So the size of the understatement is itself uncertain. It should be reported as a range across tail extrapolations.

**What would change it:** the archive could show that errors at high flow are small, unbiased and uncorrelated in time. Then the simple models would be adequate, and Step 4 would show that directly.

**Q3 – How should releases be coordinated?**

**Provisional recommendation:** Use **staggered, compensating operation**, re-planned every 6 h, with a margin set by risk:

- Guxian passes its earlier flood mostly before Luhun's routed peak reaches Heishiguan.
- Luhun pre-releases within its 3 m margin while Guxian is still rising, then stores its peak.
- Guxian cuts back while Luhun's water passes.
- On the recession, both reservoirs draw back to FLL with staggered releases that do not create a second peak.

The plan should be optimised against the forecast ensemble, not the single forecast. It should keep a storage buffer sized from the conditional error model.

**Assumptions:**

- The ~20 h peak lag survives routing.
- Local inflow is modest compared with the releases.
- The safety thresholds leave room for this storage.

**What would change it:**

- If routing travel times cancel the 20 h lag, the logic shifts toward simple peak-shaving at both reservoirs together.
- If release capacity at FLL − 3 m is too small to pre-release meaningfully, pre-release adds little.
- If local inflow dominates the Heishiguan peak, reservoir coordination has limited leverage, and the agency should be told that plainly.

**How to pick one option:** First apply hard safety screening under uncertainty. Then show the Pareto front with its knee point. Rank the options with stakeholder weights and a sensitivity analysis, and favour robustness across error models.

## 3. Limits to state in the deliverable

- **Results apply to a single design event (2021).** Before generalising, repeat with other yearbook events, or with the 2021 event scaled up and with its timing shifted.
- **The large-flood error behaviour is extrapolated** from an archive that is mostly below 500 m³/s. It applies to Guxian only, and is assumed for Luhun.
- **Deterministic forecasts driven by observed rainfall** (if A4 holds) understate real-time uncertainty.
- **Level results depend on the routing calibration** and on how local inflow is treated.

# Judgment V03-D-j1

## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### KQ (key questions)
- **Answer 1.** It finds and works through the questions that decide the study, using numbers first. Q1 asks whether a 100-year flood can physically be held to 1000 m³/s and 180.69 m. Q2 asks what ramping and low-head outlet capacity cost a reactive rule. Q3 asks what lead time and error structure a six-gauge forecast can give. It also settles the meaning of the two capacity figures with a hypothesis that can be checked, defines storage use in two directions (idle versus premature filling), defines risk over an error ensemble, and states how one plan is chosen. Some questions are settled only provisionally because values are missing, such as the ramping limit and the outlet curve, but it says so.
- **Answer 2.** It identifies the same core questions: the feasibility check under perfect foresight, building the forecast and its error model, a storage-use metric, risk defined over scenarios, a Pareto set, and a selection rule. It settles them less concretely. The storage-use metrics (utilisation ratio and use at the inflow peak) are sensible but loosely tied to the "too risky / too conservative" goal. It gives no first-order arithmetic for feasibility or for what the forecast is worth.

### TC (technical correctness)
- **Answer 1.** I checked the arithmetic and it holds: 6.8 to 7.7 Mm³ per metre; 4.3 Mm³ is about 0.63 m, which matches the 0.65 m between 180.69 m and 181.34 m; 1 mm of runoff is 0.296 Mm³; 1000 m³/s is about 12.2 mm/h; 30.8 mm/h is about 2530 m³/s; the ramp-lag storage is about 8.8 Mm³; the Monte Carlo sample is n ≈ 1900. There is one slip. The correlated-error variance 6σ²(1+5ρ) assumes every pair of hours is equally correlated. The answer calls ρ a lag correlation and fits an AR(1) model elsewhere, and AR(1) gives about 24.5σ², not 30σ². This overstates the margin a little but does not change the design.
- **Answer 2.** It has no numerical errors, partly because it computes very little. In one place it moves the release ceiling to outlet capacity "once the projected level approaches 181.34 m". That allows the level to pass the 20-year upper flood-control level of 180.69 m without saying so. The rest of the answer treats 180.69 m as the upstream risk threshold, so the release logic is inconsistent at that point. The other concepts are correct: back-computed inflow noise, Xinanjiang for a humid Zhejiang basin, the MPC terminal cost, and chance constraints and CVaR.

### ME (method)
- **Answer 1.** The method is ordered and executable: data checks, the baseline, a leave-one-out split with the test event held out, a reference forecast model, a heteroscedastic AR(1) error model with coverage and PIT checks, a synthetic error grid, policy families (a) to (d), an hourly stage logic with a point-of-no-return definition, degradation curves, break-even accuracy, a safety screen followed by the noninferior set and a lexicographic choice, and robustness checks. Verification is built in at each step.
- **Answer 2.** The sequence is also clear and executable: a perfect-foresight bound, an information-state model, P0 to P3 (the current rule, an adaptive rule optimized with NSGA-II or Borg, deterministic MPC, chance-constrained MPC), closed-loop Monte Carlo over error levels, scaled events, and a selection procedure. It is less specified in places. The error-grid values, the size of the scenario ensemble inside each MPC solve, and how the objectives enter the multi-objective search are not pinned down, and it gives fewer verification checks on the error model.

### PR (practicality)
- **Answer 1.** It is specific. It costs pre-release below 176.04 m in terms of irrigation storage, caps it with a refill condition, and asks whether the downstream control point is at the dam. It gives a fallback rule, reversion triggers, a shadow-mode trial, downstream warning lead, the approval chain under the Chinese flood-season operation plan, and operator training. It also handles gate unavailability and level-gauge error.
- **Answer 2.** It is practical and realistic. It recommends an adaptive rule as the default because forecasts are not operational, keeps the data confidential, provides a one-page operating chart, and leaves the agency to decide which goal gives way. It says less about fallback triggers, the trial period and warnings.

### CA (calibration)
- **Answer 1.** Assumptions are labelled, for example the 200 m³/s/h ramping limit and the 2 to 6 h lead time, and gathered in a table that says what would change the recommendation. It warns that event-conditional risk is not annual probability, and it declines to claim a ranking the data cannot resolve. Some expected-shape statements are presented as expectations only.
- **Answer 2.** It is well scoped. Its expectations are labelled provisional, and it notes that the 2703 m³/s peak may or may not belong to the test event. It treats the error model as a stand-in. There is no overclaiming.

### RC (reference coverage)
- **Answer 1.** It covers the following: a forecast built from the six gauges, scenario uncertainty, risks defined over scenarios (the probability of exceeding the level and release limits, plus severity and duration), storage use over the whole event in both the "too risky" and "too conservative" directions (an equivalent to RRCA), comparison against perfect foresight (MOOB) and a deterministic forecast, and selection of one plan. It does not cover a multi-forecaster ensemble, stacking, or an explicit multi-objective evolutionary optimizer producing a Pareto set from scenario fitness. Its selection is lexicographic rather than weighted, which is justified, but it carries no minimax-regret aggregation.
- **Answer 2.** It covers a similar set and sits closer to the reference structure: a Pareto set from multi-objective evolutionary search, risk objectives as probabilities and durations above the level and release limits, minimax regret against the perfect-foresight bound as the robustness criterion (close to STP), an entropy or TOPSIS cross-check on selection, deterministic versus stochastic MPC (close to MOSEM versus MOROU), and the perfect-foresight bound (close to MOOB). It also misses the stacking ensemble, and its storage metric is a weaker substitute for RRCA's whole-event ratio of inflow to remaining capacity.

## 2. Notes

**Answer 1: good.** Its quantitative scoping is excellent. It shows that one hour at 30.8 mm is about 25 to 29% of flood storage. It shows that feasibility is set by runoff above about 12 mm/h at the peak, not by total event volume. Its reading of 31.4 versus 35.7 Mm³ as the space to 180.69 m versus 181.34 m is a sharp hypothesis that can be checked. It explains why ramping makes a few hours of forecast valuable. It defines storage use against the perfect-foresight trajectory, separating idle storage from premature filling. Its stage logic includes a point of no return and early, minimal exceedance to spread the excess. It accounts for Monte Carlo sample size and asks whether 22 events can resolve the differences between candidates. Its adoption section (shadow mode, reversion triggers, approval chain) is realistic.

**Answer 1: missing or wrong.** The correlated-variance formula is equicorrelated rather than AR(1), a minor overstatement. It does not build or combine multiple forecast models; it makes one reference model plus an optional data-driven model. It does not explicitly optimize a Pareto set with an evolutionary solver and has no scenario-to-fitness aggregation such as minimax regret. Candidate family (b) is tuned by grid or evolutionary search, but the objectives are not formalized as a multi-objective problem.

**Answer 2: good.** It frames the problem as a sequential decision under an explicit information state. It runs the perfect-foresight feasibility check and V_excess before judging any policy. It offers a clean policy ladder (current rule, optimized adaptive rule, deterministic MPC, chance-constrained MPC), uses closed-loop Monte Carlo with an error model that is heteroscedastic, autocorrelated, biased and timing-shifted, and defines value of information and break-even error. Its selection uses hard screens, agency tolerances, then minimax regret or percentile robustness, with TOPSIS or entropy as a cross-check that is sensitivity-tested. Choosing the adaptive rule as the default is well argued.

**Answer 2: missing or wrong.** The switch to outlet capacity is tied to 181.34 m rather than 180.69 m, which is inconsistent with its own upstream risk metric. It has no first-order numbers on storage, runoff depth or ramping cost, so the deciding questions stay qualitative. Its storage metrics (peak use ÷ capacity, use at the inflow peak) do not capture whole-event balance as directly as a time-integrated inflow-to-remaining-storage ratio. It gives no sample-size reasoning for the Monte Carlo runs and gives fewer operational safeguards (fallback triggers, trial period, warnings).

**Reference essentials neither answer covered:**
- An ensemble of several ML forecasters (BP, LSTM, ELM) combined by stacking with a linear meta-model on out-of-fold predictions. Neither answer combines forecast models.
- Generating forecast scenarios from model-structure or hyperparameter perturbation with an NSE screen. Both answers use a synthetic error model instead, which is arguably a better-calibrated equivalent.
- A single whole-event storage index like RRCA with a target of 1, with its exceedance probability used as an objective.
- An ablation that drops the storage-use objective to show its effect.
- Both answers use event-wise cross-validation, though leave-one-out rather than the reference's 4-fold split by events.

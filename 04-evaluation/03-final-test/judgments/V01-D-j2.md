## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1

- **KQ (7).** Identifies the deciding questions: lead-time-dependent error description, a per-day upper bound for the level from drawdown capacity, a daily trust test for lead times, definitions of upstream and downstream risk, and comparison against the 3-day baseline across a risk sweep. It explicitly notes that misses matter for safety and false alarms only cost power. It does not state the graded-rule tolerance idea, that a forecast error is harmless when forecast and observation fall in the same rule grade. It touches it only indirectly, through threshold hit/miss scoring. The trust test (Monte Carlo risk at Z_up ≤ α) settles lead-time selection, but somewhat loosely.
- **TC (7).** The mass balance (35,000 − local inflow − upper-quantile inflow, minus the 2 h delay) is sound. The order-of-magnitude check is correct: about 8 × 10⁸ m³/day of drawdown, about 25 × 10⁸ m³ over 3 days, a few metres at about 7 × 10⁸ m³/m, consistent with 145–148 m. One small logic slip: it claims Z_up(t, L, α) "is not necessarily increasing in L". But its own formula sums only positive daily terms, so the bound is non-decreasing in L unless the flood day truncates the window. Using the same α as both the quantile and the acceptance threshold is slightly circular but workable.
- **ME (7).** The steps are ordered and executable: error dataset → conditioning → joint error vectors → Z_up → Monte Carlo risk → trust test → no-look-ahead replay → calibration (PIT/coverage) → stress tests. Gaps: the per-day, per-lead, per-α Monte Carlo is heavy and its mechanics are underspecified. There is no explicit fallback for when no lead time passes, and no explicit rule for the falling-limb refill rate.
- **PR (6).** It flags that local inflow is missing from the data, the need for a power model with tailwater and unit limits, the 2 h delay, design and check flood routing, and the power cost of false alarms. It omits drawdown-rate and bank-stability limits, the regulatory approval needed to exceed 148 m, shadow operation, and fallback when forecasts are missing. Practicality points are sound but mostly generic.
- **CA (8).** Assumptions A1–A6 are explicit, and it warns that the 2019 season is a weak risk test (in-sample, few onsets). The provisional judgment is labelled as such and lists what would change it. It invents no facts.
- **RC (7).** It covers lead-time error characterization (by a justified alternative: empirical joint vectors instead of fitted normals), the pre-release capacity upper limit with the 2 h delay and local inflow, the three-stage structure (pre-release, flood rules, refill), daily lead-time selection by a probability threshold (analogous to Pθ), RP/RL-type risk definitions, Monte Carlo scenarios, the 2019 rolling simulation, the 3-day baseline, and a risk sweep. It misses the explicit error-domain / rule-grade concept and the reference's degraded-forecast comparison options (relaxed accuracy, enlarged errors).

## 2. Notes

**Answer 1, good.** Correctly sees that the decision depends on cumulative inflow over the drawdown window, so error vectors should be resampled jointly rather than independently. Separates misses (safety) from false alarms (power cost) via POD/FAR at the 35,000 m³/s threshold. Defines upstream risk as the level above 145 m at first exceedance of 35,000 m³/s, which matches the reference's RP. Ties "acceptable" to the baseline's own simulated risk. Insists on a no-look-ahead replay and a PIT/coverage calibration check. Includes design and check flood routing from raised levels. Gives an order-of-magnitude check showing the current 145–148 m band is roughly a deterministic 3-day version of the same logic.

**Answer 1, missing or wrong.** The claim that Z_up is not necessarily increasing in L is inconsistent with its own positive-part sum. There is no rule for when no lead time is trusted (the reference falls back to real-time inflow only). There are no drawdown-rate limits, no regulatory or approval path, and no fallback for missing forecasts. The Monte Carlo trust test is computationally heavy and loosely specified. It does not test degraded-forecast scenarios (relaxed accuracy, enlarged errors) as distinct options.

**Answer 2, good.** Gives a worked, numerically correct drawdown-capacity table showing diminishing returns with lead. Self-checks against current practice (3 days ≈ 3.1 m ≈ 148 m). Quantifies how local inflow below the dam erodes the ceiling. Gives a correct correlated-error variance inflation. Specifies an explicit operational rule with point of no return and fallbacks. Includes a sample-size argument (3/k bound) and Monte Carlo precision. Uses confidence-bound screening against the baseline risk R0 and a perfect-forecast upper bound. Stress tests include error inflation, which parallels the reference's option 4. Its adoption path and stakeholder list are realistic. It correctly warns against presenting the gain as spill recovery.

**Answer 2, missing or wrong.** The trust criterion per lead is less formal than a probability-of-correct-decision threshold. It has no explicit statement that graded release rules make some forecast error harmless. The 149–150 m expectation depends heavily on assumed σ and is somewhat conservative relative to a mean-inflow capacity bound. It would not reach the reference's 153.72 m at 7 days, though it is labelled illustrative. Fitting error distributions with a goodness-of-fit test is not specified, although the stratified empirical and copula approach is a defensible alternative.

**Reference essentials neither answer covered.**
- The error-domain concept: graded, threshold-based release rules tolerate forecast error as long as forecast and observed inflow stay in the same rule grade. This gives the bounds [θL, θU] relative to the pre-release threshold.
- The probability that the observed inflow falls inside that error domain, used as the per-lead "correct decision" probability. Leads are dropped from the longest down until the probability meets Pθ (e.g., 0.9995), and the result is swept over Pθ.
- An explicit "real-time inflow only" mode when no lead time passes. Answer 2's fallback to the 3-day rule is a partial equivalent.
- A comparison option with relaxed accuracy requirements, alongside enlarged errors. Answer 2 covers only the enlarged-error case.

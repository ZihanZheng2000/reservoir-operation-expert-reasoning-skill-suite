# Judgment V03-S-j1

Neither answer follows the reference's route: an ML forecast ensemble, then hyperparameter-perturbed scenarios, then robust multi-objective optimization with the RRCA index, minimax-regret aggregation and an AHP/entropy compromise. Both instead propose a simulation-based comparison of policies with different information. They use a conceptual rainfall-runoff forecast plus an empirical error model, Monte Carlo error experiments, and a lexicographic or admissibility-based choice of plan. This is a sound, defensible alternative for a small catchment with 22 events and no operational forecast, so the departure itself is not penalized. Coverage is judged on whether each answer settles the reference's deciding questions in an equivalent way.

## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1 (V03-p307)

- **KQ (7).** It finds the main deciding questions:
  - the rainfall-to-inflow lag as the usable lead time;
  - how to represent forecast error (an empirical hindcast error model with an autocorrelated block bootstrap);
  - upstream and downstream metrics, kept separate;
  - the break-even error level beyond which forecasts stop helping;
  - how to pick one plan (lexicographic order).

  Some questions are settled loosely. Downstream local inflow and travel time are only conditional ("if there is meaningful..."). There is no explicit treatment of how storage use over the whole event is judged, beyond end-of-event level. How performance across scenarios is collapsed is left at "probability of exceeding" plus "regret vs P1", with no rule for which governs.
- **TC (8).** No conceptual errors. The asymmetry between under- and over-forecasting is correct. Its warning that white-noise errors understate danger is correct. Xinanjiang is appropriate for humid SE China. MPC with error-aware quantiles is correctly framed. It contains no quantitative checks, so there is little to get wrong, and also little to verify.
- **ME (8).** The steps are ordered and executable: data audit, forecast model with LOEO, reservoir/downstream simulator, P0-P4 policies, a structured error experiment (magnitude, bias, timing, lead time, structure), stress tests, the held-out Rananim test, and a selection procedure. Verification is included: the simulator is checked against records and Rananim is held out. Gaps: the tuning objective for P2-P4 is only "lexicographic", and there is no Monte Carlo sample-size or statistical-precision plan.
- **PR (7).** It is specific about back-calculated inflow noise, gate ramping limits, the existing regulation as baseline, design-flood stress tests, the cost of false-alarm pre-release to irrigation, an automatic fallback rule, and an emergency override. It is less specific on downstream local inflow and on equipment-failure cases. It does not cover the regulatory approval or trial-deployment path.
- **CA (8).** Assumptions are listed explicitly and nothing is invented about Lishimen. The recommendation is provisional, with clear "what would confirm / what would change it" conditions. It says it would flag Rananim as extrapolation if it is the largest event.
- **RC (6).** Justified equivalents:
  - a forecast model with an error model and scenarios (vs ML+SEM with hyperparameter scenarios);
  - upstream and downstream exceedance probabilities and durations, which correspond closely to the reference's F1-F4;
  - a regret metric;
  - a constraint set (mass balance, rating, ramping);
  - a deterministic upper bound (P1, like MOOB) and a deterministic-forecast policy (P3, like MOSEM) vs a robust policy (P4, like MOROU);
  - a selection rule.

  Missing or weaker: no multi-model forecast combination, no whole-event storage-use balance index (too risky vs too conservative), no explicit scenario-to-point aggregation rule, and no Pareto-set treatment.

### Answer 2 (V03-dd22)

- **KQ (8).** It names three deciding questions explicitly and answers them in depth: lead time from already-fallen rain vs the time a release needs; the error threshold σ* at which forecasts stop helping; and which limit binds and how upstream and downstream are combined. It adds questions that matter in practice:
  - local inflow between the dam and the control point, and its timing;
  - initial level as an uncertain input;
  - successive floods;
  - whether 22 events can statistically resolve differences between policies.

  Like Answer 1, it gives no whole-event storage-use index. Storage balance is handled only through pre-release caps and end-of-event level.
- **TC (9).** The quantitative checks are correct:
  - variance of a correlated sum nσ²(1+(n−1)ρ) = 30σ², giving 0.91σ vs 0.41σ;
  - pre-release 300 m³/s × 21,600 s ≈ 6.5×10⁶ m³;
  - flood volume 2.4×10⁷ m³ per 100 km²;
  - about 1 m of level error from 4.8×10⁶ m³ over 5 km²;
  - travel time 1.4–2.8 h;
  - the rule-of-three bound 3/22 ≈ 14%;
  - Monte Carlo standard error at p = 0.01;
  - risk over a horizon 1−(1−p)ⁿ.

  All numbers are labelled illustrative. Common random numbers for paired policy comparison is correct practice. There are no material errors.
- **ME (8).** It is executable and ordered. It defines two separate information types: a committed-inflow hindcast error, and synthetic future-rain error with a grid of σ, bias and timing. It specifies the error generator, verifies it against hindcast statistics and lag-1 correlation, and compares five policies on identical events. It uses closed-loop rolling optimization, sets explicit Monte Carlo sizes, and adds synthetic events from the 34-year rainfall record to address the 22-event sample. Gap: the order is somewhat less linear than Answer 1's (the feasibility arithmetic is interleaved), and threshold calibration of the rule is described only briefly.
- **PR (9).** This is the strongest part. It treats historical Rananim operation as context, not as a counterfactual (gate availability, information available to operators). It stress-tests equipment failure (one gate out, loss of power) and level-measurement error. It defines man-made downstream exceedance, warning before the dam-safety switch, a fallback when gauges fail, and logging. It describes the Chinese approval path (operating regulations, flood-season plan, flood-control HQ) with appropriate hedging, a shadow-mode trial for a full season, and pre-agreed acceptance criteria. It notes that typhoon warnings come from the meteorological service.
- **CA (8).** Illustrative numbers are flagged throughout. The asymmetric effect of forecast error is stated as hypotheses, not findings. Agency-supplied data are listed as required inputs. Slight over-commitment: it rules out future-rain forecasts in event operation before the study has run. The ruling is justified by the brief and is reversible through σ*, but it partly presumes the answer.
- **RC (6).** Its equivalents are similar to Answer 1's. Upstream and downstream exceedance probability, volume and duration map to the reference's F1-F4. Perfect foresight and forecast-informed policies serve as the reference's MOOB/MOSEM-style controls. Admissibility at the upper confidence bound vs the current rule plays the role of collapsing scenarios and choosing a plan. Missing, as in Answer 1: a multi-model or ensemble forecast, a whole-event storage-use index, explicit regret or worst-case aggregation, and a Pareto front with compromise selection.

## 2. Notes

**Answer 1, good.**
- It frames the problem as "a policy that degrades gracefully with forecast error."
- It builds an empirical hindcast error model, separated by lead time and flow level.
- It compares observed-rain-only vs perfect-future-rain forecast modes.
- Its error experiment covers magnitude, bias, timing, lead time and autocorrelation, and yields a break-even error level and value-of-lead-time curves.
- It sets up a perfect-foresight / deterministic-forecast / error-aware MPC ladder that mirrors the reference's MOOB / MOSEM / MOROU comparison.
- Its stress tests include two-peak typhoons and high starting levels.
- Its fallback logic is clear.

**Answer 1, missing or weak.**
- Downstream local inflow and routing are optional.
- There is no Monte Carlo precision plan and no statistical check of whether 22 events can separate policies.
- It does not say how results across scenarios are aggregated into one fitness value when tuning P4.
- Storage use across the whole event has no balance index; only the end level is used.
- There is no equipment-failure stress test and no regulatory adoption or trial path.

**Answer 2, good.**
- "Committed inflow" from rain already fallen, at a calibrated upper quantile, is a well-reasoned, forecast-free anticipatory signal for a flashy catchment.
- Its demonstration that correlated errors inflate volume error by about 2.2 times is correct.
- It checks feasibility arithmetic on pre-release volume against the safe discharge.
- It handles the timing of local inflow vs the dam release at the control point.
- It extends the 22 events synthetically from the 34-year rainfall record.
- Its Monte Carlo sizing uses common random numbers.
- Admissibility at the upper confidence bound vs the current rule is a robust acceptance test.
- It covers equipment-failure tests, the regulatory approval and shadow-mode path, downstream warning, and man-made exceedance.

**Answer 2, missing or weak.**
- It has no whole-event storage-use index and no explicit worst-case or regret aggregation.
- It has no multi-model forecast comparison or combination; the rainfall-runoff model is "simple conceptual or UH" only.
- It somewhat presumes that future-rain forecasts are excluded from the plan.
- There is no Pareto set; the choice is made by admissibility and then benefit comparison, which is acceptable but less transparent about trade-offs among downstream, conservation and smoothness.

**Reference essentials neither answer covered.**
- Several forecast models (e.g. BP/LSTM/ELM) combined into one deterministic forecast through a stacking ensemble, with cross-validation by event folds and scoring by NSE/RMSE/MAE/R2.
- A whole-event index balancing remaining flood storage against inflow (RRCA, target 1) to avoid operation that is "too risky" or "too conservative", plus an ablation showing its effect.
- An explicit scenario-to-point aggregation: per-scenario regret, minimax-regret fitness, and worst-case vs optimal-case solution sets.
- Multi-objective evolutionary search (NSGA-III) producing a Pareto set, with a compromise solution chosen by combined subjective/objective weights (AHP plus entropy).
- Generating forecast scenarios from model-structure or hyperparameter variability (both answers use residual-based or synthetic error models instead, which is arguably better calibrated).

Overall, both are high-quality, justified alternatives. Answer 2 has more correct quantitative reasoning and is more specific on operations, downstream timing, statistical resolution and adoption. Answer 1 is slightly cleaner in its experimental ladder and has an explicit regret metric. Reference coverage is about equal.

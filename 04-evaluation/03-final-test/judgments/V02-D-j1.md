# Judgment V02-D-j1

Both answers land on the same core route as the reference: a receding-horizon MPC that executes only the first hour, with explicit penalties for operator preferences (spill, peak outflow, ramping, revisions of the announced schedule, gate switching, turbines first, no outflow above the inflow peak so far). Both choose a mixed-integer formulation. This is a justified equivalent to the reference's linear MPC plus nonlinear evaluator, because binaries handle the complementarity-type objectives (gate state, turbines before spill) exactly. Neither builds a Pareto set at each step. The two answers differ mainly in emphasis and in how concrete they are.

## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1 (ltub)

- **KQ.** It identifies the deciding questions clearly. (i) What the small storage can physically do: the band can only shift and smooth releases, so free volume when each peak arrives is the deciding quantity. (ii) How to plan when 6-h forecasts are short relative to 200–300 h multi-peak events, which centres on drawdown between peaks. (iii) What operators find acceptable, given that preferences are unwritten and must be elicited and ranked. It settles the priority of dam safety over no-amplification explicitly through slack logic. It does not ask whether weights and parameters such as the level ceiling should change with the hydrological state, which is the reference's third key question. Its state-dependent terminal band only partly substitutes for this.
- **TC.** The quantitative checks are correct. They cover flood volume ≈ 10,700 × 6.5 × 3,600 ≈ 250 × 10⁶ m³, level-rise rate at the 2020 peak, a derived-inflow noise of about ±200 m³/s from 1-cm level resolution, the correlated-error variance 6σ²(1+5ρ) = 30σ², drawdown time between peaks, and the ×2.4 scaling. Assumptions such as the ~70 km² area are labelled. There are two slips. It proposes a centred 3-h moving average to define the "peak inflow so far", which is non-causal in real time. Its 48-h default horizon is long given that only 6 h are forecast, although it is justified by scenario continuations and tested at 24/48/72 h.
- **ME.** The method is complete and ordered: data reconstruction, piecewise-linear physics, a full MILP formulation with constraints and tiered objective, a scenario generator with non-anticipativity, phase logic, and fallbacks. The closed-loop hindcast protocol uses five comparators (historical, rule, deterministic MPC, scenario MPC, perfect foresight), and the differences between comparators are given meanings. It also specifies metrics including a schedule-revision metric by lead time, leave-one-event-out tuning, sensitivity tests, stress tests and a blind operator acceptability test. This is executable, with verification.
- **PR.** It is very specific. It covers what to request from K-water and KMA (archived forecasts, gate logs, announced schedules, downstream requests, minimum-release values, gate ramp limits), and it uses historical starting levels for a fair comparison. It defines solve-time limits with a fallback, a schedule "freeze" for downstream agencies, reversion triggers, hand-over to emergency procedures near 200-year scale, shadow-mode trial, approval routes and logging.
- **CA.** It is well scoped. It states that three events cannot show general superiority and that stress tests do not certify dam safety, and it hedges its statement about the approving authority. Planning values (margin of 0.3–0.5 m, tier order) are labelled provisional. Placing terminal storage as tier 1 above stability and peak outflow is asserted somewhat firmly, but it is flagged for operator confirmation.
- **RC.** It covers the receding-horizon structure, almost all eight practical objectives (a terminal band rather than an in-horizon target band; FWL − m acts like SH), the start-from-actual and start-at-NHWL conditions, three events, synthetic multiplicative forecast error at several levels including perfect, perfect versus uncertain inflow, horizon sensitivity and weight sensitivity. It misses state-dependent or adaptive weights/SH and the comparison of adaptive versus fixed-weight MPC baselines. Its horizon set (24/48/72) differs from the reference's 6–24 h.

### Answer 2 (kp0t)

- **KQ.** It frames the same core issues: the rolling schedule is MPC, the small storage leaves little time to correct errors, short forecasts mean the terminal condition carries the decision, and unwritten preferences make acceptance the goal. It is strong in treating the terminal condition as the link to the long event. It proposes a state-dependent target (lowered while rain is forecast, raised in recession), which is the closest of the two answers to the reference's "should parameters change with the hydrological state" question. It weighs lexicographic ordering against goal programming against a weighted sum, which is the multi-objective real-time question. Its feasibility reasoning is less quantitative than Answer 1's.
- **TC.** It has no significant errors. It correctly notes that the real-time "peak so far" must use a causal filter, not a centred smoother, and that the hindcast must use the same causal estimate. It flags sedimentation affecting the level–storage curve of a 40-year-old dam, and it sets the margin from forecast-error analysis rather than ad hoc. Its claim that turbine-first "follows from" a spill penalty is slightly loose, but it then enforces the rule with a binary. It gives few numeric checks, so there is little to get wrong and little feasibility evidence.
- **ME.** It is executable. The formulation is clear, with forecast cases (perfect, realistic archived, synthetic with replicates, persistence) and an E1–E8 experiment table that separates the cost of the short horizon (E2 versus E3) from the cost of forecast error (E3 versus E4). It tests terminal-rule variants and horizons of 6/12/24 h, and it lists metrics including solve time. Some choices are left open (lexicographic or goal programming or weighted; terminal rule (b) not yet specified). It proposes chance constraints without saying how they are formulated with about 10–30 scenarios. It lists no tuning/validation split.
- **PR.** It is good and specific in places: downstream safe-discharge thresholds, gate-rate and structural limits given the dam's age, archived issued schedules, operator interviews on concrete topics, inverse optimisation from history with the caveat that it copies habits, and trade-off curves shown to operators. It has less on deployment (no shadow mode, reversion triggers, approval or communication with downstream agencies) and less on comparing from historical starting levels.
- **CA.** It is very well calibrated. Assumptions A1–A7 are explicit and it refuses to invent the supply target. Stress tests are labelled as not design-flood analyses, results are presented as demonstration rather than proof, and it lists clear conditions that would confirm or change the recommendation.
- **RC.** It covers the receding horizon, nearly all practical objectives, horizons of 6/12/24 h (matching the reference), perfect versus uncertain inflow, synthetic error growing with lead time, three events, starting at NHWL as a sensitivity case, and sensitivity to weights/tolerances. Its state-dependent terminal target partly covers the dynamic-parameter idea. It also misses the comparison of adaptive versus fixed-weight baselines and the time-varying weights themselves.

## 2. Notes

### Answer 1: good

- Quantitative reasoning on storage shows the reservoir can only re-time releases over hours to a day. It identifies inter-peak drawdown as the deciding phase for 2016/2018 and proposes a terminal band whose upper edge is sized so a repeat of the largest peak could be absorbed.
- It encodes dam safety > no-amplification through a slack that may be used only when the level constraint would otherwise bind.
- It explains why forecast errors must be correlated across lead times, and the arithmetic is correct.
- The comparator ladder separates the value of uncertainty handling from the value of better forecasts. It uses leave-one-event-out tuning, a blind operator test, shadow-mode trial and fallbacks.

### Answer 1: missing or wrong

- The centred moving average for the "peak inflow so far" is non-causal in real time.
- The 48-h default horizon rests mostly on synthetic continuations.
- It has no state-dependent weights or adaptive SH, and no fixed-weight baseline comparison.
- It does not check the level–storage curve for sedimentation.

### Answer 2: good

- It treats the terminal condition as the main link to the long event and gives three tiers of options (fixed, state-dependent, SDP value function).
- It specifies causal filtering of derived inflow, the sedimentation check, and a margin derived from forecast error.
- Its experiments separate horizon cost from forecast cost, test horizons of 6/12/24 h, and include persistence as a lower bound.
- It gives a reasoned choice between lexicographic, goal-programming and weighted formulations, and plans preference capture through interviews, inverse optimisation and trade-off curves.

### Answer 2: missing or wrong

- It has less quantitative feasibility reasoning. It gives no storage-volume or between-peak drawdown arithmetic.
- Several key choices are left open (objective scheme, rule (b), how chance constraints are formulated).
- It has no tuning/test split across events.
- Deployment detail is thinner.
- It has no adaptive-weight or fixed-weight baseline comparison.

### Reference elements neither answer covered

- Weights and the highest tolerated level (SH) that change at each time step according to the hydrological state, searched online (the reference uses a GA warm-started from the previous step). Both answers instead fix weights or tiers offline and only vary a terminal target.
- Explicit comparison of the adaptive scheme against fixed-weight MPC baselines tuned for opposite goals (stability versus peak reduction), showing that fixed weights fail even on their own target objective.
- Normalisation of heterogeneous objectives (MAVE-based multipliers), and evaluator-weight sensitivity showing the trade-off between schedule changes and peak outflow (both answers mention weight sensitivity only generically).
- An in-horizon target level band [SL, SU] separate from the ceiling (both use only FWL − margin plus a terminal target).

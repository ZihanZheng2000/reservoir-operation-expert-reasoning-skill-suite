## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1 (V01-dorv)

- **KQ (7).** Identifies the deciding questions: the forecast error at each lead time, an effective lead time, pre-release capacity as the factor that sets the upper level, and an explicit "safe enough" test fixed before simulation (equal or lower risk than R0/R1). It defines risk mostly as exceedance of design flood levels and downstream limits, and lists "probability the reservoir is not back near 145 m when the flood rises", which matches the reference's upstream risk. Lead-time selection appears only as a searched parameter L. It is not treated as a real-time choice. It does not see that graded release thresholds tolerate forecast error.
- **TC (8).** The pre-release formula is sound: cumulative spare release below Q_safe, with an error quantile on the forecast, a minimum over k, and caps for outlet capacity and ramp rate. The feasibility arithmetic holds: about 0.5-0.7 x 10^9 m3/m, and 3 x 10^9 m3 released over 3 days needs about 11,600 m3/s. It correctly separates head gain from spill recovery and notes that spill above turbine capacity cannot be recovered. I found no errors that change the design. Some figures are only rough, and the answer says so.
- **ME (7).** The steps run in a clear order: forecast characterization, error model, rule family R0-R5, three tests (hindcast, design-flood Monte Carlo, stress), then the plant model. It includes a perfect-forecast bound and a static raised-level comparator, which is a good way to isolate the value of the forecasts. Gaps: there is no closed-loop description of how the state is updated with observed inflow. Recession and refill logic is thin. There is no Monte Carlo sample-size reasoning and no confidence-interval acceptance rule. "Not statistically worse" is left loose.
- **PR (7).** It lists the data needed (curves, compensation rules for Zhicheng/Shashi/Chenglingji, drawdown-rate limits, 2019 operation). It treats the operating authorities as the ones who set risk tolerance. It considers sediment, Chongqing backwater, navigation and Gezhouba, and Qingjiang interval inflow. It mentions trial operation only briefly and does not cover governance or fallback.
- **CA (8).** It refuses to assume official values and labels the provisional ceiling (146-150 m) as a hypothesis. It is candid about the limits of one season. The claims are well scoped.
- **RC (6).** Covered: error by lead time with Monte Carlo scenarios from a multiplicative error model; an upper limit from pre-release capacity that depends on lead time; a pre-release trigger; comparison of the 3-day current practice against other lead times (L swept 1-7); error inflation (the reference's +20% option); upstream and downstream risk. Missing: the error-domain idea, the probability of a correct decision with a threshold Pθ, real-time coupling of lead times, an explicit three-stage model with pre-refill on the falling limb, and a Pθ-style sensitivity.

### Answer 2 (V01-hzdc)

- **KQ (8).** It frames the three deciding questions sharply: can the reservoir return to 145 m in time, what "safe enough" means and against what (non-compensable, upper confidence bound), and whether one season of data can resolve the difference. It assigns lead times explicitly by role: 1-3 days set the bound, 4-7 days give early warning only. This settles the question of which lead time to trust, but with a fixed split rather than an adaptive one. p_miss is in substance the reference's upstream risk. A man-made rise from pre-release appears as a downstream failure event. It also misses the idea that graded thresholds tolerate error.
- **TC (8).** The arithmetic checks out: 3 m x 0.65 x 10^9 ≈ 2 x 10^9 m3, which needs about 7,700 m3/s over 3 days; the variance of a 3-day sum with ρ = 0.7 is 7.2σ², a factor of 1.55; the 50-year risk goes from 39.5% to 41.0%; the Monte Carlo standard error is about 0.001 at N = 10^4. The turbine flow (~30,000 m3/s), 22.5 GW, check level 180.4 m and a gain of 1-3% are all plausible, and the 1-3% agrees with the reference's ~1.5%. One questionable emphasis: it makes the daily drawdown-rate limit (0.6/1.0 m/day, assumed values) the likely binding constraint, while the reference binds on the release cap (35,000 m3/s less lateral inflow). The answer offers this as a test with sensitivity, not as a fact, so it is a minor issue. Capping Q_out,max at the natural flow so that pre-release causes no man-made flood is a correct and useful refinement.
- **ME (9 -> 8).** This is a complete, ordered and executable design. It specifies a simulator and checks it against 2019. It has executable baselines B0-B3, a closed loop updated with observed inflow, and explicit stage logic (normal, prerelease, flood control, recession, fallback, abort). Parameters are tuned on the stress test and not on the 2019 replay. The error model is a copula across leads, verified with rank histograms and coverage. Monte Carlo sample sizes are justified. Acceptance uses upper confidence bounds. It reports the forecast accuracy at which each rule stops passing. The only gap is that lead-time combination is not adaptive, so I score it 8.
- **PR (9).** It is specific on the comparator encoding (how a 3-day forecast "supports" 148 m), the data caveats (back-calculated inflow, intra-day peaks, forecast-system version), Muskingum routing to Zhicheng/Shashi, the Dongting/Qingjiang timing, and the cascade. It also covers governance (MWR, CWRC, the flood-control headquarters, CTGC), shadow operation, reversion rules and who must be warned. This is realistic.
- **CA (8).** Planning values are labeled. Governance is hedged ("as far as I know"). The risk illustration is explicitly not an estimate. It states that the 2019 replay is in-sample and gives no risk figure. The provisional numbers are given up front, but they are presented as provisional and tied to the arithmetic.
- **RC (6).** Covered: error by lead time, with normality testing and Monte Carlo; an upper limit from pre-release capacity; a stage structure that maps closely to the reference's rising, flood and falling modules (prerelease, then the original compensation rules, then recession); upstream risk (p_miss) and downstream risk; error inflation; and a comparison against the 3-day current practice. Missing: the error domain and graded-threshold tolerance, the probability of a correct decision with Pθ, adaptive multi-lead coupling (it uses a fixed 1-3/4-7 split), pre-refill to the upper limit on the falling limb while keeping power output (it gives recession rules but frames them conservatively), and comparison against a longer single lead time such as 6 days.

## 2. Notes

**Answer 1, good.** Pre-release capacity is the core logic, written with an explicit error-quantile margin. The feasibility arithmetic is sound. A static raised-FLWL comparator (R2) and a perfect-forecast bound (R5) separate what the forecasts add. The safe ceiling is defined as the worst case over flood shapes and frequencies, which is conservative. Hydropower is split into head effect and spill recovery. Secondary constraints are covered: Chongqing backwater, sediment, Gezhouba.

**Answer 1, missing or weak.** The closed-loop simulation and fallback rules are not specified. Falling-limb refill logic is thin. The acceptance criterion ("not statistically worse ... within a tolerance") has no confidence-bound rule and no Monte Carlo sample-size reasoning. Lead time is a global parameter, not chosen in real time. It does not see that a forecast in the same rule grade as the observed inflow leaves the decision unchanged.

**Answer 2, good.** The drawdown and lead-time arithmetic is quantitative. It shows that correlated errors across leads widen the margin by about 1.55 times. The safety criterion is non-compensable and applied to upper confidence bounds. p_miss is a central diagnostic. The man-made flood constraint caps pre-release at the natural flow. Stage logic includes fallback and abort rules. The error model is verified with rank histograms. The effective sample size is the number of flood rises, not the number of days. Governance and the shadow-operation pathway are realistic. The hydropower magnitude agrees with the reference.

**Answer 2, missing or weak.** The fixed 1-3/4-7 lead-time split is more conservative than the reference's adaptive selection. Because 4-7-day leads are never used to permit a higher level, it gives up the reference's finding that longer leads are usable when inflow is low. The drawdown-rate limit is offered as the probable binding constraint on assumed values. There is no error-domain or graded-threshold reasoning. Pre-refill on the falling limb to capture power is not developed.

**Reference essentials neither answer covered.**
- The error domain: the relative gap between the forecast maximum inflow and the release-rule thresholds (bounds θL, θU), which uses the fact that graded threshold rules tolerate forecast error.
- The probability of a correct decision per lead time, with a threshold Pθ (0.9995) used to drop lead times from the longest down, and a fall back to real-time inflow only.
- Adaptive real-time coupling of several lead times: longer leads at low inflow, shorter ones near the threshold.
- A sensitivity sweep on Pθ to trace the risk-benefit trade-off. Both answers sweep error quantiles and ceilings, which is a partial equivalent.
- An effective lead time adjusted for decision latency (lead minus 2 h).
- An explicit pre-refill module that raises the level back to the upper limit on the falling limb while keeping power output.

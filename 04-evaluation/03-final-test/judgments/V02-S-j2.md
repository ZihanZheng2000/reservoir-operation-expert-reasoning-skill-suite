## 1. Reasoning per criterion

### Answer 1 (V02-algv)

- **KQ (7).** It identifies the three questions that decide the design: (a) the 6 h forecast against 8-day multi-peak floods, settled with a terminal free-storage term and beyond-horizon scenarios; (b) acceptance as a designed property; (c) robustness with only three events. It also correctly says that operator objectives must be elicited, not taken from manuals. It does not ask whether objective weights should stay fixed or change with hydrological state. It also leaves the multi-objective handling loosely settled ("weighted or lexicographic", plus Pareto fronts offline).
- **TC (8).** No real errors. It correctly notes that hourly derived inflow is noisy, that a cardinality limit on release changes needs integers (with an L1 proxy), and that convex LP/QP is fast enough for a 1 h cycle. It treats level as a hard constraint backed by slack variables, which is sound. The 12 to 72 h horizons are justified by catchment memory and scenarios. It does not mention the nonlinear level-storage relation inside an LP, which is a small gap.
- **ME (8).** The phases are clear and ordered: elicitation, data/testbed with a replay check, forecast tiers, candidate schemes B0/B1/S1–S4, and evaluation. It includes leave-one-event-out tuning, perfect versus no-forecast bounds, Pareto fronts of performance against acceptability, and a distilled rule. It is executable. Fallback logic and pre-registered success criteria are less specific than in Answer 2.
- **PR (7).** It is realistic about operators: blind comparison, hour-by-hour replay, gate increments, notification lead time, and stated rather than live acceptance. It treats the institutional and regulatory setting and the upstream reservoir only generically.
- **CA (8).** Well scoped. Assumptions are listed for the operator to confirm, values are not invented, synthetic forecasts are labelled, and it says plainly that results from three events are case studies.
- **RC (6).** It covers receding-horizon MPC with only the first step applied, operator-derived practical objectives, step-wise changes and changes between consecutive schedules, the level band, smoothness as a soft term, varied horizons, synthetic forecast error, perfect versus uncertain inflow, the three events, and baselines with differently weighted MPC (via the Pareto/weight sweep). It misses state-dependent (dynamic) weights and SH, the two-level linear-solver/nonlinear-evaluator split, the "peak outflow below the inflow peak so far" objective, turbines before the spillway, and gate-state continuity.

### Answer 2 (V02-5yg5)

- **KQ (8).** Its Q1–Q3 match Answer 1's but are settled more sharply. Q1 is answered with continuation scenarios plus an offline terminal-storage guide, and D6 isolates the cost of myopia. Q2 splits acceptance into four measurable parts. Q3 states plainly what three events can and cannot support. The quantitative pre-analysis (pool fraction per hour of lag; inter-peak recovery time f·6.5/k) is the kind of check that decides where gains are possible. Like Answer 1, it fixes the weights and does not ask whether they should vary with the hydrological state.
- **TC (8).** The arithmetic is correct: about 15 % of the pool per hour of lag at 200-year inflow; 1 cm over 10 km² ≈ 10⁵ m³ ≈ 28 m³/s; the variance of a 6 h correlated error sum, 6σ²(1+5ρ), with the √5 understatement; Monte Carlo standard error √(p(1−p)/N). It separates the forecast lead time (6 + basin lag) from the 6.5 h fill time. Updating the state with observed inflow is correct. One small inconsistency: a 1,000-run Monte Carlo resolution argument is used to justify N ≥ 2,000 per event, scenario and scheduler, which may be computationally heavy with scenario MPC, but this is not an error.
- **ME (9).** Complete and ordered: diagnose current practice first, then the water-balance closure check (<1 % of V_FC), the error model by lead time and flow regime with a coverage check, and a ladder of schedulers (H, C, M, D6, S, R, P, Pₕ) that separates information effects from logic effects. It also covers leave-one-out for every tuned element, a short list of tuned parameters, stress tests (gate out of service, false alarm, peaks closer together), and success criteria agreed in advance.
- **PR (9).** Specific and realistic: gate increments, holding times, reversion triggers, an emergency branch, pre-release caps tied to refill and to "no worse than natural" downstream flow, upstream release split from natural inflow, shadow operation, approval roles and audit, and downstream warning lead time.
- **CA (8).** Assumptions are explicit and hedged ("as far as I know"; "to be confirmed"). The institutional facts (K-water, the Geum River Flood Control Office, Yongdam upstream) go beyond the brief but are flagged and broadly correct. Illustrative numbers (ρ = 0.8, f, k) are labelled as such. A full flood-season shadow trial is ambitious, though it is framed as the strongest available evidence rather than a promise.
- **RC (6).** Similar coverage to Answer 1: MPC with only the first step applied, operator objectives, plan churn between consecutive schedules, the change-count penalty, monotone release on the rising limb (close to gate-state continuity), hydropower ranked last, horizon variation (Pₕ 1–48 h), correlated synthetic errors (close to the reference's smoothed noise), perfect versus uncertain inflow, and Yongdam inflow handled explicitly. The pre-release cap ("not above the natural flood") is close to "peak outflow below the inflow peak". It also misses dynamic weights and SH, the GA weight search, and the linear-plus-nonlinear-evaluator split, and it treats the level band as hard constraints rather than soft band penalties.

## 2. Notes

**Answer 1, good:** it recognises operator practice as manual MPC. It builds acceptability into the optimisation (move blocking, a schedule-stability penalty, a no-reversal rule, notification lead time) and puts a price on it with Pareto fronts. Its forecast tiers (forecast, then catchment memory, then scenarios) make good sense, and it proposes a distilled rule chart for operators who distrust optimisers. Inflow QC, leave-one-event-out tuning and perfect versus no-forecast bounds are all included.
**Answer 1, missing or weak:** it gives no explicit treatment of weights that change through the event (the best weights shift between steps and peaks). It has no explicit "release below inflow peak" principle and does not handle turbines before the spillway. It leaves the nonlinear height-volume curve inside a convex formulation unaddressed. Fallback and abort rules are thin, and the upstream reservoir is not treated explicitly.

**Answer 2, good:** its back-of-envelope feasibility analysis (pool used per hour of lag, inter-peak recovery time, a mass-balance bound on gains) points the study at where gains can exist. Correlated forecast errors by lead time and regime are well handled. The scheduler ladder isolates the value of horizon (Pₕ) and of the beyond-horizon tail (D6 against S). Safety stays as constraints, with an explicit emergency branch. Fallback and reversion triggers are concrete, it splits out the Yongdam release, and the institutional adoption pathway is specific.
**Answer 2, missing or weak:** like Answer 1, it keeps tuned weights fixed rather than state-dependent. Treating the level band as hard rather than soft may make the schedule rigid near SH-type thresholds. It does not discuss the nonlinear level-storage relation inside an LP, and it has no turbines-before-spillway rule. The N ≥ 2,000 Monte Carlo per scheduler with scenario MPC may be costly.

**Reference elements neither answer covered:**
- State-dependent, time-varying objective weights, and a dynamic highest tolerated level (SH), searched at each step (GA, warm-started).
- A two-level structure: a linearised MPC (slack variables, time-decaying change weights) plus a nonlinear evaluator holding the complementarity-type objectives and the nonlinear height-volume curve.
- Explicit objectives for gate-state continuity and for using turbines before the spillway.
- MAVE-type normalisation of objectives with different units.
- Comparison against fixed-weight MPC baselines tuned for different priorities, to test whether fixed weights reliably win even on their own objective.
- Sensitivity of the evaluator weight on schedule changes.

Both answers did add a terminal-storage or end-of-horizon term, which the reference lists as its own main limitation.
